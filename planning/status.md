## The argument in plain words

The texts changed the story. This is the version they currently support.

### 1. The Self has two early roots, and both were bounded (§4, §4c)

- **Answerability.** Each person stands answerable on their own: before God, through conscience and consciousness. This runs from Augustine through Petrarch ("No one can become or be unhappy except through his own fault") to Luther, Calvin, Descartes and Locke.
- **Self-shaping and self-presentation.** The humanist self of excellence, glory and craft: Petrarch, Pico, Machiavelli, Castiglione, Cellini. It runs parallel to the Reformation, not out of it.

Every one of these thinkers **bounded** the Self: by God, community, the body, fortune, a given nature, or limits on guilt. Pico's man is the "plastes et fictor" (moulder and maker) of himself, but as a gift of God ("summam dei patris liberalitatem," the supreme generosity of God the Father), aimed at being "unus cum deo spiritus factus," made one spirit with God. The English renderings are ours. Burckhardt calls the unbounded individual "the fundamental vice" of the age.

> **The pattern:** the later Self kept the structure and dropped the limit. This holds in every section tested so far.

### 2. The Enlightenment freed the person from institutions, not the Self from God or community (§4c)

- **Religion:** belief leaves the hands of church and state. Locke calls the church "a voluntary society"; Paine says "My own mind is my own church." But the Self stays "accountable to God only" (Locke).
- **Community:** political authority is founded on consent. But the moral self stays social: Hume calls the individual before society "a mere philosophical fiction," and Smith's inner judge is "the representative of mankind, and substitute of the Deity."
- **Autonomy:** Kant's self-given law is universal, reached by abstracting "from the personal differences of rational beings." It is the opposite of singularity.

### 3. Difference arrived later, still inside a shared frame (§4b)

From Rousseau to Emerson, difference becomes a value ("I am not made like any one I have been acquainted with"). But each holds it inside something common: Rousseau's divine conscience "speaks to all hearts"; Emerson's Over-Soul; Mill's utility. Earlier writers value superiority (Cellini) or study themselves as a sample of "the entire form of human condition" (Montaigne).

### 4. Philosophy removed the frames in layers, but never made the Self its own origin (§5b)

- **Answerability went first:** Whitman ("I do not trouble my spirit to vindicate itself or be understood"), Wilde.
- **The common frame went next:** Stirner drops God, Man, society, morality and conscience; Nietzsche drops God and the herd.
- **Self-origination never arrives.** Stirner's "unique one" is a "creative nothing." Nietzsche calls the wish to "absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society" a self-contradiction of "the half-educated."

### 5. The culture made the self a cause first, and its own origin later (C5.14)

- **Step 1, tested:** the 1890s mind-cure movement made the self the *cause* of its conditions ("Within yourself lies the cause of whatever enters into your life"), still powered by a borrowed Infinite. William James called it America's "only decidedly original contribution to the systematic philosophy of life."
- **Step 2, untested:** the borrowed power thins and the self becomes its own origin, answerable to nothing. Probably 20th century.

### 6. Blame comes from making the self the cause (§7)

Personal blame arrives *before* any frame is lost: Smiles (1859) blames misery "solely" on "the weakness, self-indulgence, and perverseness of man himself," with God's order in place. Nietzsche explains why it becomes unbearable: guilt turned inward becomes unpayable, and its one relief was God, "the one being who can deliver man."

### 7. §8: every age dresses conformity in its highest value word

The mechanism is old: "grace" (Castiglione, 1528, "a deliberate purpose to like that which perhaps he by nature dislikes"), the wish to rise (Mandeville, 1714), "reason and nature" (Adam Smith, 1759), station (Mill, 1859), the "natural" (Wilde, 1891), "his own idea" (Bernays, 1928). What is new is that the disguise word is now the Self itself, **"self-expression,"** and the motive has shifted from rising to **belonging**. Once the Self is the only vocabulary left (§5), conformity can only speak as the Self.

## What changed in the thesis

| Then (SPEC) | Now (tested) |
|---|---|
| The Self began as an acknowledgment of **difference** | It began as **two** acknowledgments: that each person stands answerable on their own, and that each can shape and present themselves (C1.1) |
| The Self is a Western invention of the Reformation, Descartes and Locke | Two bounded roots, humanist and Christian, both older than Luther (C4.21) |
| The Enlightenment separated the Self from religion and community | It separated the person from *institutions*; the Self stayed answerable and social (C4.30, C4.31) |
| The mutation is the Self becoming its own source | Philosophy never got there; the culture did, in two steps (C5.9, C5.14) |
| Blame is personal because other frames were lost | Blame is personal because the self was made the cause (C7.4) |
| §8's mechanism is the essay's discovery | The mechanism is centuries old; the new disguise word is the Self itself (C8.2, C8.7) |
| Locke's forensic Self is the root of modern guilt | Locke *limits* guilt; the genealogy rests on Nietzsche, who never mentions Locke (C7.1, C4.12) |
