## The argument in plain words

The texts changed the story. This is the version they currently support.

### 1. The Self began as answerability, not difference (§4)

Luther, Calvin, Descartes and Locke made each person **answerable on their own**: before God, through conscience and consciousness. None of them valued individual difference. They argued for equality and service ("no difference among them, save of office"; "We are not our own").

Every one of them **bounded** that answerability: by God, by the community, by the body, by limits on guilt (Locke: "no one shall be made to answer for what he knows nothing of").

> **The pattern:** the later Self kept the structure and dropped the limit. This holds in every section tested so far.

### 2. Difference arrived later, but always inside a shared frame (§4b)

From Rousseau to Emerson, difference becomes a value ("I am not made like any one I have been acquainted with"). But each author holds it inside something common: Rousseau's divine conscience "speaks to all hearts"; Emerson's Over-Soul; Mill's utility and "the rights and interests of others." Montaigne, earlier, studies himself only as a sample of "the entire form of human condition."

### 3. Philosophy removed the frames in layers, but never made the Self its own origin (§5b)

- **Answerability went first:** Whitman ("I do not trouble my spirit to vindicate itself or be understood"), Wilde.
- **The common frame went next:** Stirner drops God, Man, society, morality and conscience; Nietzsche drops God and the herd.
- **Self-origination never arrives.** Stirner's "unique one" is a "creative nothing." Nietzsche attacks the "I" as a fiction, and calls the wish to be one's own cause, to "absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society," a self-contradiction of **"the half-educated."**

### 4. The culture made the self a cause first, and its own origin later (§5, C5.14)

- **Step 1, tested:** the 1890s mind-cure movement made the self the *cause* of its own conditions ("Within yourself lies the cause of whatever enters into your life"), still powered by a borrowed Infinite. William James called it America's "only decidedly original contribution to the systematic philosophy of life."
- **Step 2, untested:** the borrowed power thins and the self becomes its own origin, answerable to nothing. Probably 20th century.

### 5. Blame comes from making the self the cause (§7)

Personal blame arrives *before* any frame is lost. Smiles (1859) blames misery "solely" on "the weakness, self-indulgence, and perverseness of man himself," with God's order fully in place. Mind-cure blames illness on the sufferer. Nietzsche explains why it becomes unbearable: guilt turned inward becomes unpayable, and its one relief was God, "the one being who can deliver man" (the creditor "playing scapegoat for his debtor"). Without that God, nothing can discharge it.

### 6. §8's mechanism is old; its vocabulary is new

Conformity felt as one's own choice is already in Mill (1859, in the language of rank), Wilde (1891, passed off as "natural") and Bernays (1928, choices engineered to feel like "his own idea"). What §8 can still claim as new is the idiom: **"self-expression" as the consumer's own word for conforming**, after 1928.

## What changed in the thesis

| Then (SPEC) | Now (tested) |
|---|---|
| The Self began as an acknowledgment of **difference** | It began as an acknowledgment that each person **stands answerable on their own** (C1.1) |
| The founders acknowledged difference against orders that denied it | They acknowledged equal standing and answerability; difference came later, always framed (C4.13, C4.15) |
| The mutation is the Self becoming its own source | Same, but philosophy never got there; the culture did, in two steps (C5.9, C5.14) |
| Blame is personal because other frames were lost | Blame is personal because the self was made the cause, even with frames intact (C7.4) |
| §8's mechanism is the essay's discovery | The mechanism is old; the idiom of self-expression is new (C8.2, C8.6) |
| Locke's forensic Self is the root of modern guilt | Locke *limits* guilt; the genealogy rests on Nietzsche, who never mentions Locke (C7.1, C4.12) |
