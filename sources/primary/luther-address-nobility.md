# Luther, *Address to the Christian Nobility of the German Nation* (1520)

- **Edition used:** trans. Wace & Buchheim, *First Principles of the Reformation* (1885 printing)
- **Text file:** `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` (OCR)
- **Standard modern edition** (for final citations): *Luther's Works* (American Edition), vol. 44
- **Claims to test:** C4.2, C4.3

## Passages

### luther-nobility.01 · "The First Wall": the priesthood of all believers

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for), C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The First Wall," Wace & Buchheim pp. 21–22 (check scan); `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 4398–4420
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up)

> It has been devised, that the Pope, bishops, priests and monks are called the Spiritual Estate; Princes, lords, artificers and peasants, are the Temporal Estate; which is a very fine, hypocritical device. But let no one be made afraid by it; and that for this reason: That all Christians are truly of the Spiritual Estate, and there is no difference among them, save of office alone. As St. Paul says (1 Cor. xii.), we are all one body, though each member does its own work, to serve the others. [...] Thus we are all consecrated as priests by baptism [...]

**For:** this attacks the social order that assigned spiritual standing by role, which is exactly the "role-assigning order" of C4.1. **Complicates:** the argument is made in the name of *one body* whose members serve one another. The individual is freed from the clerical hierarchy, not from the collective.

### luther-nobility.02 · Cobbler, smith, peasant: every calling a priestly office in one community

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against), C4.3 (complicates), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The First Wall," Wace & Buchheim p. 23; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 4506–4511
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up)

> A cobbler, a smith, a peasant, every man has the office and function of his calling, and yet all alike are consecrated priests and bishops, and every man in his office must be useful and beneficial to the rest, that so many kinds of work may all be united into one community: just as the members of the body all serve one another.

Luther does not free people from their socioeconomic roles. He sanctifies them, so the trade itself becomes a spiritual office within one body, which is the opposite of C4.2's "rather than fill socioeconomic roles." Spiritual standing is made equal across roles while the roles themselves stay in place.

### luther-nobility.03 · "The Second Wall": every Christian may judge by his own understanding of Scripture

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for)
- **Location:** "The Second Wall," Wace & Buchheim p. 27; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 4696–4699, 4706–4719
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; margin marks "r" and "I" removed at 4697–4698; "spirit of -liberty" → "spirit of liberty"; "St. Paulhas" → "St. Paul has")

> Besides that, we are all priests, as I have said, and have all one faith, one gospel, one sacrament; how then should we not have the power of discerning and judging what is right or wrong in matters of faith? [...] By these and many other texts we should gain courage and freedom, and should not let the spirit of liberty (as St. Paul has it) be frightened away by the inventions of the Popes; we should boldly judge what they do and what they leave undone, by our own understanding of the Scriptures, and force them to follow the better understanding, and not their own. [...] Therefore it behoves every Christian to aid the faith by understanding and defending it, and by condemning all errors.

This is the strongest "for" in the treatise. The right to judge in matters of faith passes from the papal office to every believer's "own understanding of the Scriptures." Note the limits: the judging is done by "we," it is bound to Scripture, and its aim is "the better understanding," not one's own view.

### luther-nobility.04 · "The Third Wall": the temporal authorities should call the council

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Third Wall," Wace & Buchheim pp. 28–29; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 4764–4776
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; "fellow - Christians" → "fellow-Christians")

> Therefore when need requires and the Pope is a cause of offence to Christendom, in these cases whoever can best do so, as a faithful member of the whole body, must do what he can to procure a true free council. This no one can do so well as the temporal authorities, especially since they are fellow-Christians, fellow-priests, sharing one spirit, and one power in all things; and since they should exercise the office that they have received from God without hindrance, whenever it is necessary and useful that it should be exercised.

Freeing believers from the papal structure is, in practice, handed to princes and magistrates. The treatise is addressed to them. Authority over the church moves sideways to the temporal power, not down to the individual.

### luther-nobility.05 · Clerical celibacy: an order forbidding what nature requires

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** proposal 14, Wace & Buchheim pp. 59–61; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 6206–6211, 6241–6243, 6290–6292
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; "Bonie" → "Rome"; stray "\h." and "/" removed at 6290–6292)

> My advice is, to restore liberty, and to leave every man free to marry or not to marry. But if we did this we should have to introduce a very different rule and order for property; the whole canon law would be overthrown and but few benefices would fall to Rome. I am afraid greed was a cause of this wretched, unchaste chastity; [...] for human frailty does not allow men to live an unmarried life, but only angelic fortitude and celestial virtue. [...] The Pope having no authority for such a command, any more than to forbid a man to eat and drink, or to digest or to grow fat, no one is bound to obey it

The best "for" for C4.1 in the three treatises. A role-assigning order (the priestly estate and its celibacy) is said to repress something already present (bodily nature), and to do it for property and benefices. For C4.13, note that what is acknowledged is a *common* human frailty, not individual difference.

### luther-nobility.06 · Pilgrimages: the "self-willed" path against the duties of one's estate

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against), C4.13 (against)
- **Location:** proposal 12, Wace & Buchheim pp. 55–56; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 5992–6002, 6047–6050
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; hyphenated line-breaks joined: "pilgrim- age," "dis- obedience," "self- willed"; "Eome" → "Rome")

> [...] for God has not commanded it. But He has commanded that each man should care for his wife and children and whatever concerns the married state; and should, besides, serve and help his neighbour. Now it often happens that one goes on a pilgrimage to Rome, spends fifty or one hundred guilders, more or less, which no one has commanded him, while his wife and children, or those dearest to him, are left at home in want and misery; and yet he thinks, poor foolish man, to atone for this disobedience and contempt of God's commandments by his self-willed pilgrimage [...] No one is content to walk on the broad high road of God's commandments; every one makes for himself new roads and new vows, as if he had kept all God's commandments.

Luther's complaint here is against people choosing their own religious path ("self-willed," "makes for himself new roads"). He sends them back to the duties of household and neighbour. This is a direct "against" for any reading of Luther as championing self-directed lives.

### luther-nobility.07 · Abolish begging: let each work and each town keep its own poor

- **Claims:** C4.2 (complicates), C4.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** proposal 21, Wace & Buchheim pp. 70–71; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 6701–6705, 6738–6745
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up)

> It is one of the most urgent necessities to abolish all begging in Christendom. No one should go about begging among Christians. [...] each town should support its own poor [...] If a man will be poor, he should not be rich; if he will be rich, let him put his hand to the plough, and get wealth himself out of the earth. It is enough to provide decently for the poor, that they may not die of cold and hunger. It is not right, that one should work that another may be idle, and live ill that another may live well, as is now the perverse abuse, for St. Paul says (2 Thess. iii. 10): "If any would not work, neither should he eat."

This is the passage that leans most toward a work ethic: wealth through one's own labour, and no living off others. But the labour is agricultural ("out of the earth"), and the answer to poverty is town-by-town communal provision. It looks more like a reformed parish economy than the "rise of capital."

### luther-nobility.08 · "Every man wishes to be every other man's equal": self-will condemned

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against), C4.13 (against)
- **Location:** proposal 27 (defects of the temporal estates), Wace & Buchheim p. 88; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 7535–7538, 7545–7550
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up)

> In the first place, we require a general law and consent of the German nation against profusion and extravagance in dress, which is the cause of so much poverty among the nobles and the people. [...] As it is we see that every man wishes to be every other man's equal, and that this causes and increases pride and envy among us, as we deserve; all which would cease, with many other misfortunes, if our self-will would but let us be gratefully content with what God has given us.

Luther does see people trying to move out of their assigned rank, and he condemns it as "self-will," asking for a sumptuary law to keep the ranks visible. Spiritual equality (.01) does not carry over into social equality. A clear "against" for C4.2 and C4.13, if either is read as Luther's own view.

### luther-nobility.09 · The Fuggers and interest: "much more godly to encourage agriculture and lessen commerce"

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against), C4.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** proposal 27, Wace & Buchheim p. 89; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 7595–7609
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up)

> Doubtless we should also find some bridle for the Fuggers and similar companies. Is it possible that in a single man's lifetime such great wealth should be collected together, if all were done rightly and according to God's will? I am not skilled in accounts. But I do not understand how it is possible for one hundred guilders to gain twenty in a year, or how one guilder can gain another, and that not out of the soil, or by cattle, seeing that possessions depend not on the wit of men, but on the blessing of God. [...] All I know is that it were much more godly to encourage agriculture and lessen commerce; and that they do the best who, according to the Scriptures, till the ground to get their living

Luther meets the rising capital of his day (the Fuggers, interest, credit; see also lines 7564–7574 on "buying on credit": "The Devil invented this system") with hostility and calls for restraint. C4.2 and C4.5 are causal claims, so this cannot refute them alone. But it does rule out using Luther as a witness *for* them. If capital helped produce the Self, it did so against Luther's intentions.
