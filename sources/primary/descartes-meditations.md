# Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy* (1641)

- **Edition used:** trans. Haldane & Ross, *Philosophical Works* vol. I (1911)
- **Text files:** `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` (OCR); `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` (an early English translation, for reception)
- **Standard reference** (for final citations): AT vol. VII; CSM vol. II
- **Claims to test:** C4.6, C4.7, C4.8, C5.8

## Passages

### descartes-meditations.01 · Meditation II — "a thing which thinks"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation II, Haldane & Ross I p. 153 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` line 7468
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "isa" corrected to "is a")

> But what then am I? A thing which thinks. What is a thing which thinks? It is a thing which doubts, understands, [conceives], affirms, denies, wills, refuses, which also imagines and feels.

Identity is located in thinking. Note that the list includes *willing* and *feeling*: the "thinking thing" already holds the feelings, will and desires that the spec's definition of the Self names (§2).

### descartes-meditations.02 · Meditation VI — not a pilot in a vessel

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C4.7 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation VI, Haldane & Ross I p. 192 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 9478–9481
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> Nature also teaches me by these sensations of pain, hunger, thirst, etc., that I am not only lodged in my body as a pilot in a vessel, but that I am very closely united to it, and so to speak so intermingled with it that I seem to compose with it one whole.

Descartes himself resists the pure "ghost in a machine" picture. That's why C4.7 matters: if the culture took the separate Mind from Descartes, it kept the first half and dropped the union, which Descartes states in every major work (*Discourse* V, descartes-discourse.02; *Passions* I.30, descartes-passions.02). The gap between what Descartes said and what was received is evidence for a reception claim, but it can't be cited as Descartes's own view.

### descartes-meditations.03 · Meditation II — "Am I so dependent on body and senses?"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation II, Haldane & Ross I p. 150 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 7322–7333
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "‘Then" corrected to "Then"). The cogito sentence that follows (line 7335, "this proposition: Lam, I mentally conceive it") has lost a line in the OCR; the Molyneux rendering is in descartes-meditations.12.

> But I have already denied that I had senses and body. Yet I hesitate, for what follows from that? Am I so dependent on body and senses that I cannot exist without these? But I was persuaded that there was nothing in all the world, that there was no heaven, no earth, that there were no minds, nor any bodies: was I not then likewise persuaded that I did not exist? Not at all; of a surety I myself did exist since I persuaded myself of something [or merely because I thought of something]. [...] Then without doubt I exist also if he deceives me, and let him deceive me as much as he will, he can never cause me to be nothing so long as I think that I am something.

The "I" is certain while the whole world ("no heaven, no earth") is supposed away. This is the textual core of C5.8: the self is reached by subtracting the world, and survives the subtraction. But note it is a claim about *certainty*, made before Descartes has said what the I is (see .04).

### descartes-meditations.04 · Meditation II — "I am not sure about this"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation II, Haldane & Ross I p. 152 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 7424–7437
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> I am not a wind, a fire, a vapour, a breath, nor anything at all which I can imagine or conceive; because I have assumed that all these were nothing. Without changing that supposition I find that I only leave myself certain of the fact that I am somewhat. But perhaps it is true that these same things which I supposed were non-existent because they are unknown to me, are really not different from the self which I know. I am not sure about this, I shall not dispute about it now; I can only give judgment on things that are known to me. I know that I exist, and I inquire what I am, I whom I know to exist. But it is very certain that the knowledge of my existence taken in its precise significance does not depend on things whose existence is not yet known to me [...]

Decisive for how to cite Med II: Descartes explicitly leaves open that the I is "not different from" the bodily things set aside. Med II separates mind and body only in the order of *knowing*; the real distinction waits for Med VI (he says so to Hobbes, descartes-meditations.13). For C5.8 the last sentence is the strongest form: self-knowledge does not depend on knowing the world.

### descartes-meditations.05 · Meditation III — "holding converse only with myself"

- **Claims:** C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation III, opening, Haldane & Ross I p. 157 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 7705–7711
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "| shall" corrected to "I shall", "J am" to "I am")

> I shall now close my eyes, I shall stop my ears, I shall call away all my senses, I shall efface even from my thoughts all the images of corporeal things, or at least (for that is hardly possible) I shall esteem them as vain and false; and thus holding converse only with myself and considering my own nature, I shall try little by little to reach a better knowledge of and a more familiar acquaintanceship with myself.

The method itself is withdrawal: self-knowledge is pursued by shutting the world out. Note the aside "for that is hardly possible": Descartes concedes he cannot actually empty the mind of the corporeal, only discount it.

### descartes-meditations.06 · Meditation III — not the author of my own existence

- **Claims:** C5.8 (against)
- **Location:** Meditation III, Haldane & Ross I pp. 168, 170 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 8298–8300, 8312–8319, 8418–8422
- **Checked:** text file (OCR). The sentence opening this argument is garbled across the page break at lines 8284–8288 ("author of my being, I should doubt nothing an / nothing"), so it is not quoted.

> But it is clear that if I had of myself possessed this greater perfection of which I have just spoken [that is to say, if I had been the author of my own existence], I should not at least have denied myself the things which are the more easy to acquire [...] For all the course of my life may be divided into an infinite number of parts, none of which is in any way dependent on the other; and thus from the fact that I was in existence a short time ago it does not follow that I must be in existence now, unless some cause at this instant, so to speak, produces me anew, that is to say, conserves me. [...] when I reflect on myself I not only know that I am something [imperfect], incomplete and dependent on another [...]

The strongest text against reading Descartes's self as "self-originating" (the spec's *Singularity*, §2): the thinking thing is "incomplete and dependent", held in being moment by moment by God. Descartes's mind is prior to the *material* world, not prior to everything.

### descartes-meditations.07 · Meditation VI — the real distinction: "can exist without it"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation VI, Haldane & Ross I p. 190 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 9380–9393
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> [...] just because I know certainly that I exist, and that meanwhile I do not remark that any other thing necessarily pertains to my nature or essence, excepting that I am a thinking thing, I rightly conclude that my essence consists solely in the fact that I am a thinking thing [or a substance whose whole essence or nature is to think]. And although possibly (or rather certainly, as I shall say in a moment) I possess a body with which I am very intimately conjoined, yet because, on the one side, I have a clear and distinct idea of myself inasmuch as I am only a thinking and unextended thing, and as, on the other, I possess a distinct idea of body, inasmuch as it is only an extended and unthinking thing, it is certain that this I [that is to say, my soul by which I am what I am], is entirely and absolutely distinct from my body, and can exist without it.

This is the passage that decides C4.6 *for*, in Descartes's own voice and as his conclusion, not a stage in the doubt. Even here the union is flagged in the same sentence ("very intimately conjoined"), so the claim should be cited with .02 and .08 beside it.

### descartes-meditations.08 · Meditation VI — "myself in my entirety [...] formed of body and soul"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C4.7 (for)
- **Location:** Meditation VI, Haldane & Ross I pp. 192, 193, 195 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 9502–9505, 9539–9540, 9647–9650
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> [...] it is quite certain that my body (or rather myself in my entirety, inasmuch as I am formed of body and soul) may receive different impressions agreeable and disagreeable from the other bodies which surround it. [...] for in talking of nature I only treat of those things given by God to me as a being composed of mind and body. [...] yet in regard to the composite whole, that is to say, to the mind or soul united to this body, it is not a purely verbal predicate, but a real error of nature [...]

Med VI has *two* uses of "I": the I that is "only a thinking thing" (.07) and "myself in my entirety", the mind-body composite, which is the subject of sensation, appetite and error. C4.6 holds for the first and not the second. For C4.7, the second "I" is the one the received picture drops.

### descartes-meditations.09 · Synopsis — distinct and "a single thing"; the world less certain than mind and God

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Synopsis of the Six Meditations, Haldane & Ross I pp. 142–143 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 6967–6973, 6981–6989
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "ts" corrected to "is", "ure" to "are", "sce" to "see")

> I here show that the mind of man is really distinct from the body, and at the same time that the two are so closely joined together that they form, so to speak, a single thing. [...] Not that I judge them to be very useful in establishing that which they prove, to wit, that there is in truth a world, that men possess bodies, and other such things which never have been doubted by anyone of sense; but because in considering these closely we come to see that they are neither so strong nor so evident as those arguments which lead us to the knowledge of our mind and of God; so that these last must be the most certain and most evident facts which can fall within the cognizance of the human mind.

Descartes's own summary of Med VI states both halves together, so neither can be quoted as "his view" alone. The second sentence is the cleanest statement of the *epistemic* priority behind C5.8: mind and God are more certain than "that there is in truth a world".

### descartes-meditations.10 · Synopsis — the mind "a pure substance", immortal

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for), C4.8 (for)
- **Location:** Synopsis of the Six Meditations, Haldane & Ross I p. 141 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 6897–6908
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "JSrom" corrected to "from", "ts" to "is", "them)|" to "them)]")

> [...] the human body, inasmuch as it differs from other bodies, is composed only of a certain configuration of members and of other similar accidents, while the human mind is not similarly composed of any accidents, but is a pure substance. For although all the accidents of mind be changed, although, for instance, it think certain things, will others, perceive others, etc., despite all this it does not emerge from these changes another mind [...] From this it follows that the human body may indeed easily enough perish, but the mind [or soul of man (I make no distinction between them)] is owing to its nature immortal.

The separation serves an old end: the immortality of the soul, the *Phaedo*'s question, given a new demonstration. This supports the Descartes side of C4.8 ("groundwork ... made operational"). Note also "I make no distinction" between mind and soul: Descartes collapses the two, which is part of what "Self = Mind" means.

### descartes-meditations.11 · Molyneux 1680 — title page and translator's preface

- **Claims:** C4.7 (for)
- **Location:** Molyneux, *Six Metaphysical Meditations* (London, 1680), title page and preface (Dublin, Feb. 1679/80); `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` lines 44–48, 117–120
- **Checked:** text file (Project Gutenberg transcription; layout of title page flattened; `~ ~` markup removed)

> Wherein it is Proved That there is a GOD. And that Mans MIND is really distinct from his BODY.

> And all that are acquainted with this famous Authors design, do very well know, that it was his intention in these Meditations Mathematically to demonstrate, that there is a God, and that mans mind is incorporeal.

The first English *Meditations* is sold to English readers as a proof that the mind is distinct and "incorporeal"; the union is not mentioned in the packaging. This is evidence for how the work was framed in English, not yet evidence for how it was read.

### descartes-meditations.12 · Molyneux 1680 — Meditation II: "that very Self, which I know"

- **Claims:** C4.7 (complicates), C4.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Molyneux, Meditation 2; `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` lines 626–629, 686–694, 704–710
- **Checked:** text file (Gutenberg; italics markup `_ _` removed, spelling kept)

> Wherefore I may lay this down as a Principle, that whenever this sentence I am, I exist, is spoken or thought of by Me, ’tis necessarily True. [...] Here I have discovered it, ’tis my Thought, this alone cannot be separated from Me, I am, I exist,⸺tis true, but for what time Am I? Why I am as long as I think; For it May be that When I cease from thinking, I may cease from being. Now I admit of nothing but what is necessarily true: In short therefore I am only a thinking thing that is to say, a mind, or a soul, or understanding, or Reason [...] Yet perhaps it so falls out that these very things which I suppose not to exist (because to me unknown) are in reallity nothing different from that very Self, which I know. I cannot tell, I dispute it not now [...]

English readers in 1680 got the Med II hedge intact ("I cannot tell, I dispute it not now"), so the text did not hide it. Of note for §5: the 1680 English already uses "Self" as a noun, italicised, for the thinking I.

### descartes-meditations.13 · Molyneux 1680 — Hobbes's objection to Med II, and Descartes's reply

- **Claims:** C4.7 (for), C4.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Molyneux, Objections of Hobbes, "Against the Second Meditation" and Answer; `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` lines 2604–2608, 2661–2667, 2675–2679
- **Checked:** text file (Gutenberg; italics markup removed)

> It may be therefore that a Thinking Thing is the Subject of a Mind, Reason, or Understanding, and therefor it may be a Corporeal Thing, the Contrary Whereof is here Assumed and not Proved; and yet this Inference is the Foundation of that Conclusion which D.Cartes would Establish.

> Yet I willingly confess, that I have (as much as in me lay) made use of abstracted words to signifie that thing or substance, which I would have devested of all those things that belong not to it. Whereas contrarily this Philosopher uses the most concrete Words to signifie this thinking thing, such as subject, matter, Body, &c. that he may not suffer it to be separated from Body. [...] It may be (sayes he) that a Thinking thing is a corporeal thing, the contrary whereof is here assumed and not proved. But herein he is mistaken, for I never assumed the contrary, neither have I used it as a Foundation, for the rest of my Superstructure, but left it wholly undetermin’d till the sixth Meditation, and in that ’tis proved.

Reception from the start: an English contemporary, Hobbes, took the cogito as asserting an incorporeal thinking thing, and printed with the 1680 translation is that objection. Descartes denies that Med II assumes it, but admits choosing "abstracted words" to strip the thinking thing of body. Supports C4.7 (the separation is what readers fastened on) while showing it was contested, not passively absorbed.

### descartes-meditations.14 · Molyneux 1680 — Meditation VI: distinct, yet "Blended therewith"

- **Claims:** C4.7 (against), C4.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Molyneux, Meditation 6; `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` lines 2155–2160, 2231–2244, 2253–2256
- **Checked:** text file (Gutenberg; italics markup removed)

> [...] ’tis from hence certain, that I am really distinct from my Body, and that I can exist without it. [...] My Nature tells me that I am not in my Body, as a Mariner is in his Ship, but that I am most nighly conjoyn’d thereto, and as it were Blended therewith; so that I with It make up one thing; For Otherwise, when the Body were hurt, I, who am only a Thinking Thing, should not therefore feel Pain, but should only perceive the Hurt with the Eye of my Understanding (as a Mariner perceives by his sight whatever is broken in his Ship) [...] arising from the Union and (as it were) mixture of the Mind and Body. [...] my Body, or rather my Whole self (as I am compounded of a Mind and Body) [...]

The 1680 English gives the union *more* strongly than Haldane & Ross (no "I seem to compose"; flatly "I with It make up one thing"; "my Whole self ... compounded of a Mind and Body"). So the English reader was not denied the union. If English culture received only the separate Mind, that was selection by readers, not the text available to them. C4.7 survives only as a claim about selective reception, and needs later evidence (Locke, More, Stillingfleet) to show the selection happened.

### descartes-meditations.15 · Molyneux 1680 — Hobbes: the doubt is Plato's; the preface: the Fathers

- **Claims:** C4.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** Molyneux, Objections of Hobbes, "Against the First Meditation" and Answer; preface; `texts/descartes-meditations-molyneux-1680.txt` lines 2558–2563, 2568–2571, 332–335
- **Checked:** text file (Gutenberg; italics markup removed)

> But Because Plato and other Antient Philosophers argued for the same incertainty in sensible Things, and because ’tis commonly Observed by the Vulgar that ’tis hard to Distinguish Sleep from Waking, I would not have the most excellent Author of such new Thoughts put forth so antique Notions.

> [...] I made use of them not that I may vend them as new, but partly that I may prepare the Minds of my Readers for the Consideration of Intellectual Things [...]

> Those that assert these Arguments to have been long before thought upon by some of the Fathers, I shall refer to our Authors just vindication of himself in his several Answers to Objections made against these Discourses.

Early readers already traced Descartes to Plato and "the Fathers", and Descartes concedes the doubts are not new. But Hobbes links Plato to the *doubt about the senses*, not to the separate soul; the Augustine link (C4.8 pressure point, "si fallor, sum") is not in our texts; it is usually traced to Arnauld's Fourth Objections (to verify).
