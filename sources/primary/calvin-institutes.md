## Passages

### calvin-institutes.01 · III.xxiv.5 — Christ as the mirror of election

- **Claims:** C4.4 (against, as a claim about Calvin himself)
- **Location:** III.xxiv.5; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 9427–9430
- **Checked:** text file

> But if we are chosen in him, we shall find no assurance of our election in ourselves; nor even in God the Father, considered alone, abstractedly from the Son. Christ, therefore, is the mirror, in which it behoves us to contemplate our election; and here we may do it with safety.

Calvin tells believers *not* to look for proof of election in themselves. The practical drive to prove election through work (C4.4) is not found in Calvin's own teaching here. It may still be true of later Calvinism, which is Weber's actual claim. **C4.4 likely needs revising** from "Calvin's predestination drove..." to "Calvinist predestination, as lived by later generations, drove..." (But see .02–.05: Calvin does allow works as a *secondary* confirmation.)

### calvin-institutes.02 · III.xiv.18 — works confirm a conscience already founded on grace

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.xiv.18; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 686–701
- **Checked:** text file

> A conscience thus founded, built up, and established, is also confirmed by the consideration of works; that is, as far as they are evidences of God dwelling and reigning in us. [...] We do not forbid him to support and confirm this faith by marks of the Divine benevolence to him. For if, when we call to remembrance the various gifts which God has conferred on us, they are all as so many rays from the Divine countenance, by which we are illuminated to contemplate the full blaze of supreme goodness,—much more the grace of good works, which demonstrates that we have received the Spirit of adoption.

The answer to the key question is yes: Calvin does allow works as *evidence*, and says good works "demonstrate" adoption. But only for a conscience "thus founded" on grace first. This is the seed of what later became the practical syllogism. It partly rescues C4.4, as a claim about a door Calvin left open.

### calvin-institutes.03 · III.xiv.19–20 — "whence they infer their election", with a warning

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.xiv.19–20; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 703–706, 719–722, 737–741
- **Checked:** text file

> When the saints, therefore, confirm their faith, or derive matter of rejoicing from the integrity of their consciences, they only conclude, from the fruits of vocation, that they have been adopted by the Lord as his children. [...] For if they begin to estimate it by their good works, nothing will be weaker or more uncertain; for, if their works be estimated in themselves, their imperfection will menace them with the wrath of God, as much as their purity, however incomplete, testifies his benevolence. [...] We see now, that the confidence which the saints have in their works is not such as either ascribes any thing to the merit of them, (since they view them only as the gifts of God, in which they acknowledge his goodness, and as marks of their calling, whence they infer their election,) or derogates the least from the gratuitous righteousness which we obtain in Christ

The strongest text for C4.4: works are "marks of their calling, whence they infer their election." Yet the same passage says that *estimating* election by works makes it "weaker or more uncertain". So Calvin allows inference from works but forbids making works the test. The anxious proving-by-works of C4.4 is the second thing, which he rules out.

### calvin-institutes.04 · III.xxi.7 — sanctification as an index, both ways

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.xxi.7; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 7720–7725
- **Checked:** text file

> In the elect, we consider calling as an evidence of election, and justification as another token of its manifestation, till they arrive in glory, which constitutes its completion. As God seals his elect by vocation and justification, so by excluding the reprobate from the knowledge of his name and the sanctification of his Spirit, he affords an indication of the judgment that awaits them.

The reverse of .02: *absence* of sanctification is "an indication" of reprobation. A reader who fears that indication has a motive to show sanctification. Calvin does not draw that inference, but the text supplies its premises.

### calvin-institutes.05 · III.xxiii.12 — election aims at holiness, and only the elect strive

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates; for, on the "in practice it drives effort" half)
- **Location:** III.xxiii.12; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 8984–8995
- **Checked:** text file

> But Paul declares the end of our election to be, that we may lead a holy and blameless life. If the object of election be holiness of life, it should rather awaken and stimulate us to a cheerful practice of it, than be used as a pretext for slothfulness. [...] For whence could such exertion originate but from election?

Calvin answers the fatalist objection by saying predestination should *stimulate* effort, and that real moral striving can only come from election. Combined with .03, this makes effort a symptom of election. The tone is "cheerful", not anxious.

### calvin-institutes.06 · III.xxi.1 — the doctrine as a cure for anxiety

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.xxi.1; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 7383–7389
- **Checked:** text file

> Nor is there any other basis for solid confidence, even according to the authority of Christ, who, to deliver us from all fear, and render us invincible amidst so many dangers, snares, and deadly conflicts, promises to preserve in safety all whom the Father has committed to his care. Whence we infer, that they who know not themselves to be God’s peculiar people will be tortured with continual anxiety

Calvin presents predestination as the *remedy* for anxiety, not its cause. This runs against the Weber/Fromm picture of the doctrine producing dread. The anxiety it cures, though, is one the doctrine names and makes central.

### calvin-institutes.07 · III.xxiv.4 — the anxiety is real; the remedy is the calling, not works

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.xxiv.4; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 9354–9357, 9370–9375, 9389–9390
- **Checked:** text file

> we shall observe the best order, if, in seeking an assurance of our election, we confine our attention to those subsequent signs which are certain attestations of it. Satan never attacks believers with a more grievous or dangerous temptation, than when he disquiets them with doubts of their election [...] For there is scarcely a person to be found, whose mind is not sometimes struck with this thought—Whence can you obtain salvation but from the election of God? And what revelation have you received of election? If this has once impressed a man, it either perpetually excruciates the unhappy being with dreadful torments, or altogether stupefies him with astonishment. [...] Let this, then, be our way of inquiry; to begin and end with the calling of God.

Calvin himself reports the anxiety Weber and Fromm describe, and says it is near-universal ("scarcely a person"). That supports their psychological premise. But his "subsequent signs" here are the calling and the word (xxiv.5–6), not works or worldly success.

### calvin-institutes.08 · III.xxiv.7 — the reprobate can show the same signs

- **Claims:** C4.4 (against)
- **Location:** III.xxiv.7; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol2.txt` lines 9526–9529
- **Checked:** text file

> I dispute not their having similar signs of calling with the elect; but I am far from admitting them to possess that certain assurance of election which I enjoin believers to seek from the word of the gospel.

Outward signs cannot prove election, because those who fall away had "similar signs." This cuts directly against *proving* election by visible conduct. Assurance is sought "from the word of the gospel."

### calvin-institutes.09 · III.vii.1 — "We are not our own"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (complicates), C4.13 (against), C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.vii.1; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 23993–23998
- **Checked:** text file

> We are not our own; therefore neither our reason nor our will should predominate in our deliberations and actions. We are not our own; therefore let us not propose it as our end, to seek what may be expedient for us according to the flesh. We are not our own; therefore let us, as far as possible, forget ourselves and all things that are ours.

The centre of Calvin's ethics is the opposite of the SPEC §2 Self ("an individual's feelings, will, and desires are distinct from those of any collective"): one's own will is to be surrendered, not acknowledged. Calvin cannot be read as "acknowledging difference" (C4.13). At most the Reformation individuates *responsibility before God* while dissolving the self's claims.

### calvin-institutes.10 · III.vii.4 — self-love as "a kingdom in their own breast"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (complicates), C4.13 (against)
- **Location:** III.vii.4; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 24122–24127
- **Checked:** text file

> The poor yield to the rich, plebeians to nobles, servants to masters, the illiterate to the learned; but there is no man who does not cherish within him some idea of his own excellence. Thus all men, in flattering themselves, carry, as it were, a kingdom in their own breast; for arrogating to themselves the height of self‐gratulation, they pass censure on the understandings and conduct of others

Calvin sees something like a self-sovereign Self already present under the role-bound order of rich and poor, nobles and plebeians. That fits C4.1's "already present" in an unexpected way. But for Calvin it is fallen nature to be rooted out, not a truth to be acknowledged.

### calvin-institutes.11 · III.vii.9 — do not strive for wealth or credit your own industry

- **Claims:** C4.4 (against), C4.5 (against)
- **Location:** III.vii.9; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 24320–24326, 24345–24350
- **Checked:** text file

> Therefore, if we believe that all the cause of desirable prosperity consists in the Divine benediction alone, without which miseries and calamities of every kind await us, it follows also, that we should not passionately strive for wealth and honours, either relying on our own diligence or acuteness of understanding, or depending on the favour of men, or confiding in a vain imagination of chance [...] he who shall repose himself, in the manner we have mentioned, on the Divine blessing, will neither hunt after the objects violently coveted by men in general, by evil methods, from which he will expect no advantage; nor will he impute any prosperous event to himself, and to his own diligence, industry, or good fortune

Calvin forbids exactly the self-made economic actor: no passionate striving, no crediting success to one's "diligence, industry". Prosperity is not a sign of election here; he notes elsewhere in the section that "impious men" accumulate "enormous wealth" (line 24313).

### calvin-institutes.12 · III.x.4–5 — frugality and stewardship

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates: for "frugality", but not tied to election)
- **Location:** III.x.4–5; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 25160–25166, 25187–25194
- **Checked:** text file

> Therefore, though the liberty of believers in external things cannot be reduced to certain rules, yet it is evidently subject to this law, That they should indulge themselves as little as possible; that, on the contrary, they should perpetually and resolutely exert themselves to retrench all superfluities and to restrain luxury [...] they are, as it were, deposits intrusted to our care, of which we must one day give an account. We ought, therefore, to manage them in such a manner that this alarm may be incessantly sounding in our ears, “Give an account of thy stewardship.” Let it also be remembered by whom this account is demanded; that it is by him who has so highly recommended abstinence, sobriety, frugality, and modesty

The "frugality" half of C4.4 is in Calvin's own text, framed as stewardship under constant accountability. It is not framed as proof of election. Note that III.x.2–3 also rejects strict asceticism and allows pleasure in goods.

### calvin-institutes.13 · III.x.6 — vocation as "a post assigned him by the Lord"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (against), C4.2 (against), C4.13 (against), C4.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.x.6; `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 25204–25209, 25223–25227, 25235–25237
- **Checked:** text file

> he has appointed to all their particular duties in different spheres of life. And that no one might rashly transgress the limits prescribed, he has styled such spheres of life vocations, or callings. Every individual’s line of life, therefore, is, as it were, a post assigned him by the Lord, that he may not wander about in uncertainty all his days. [...] no one will be impelled by his own temerity to attempt more than is compatible with his calling, because he will know that it is unlawful to transgress the bounds assigned him. He that is in obscurity will lead a private life without discontent, so as not to desert the station in which God has placed him. [...] there will be no employment so mean and sordid (provided we follow our vocation) as not to appear truly respectable, and be deemed highly important in the sight of God.

Probably the most decisive passage against the §4 story. Calvin's doctrine of calling is itself a role-assigning order: stay at your "post", do not "transgress the bounds assigned". It is not about persons directing their own futures. What it does change is the *value* of ordinary work: every station is "highly important in the sight of God". That is Weber's *Beruf*, not the Self.

### calvin-institutes.14 · II.viii.45–46 — property as God's distribution; content with one's lot

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates), C4.2 (against)
- **Location:** II.viii.45–46 (eighth commandment); `texts/calvin-institutes-vol1.txt` lines 14160–14162, 14195–14197
- **Checked:** text file

> For we ought to consider, that what a man possesses has fallen to his lot, not by a fortuitous contingency, but by the distribution of the supreme Lord of all [...] We shall rightly obey this commandment therefore, if, contented with our own lot, we seek no gain but in an honest and lawful way; if we neither desire to enrich ourselves by injustice, nor attempt to ruin the fortune of our neighbour, in order to increase our own

Honest gain is permitted, so Calvin is no enemy of commerce. But possessions are allotted by God and the believer is "contented with our own lot." This is an economy of stations, not a "freer economic situation" setting the Self loose (C4.5).
