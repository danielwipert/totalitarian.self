# Mandeville, *The Fable of the Bees* (1714–29)

- **Edition used:** London: T. Ostell, 1806 (Parts I and II in one volume), via Project Gutenberg #57260
- **Text file:** `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): ed. F. B. Kaye, 2 vols (Oxford, 1924)
- **Citation form:** part + section (poem line, or "Remark on l. N" as this edition heads the Remarks), plus this file's line
- **Claims to test:** C4.31, C4.29, C4.5, C8.1, C8.2, C8.4

This edition heads each Remark by the poem line it glosses ("Line 182. And odious pride a million more"), not by the letters (A)–(Y) of Kaye. The edition prints a line number after every fifth verse line; in the quotations below it is given in brackets, e.g. [l. 155], where it falls. Verse line breaks are not marked.

## Passages

### mandeville.01 · "The Grumbling Hive", ll. 31–34, 155–168 — "Each other's lust and vanity"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, the poem; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 355–358, 479–492
- **Checked:** text file

> Vast numbers throng'd the fruitful hive; Yet those vast numbers made 'em thrive; Millions endeavouring to supply Each other's lust and vanity;

> Thus every part was full of vice, [l. 155] Yet the whole mass a paradise; [...] Such were the blessings of that state; Their crimes conspir'd to make them great: [...] The worst of all the multitude Did something for the common good.

Self-interest is the engine of society, but it works only *between* bees: each supplies the others' vanity. This is a society made of selves, not selves standing before or apart from society.

### mandeville.02 · "The Grumbling Hive", "The Moral", ll. 415–429 — vice "by justice lopp'd and bound"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, the poem's Moral; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 745–759
- **Checked:** text file

> Fraud, luxury, and pride must live, [l. 415] While we the benefits receive: [...] So vice is beneficial found, [l. 425] When it's by justice lopp'd and bound; Nay, where the people would be great, As necessary to the state, As hunger is to make 'em eat.

Even the most famous apologist for self-interest bounds it: vice is useful only when cut and tied, like a vine. The limit (justice, law) is part of the doctrine, not an afterthought, which fits the §4 pattern.

### mandeville.03 · "Origin of Moral Virtue" ¶1 — "no creature besides himself can ever be made sociable"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (for), C4.29 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, "An Inquiry into the Origin of Moral Virtue" ¶1; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 807–820
- **Checked:** text file

> All untaught animals are only solicitous of pleasing themselves, and naturally follow the bent of their own inclinations, without considering the good or harm that, from their being pleased, will accrue to others. [...] no species of animals is, without the curb of government, less capable of agreeing long together in multitudes, than that of man; yet such are his qualities, whether good or bad I shall not determine, that no creature besides himself can ever be made sociable: but being an extraordinary selfish and headstrong, as well as cunning animal, however he may be subdued by superior strength, it is impossible by force alone to make him tractable, and receive the improvements he is capable of.

The strongest "for" in Mandeville: man comes before society as a selfish animal, and sociability has to be *made*. But he is made social by government and management, not by his own consent (see .04), so this is a prior individual without a social contract.

### mandeville.04 · "Origin of Moral Virtue" — "the political offspring which flattery begot upon pride"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.29 (for), C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, "Origin of Moral Virtue" ¶¶2–3, ¶13; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 822–848, 1018–1024
- **Checked:** text file

> The chief thing, therefore, which lawgivers, and other wise men that have laboured for the establishment of society, have endeavoured, has been to make the people they were to govern, believe, that it was more beneficial for every body to conquer than indulge his appetites, and much better to mind the public than what seemed his private interest. [...] they were forced to contrive an imaginary one, that, as a general equivalent for the trouble of self-denial, should serve on all occasions, and without costing any thing either to themselves or others, be yet a most acceptable recompence to the receivers. They thoroughly examined all the strength and frailties of our nature, and observing that none were either so savage as not to be charmed with praise, or so despicable as patiently to bear contempt, justly concluded, that flattery must be the most powerful argument that could be used to human creatures.

> It is visible, then, that it was not any heathen religion, or other idolatrous superstition, that first put man upon crossing his appetites and subduing his dearest inclinations, but the skilful management of wary politicians; and the nearer we search into human nature, the more we shall be convinced, that the moral virtues are the political offspring which flattery begot upon pride.

Society is founded neither on consent nor on agreed interest, but on rulers working on each person's pride. The engine is inside the person (pride), but it is worked from outside (politicians): this is neither of C8.4's alternatives cleanly, and it is from 1714.

### mandeville.05 · "Origin of Moral Virtue" — glory as "the aerial coin of praise"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (for), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** Part I, "Origin of Moral Virtue" ¶17; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 1092–1116
- **Checked:** text file

> The meanest wretch puts an inestimable value upon himself, and the highest wish of the ambitious man is to have all the world, as to that particular, of his opinion: so that the most insatiable thirst after fame that ever heroe was inspired with, was never more than an ungovernable greediness to engross the esteem and admiration of others in future ages as well as his own; [...] the great recompense in view, for which the most exalted minds have with so much alacrity sacrificed their quiet, health, sensual pleasures, and every inch of themselves, has never been any thing else but the breath of man, the aerial coin of praise. [...] To define then, the reward of glory in the amplest manner, the most that can be said of it, is, that it consists in a superlative felicity which a man, who is conscious of having performed a noble action, enjoys in self-love, whilst he is thinking on the applause he expects of others.

Self-love is the motive, but its currency is the opinion of others. Mandeville's self is selfish without being separate: its value is set by what others think of it.

### mandeville.06 · "Origin of Moral Virtue", last ¶ — "man, whom Providence had designed for society"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, "Origin of Moral Virtue" last ¶; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 1166–1175
- **Checked:** text file

> If the too scrupulous reader should at first view condemn these notions concerning the origin of moral virtue, and think them perhaps offensive to Christianity, I hope he will forbear his censures, when he shall consider, that nothing can render the unsearchable depth of the Divine Wisdom more conspicuous, than that man, whom Providence had designed for society, should not only by his own frailties and imperfections, be led into the road to temporal happiness, but likewise receive, from a seeming necessity of natural causes, a tincture of that knowledge, in which he was afterwards to be made perfect by the true religion, to his eternal welfare.

Possibly ironic, but on the page the selfish origin of society sits inside Providence, and man is "designed for society." Read at face value, it keeps both frames the rival story says were dropped. Compare the Introduction (lines 789–792): he speaks of "mere man, in the state of nature and ignorance of the true Deity."

### mandeville.07 · Remark on l. 101 — shame and "what others think of us"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (for), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** Part I, Remark on l. 101 (Kaye's Remark C); `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 1434–1438, 1460–1465
- **Checked:** text file

> The reverse of shame is pride, (see Remark on l. 182) yet no body can be touched with the first, that never felt any thing of the latter; for that we have such an extraordinary concern in what others think of us, can proceed from nothing but the vast esteem we have of ourselves.

> It is incredible how necessary an ingredient shame is to make us sociable; it is a frailty in our nature; all the world, whenever it affects them, submit to it with regret, and would prevent it if they could; yet the happiness of conversation depends upon it, and no society could be polished, if the generality of mankind were not subject to it.

Self-esteem and dependence on others' opinion are one passion with two faces. The more self, the more bound to others' eyes. That is the opposite of separation.

### mandeville.08 · Remark on l. 182 ¶1 — pride "so beneficial to society"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, Remark on l. 182 (Kaye's Remark M) ¶1; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 2939–2945
- **Checked:** text file

> Pride is that natural faculty by which every mortal that has any understanding over-values, and imagines better things of himself than any impartial judge, thoroughly acquainted with all his qualities and circumstances, could allow him. We are possessed of no other quality so beneficial to society, and so necessary to render it wealthy and flourishing as this, yet it is that which is most generally detested.

Self-overvaluation is the engine of a commercial society. It is defined against an "impartial judge," so the self is measured from outside even here (compare Smith's impartial spectator, C4.29).

### mandeville.09 · Remark on l. 182 — "not as what they are, but what they appear to be"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, Remark on l. 182 ¶7; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 3041–3053
- **Checked:** text file

> But whatever reflections may be made on this head, the world has long since decided the matter; handsome apparel is a main point, fine feathers make fine birds, and people, where they are not known, are generally honoured according to their clothes and other accoutrements they have about them; from the richness of them we judge of their wealth, and by their ordering of them we guess at their understanding. It is this which encourages every body, who is conscious of his little merit, if he is any ways able to wear clothes above his rank, especially in large and populous cities, where obscure men may hourly meet with fifty strangers to one acquaintance, and consequently have the pleasure of being esteemed by a vast majority, not as what they are, but what they appear to be: which is a greater temptation than most people want to be vain.

Clothes as a sign read by strangers in the anonymous city: the accessory is already here in 1714. But it signals rank and wealth, not an inner self. The vocabulary is station, not self-expression, so C8.2's "new idiom" survives, while its "old mechanism" is pushed back a further 145 years before Mill.

### mandeville.10 · Remark on l. 182 — "We all look above ourselves": emulation in dress

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates), C8.1 (against, as an original mechanism)
- **Location:** Part I, Remark on l. 182 ¶¶9–11; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 3075–3116
- **Checked:** text file

> Though every body allows, that as to apparel and manner of living, we ought to behave ourselves suitable to our conditions, and follow the examples of the most sensible, and prudent among our equals in rank and fortune: yet how few, that are not either miserably covetous, or else proud of singularity, have this discretion to boast of? We all look above ourselves, and, as fast as we can, strive to imitate those that some way or other are superior to us.

> The merchant's lady, who cannot bear the assurance of those mechanics, flies for refuge to the other end of the town, and scorns to follow any fashion but what she takes from thence; this haughtiness alarms the court, the women of quality are frightened to see merchants wives and daughters dressed like themselves: this impudence of the city, they cry, is intolerable; mantua-makers are sent for, and the contrivance of fashions becomes all their study, that they may have always new modes ready to take up, as soon as those saucy cits shall begin to imitate those in being. The same emulation is continued through the several degrees of quality, to an incredible expence [...]

> To this emulation and continual striving to out-do one another it is owing, that after so many various shiftings and changes of modes, in trumping up new ones, and renewing of old ones, there is still a plus ultra left for the ingenious; it is this, or at least the consequence of it, that sets the poor to work, adds spurs to industry, and encourages the skilful artificer to search after further improvements.

The direct pressure point on §8, and it should be faced honestly. Imitation from below, flight from above, and endless new modes: Simmel's mechanism (1904) is fully here, with its economic payoff. Three differences remain. (1) The engine is inside the person (pride), as C8.4 wants, but it is the wish to rise, not to belong. (2) The imitators do not call it their own choice or expression; they know they are copying "superior" people. (3) "Proud of singularity" makes standing apart from one's rank itself a form of pride. So C8.1's process is not new as a mechanism; only the self-expression idiom and the motive of belonging can still be claimed.

### mandeville.11 · "A Search into the Nature of Society" — "the multiplicity of his desires"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, "A Search into the Nature of Society"; `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 8875–8896, 9610–9618
- **Checked:** text file

> I intend now to investigate into the nature of society, and diving into the very rise of it, make it evident, that not the good and amiable, but the bad and hateful qualities of man, his imperfections and the want of excellencies, which other creatures are endued with, are the first causes that made man sociable beyond other animals, the moment after he lost Paradise; [...] I demonstrate that the sociableness of man arises only from these two things, viz. the multiplicity of his desires, and the continual opposition he meets with in his endeavours to gratify them.

> After this, I flatter myself to have demonstrated that, neither the friendly qualities and kind affections that are natural to man, nor the real virtues he is capable of acquiring by reason and self-denial, are the foundation of society; but that what we call evil in this world, moral as well as natural, is the grand principle that makes us sociable creatures, the solid basis, the life and support of all trades and employments without exception: that there we must look for the true origin of all arts and sciences, and that the moment evil ceases, the society must be spoiled, if not totally dissolved.

Society arises from individual want, which supports "prior to society." But it does not arise from consent, and its frame is the Fall ("the moment after he lost Paradise"). Need binds people together; it does not separate them.

### mandeville.12 · Part II, Fourth Dialogue — "Men become sociable, by living together in society"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against, as written), C4.29 (for)
- **Location:** Part II, Fourth Dialogue (Cleomenes); `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 16089–16095
- **Checked:** text file (dialogue identified by its position between the headings at lines 14825 and 16255; confirm against Kaye)

> Cleo. Such an equivalent is demonstrable in mutual commerce: for if we examine every faculty and qualification, from and for which we judge and pronounce man to be a sociable creature beyond other animals, we shall find, that a very considerable, if not the greatest part of the attribute is acquired, and comes upon multitudes, from their conversing with one another. Fabricando fabri simus. Men become sociable, by living together in society.

In the late Mandeville the individual is raw material (the grape before wine, lines 16071–16076), but what he becomes is made by "mutual commerce." Prior as matter, not prior as a self.

### mandeville.13 · Part II, Third Dialogue — "self-liking" needs "the approbation [...] of others"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (for), C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II, Third Dialogue (Cleomenes); `texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 14297–14305
- **Checked:** text file (dialogue identified by its position between the headings at lines 13370 and 14825; confirm against Kaye)

> I fancy, that to increase the care in creatures to preserve themselves, nature has given them an instinct, by which every individual values itself above its real worth; this in us, I mean in man, seems to be accompanied with a diffidence, arising from a consciousness, or at least an apprehension, that we do overvalue ourselves: it is that makes us so fond of the approbation, liking, and assent of others; because they strengthen and confirm us in the good opinion we have of ourselves.

The self cannot confirm itself alone. Mandeville puts inside the self the dependence on others' "assent" that §8 needs. This is a third engine beside C8.4's belonging and Rousseau's distinction: *confirmation*.
