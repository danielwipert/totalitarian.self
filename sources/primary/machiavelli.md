# Machiavelli, *The Prince* (1513) and *Discourses on Livy*

- **Edition used:** *The Prince*, trans. W. K. Marriott (Gutenberg #1232); *Discourses on the First Decade of Titus Livius*, trans. N. H. Thomson (Gutenberg #10827)
- **Text file:** `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt`, `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` (both clean)
- **Citation form:** *Prince* by chapter (e.g. *Prince* XXV); *Discourses* by book.chapter (e.g. *Disc.* II.2). Chapter numbers are standard in both translations.
- **Claims to test:** C4.21, C4.25, C4.30, C4.31; also C4.1, C1.1
- **Read:** *The Prince*, dedication and chapters I–XXVI in full (lines 495–3695; the two appended pieces on Valentino and Castruccio not read). *Discourses* selectively: I.9, I.10 (opening), I.11, I.12, II.2, II.29, III.1, III.9, III.41.

**Translation note.** Marriott has no single English word for *virtù*. In the passages below it appears as "ability" (VI, VII), "talent" and "genius" (VIII), "valour" (XIII, XXIV, XXV) and "virtue" (XXVI). A reader of the English cannot see that these are one word; any claim about "*virtù*" must be checked against the Italian before it is quoted. The words in quotation marks below are Marriott's and Thomson's only.

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

Machiavelli is the strongest witness yet for a **made** self in one sense, and against it in another.

- **For the rival view.** The new prince is valued for what he achieves by his own powers rather than by birth or fortune (prince.01, prince.10), and he is told to manage how he *appears*, above all to appear religious, because "men judge generally more by the eye than by the hand" (prince.05, prince.06, prince.07). This is a self-presentation crafted for others' eyes, with no answerability before God anywhere in it. It is a real strand that does not come from the Reformation, and it is older than Luther's 1520 treatises.
- **Against it.** What *virtù* shapes is a **state**, not a self: the founder's opportunity is "material to mould into the form which seemed best to them," and the material is a people (prince.01). The inner man is *not* remade. Machiavelli says twice, in both books, that a man "cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do" (prince.09, disc.07). The mask is put on over a fixed nature, and that nature is what fortune ruins. Self-making is bounded on every side: fortune rules "one-half of our actions" (prince.08); men may "interweave their threads with her web, but cannot break it" (disc.06); God leaves us only "that share of glory which belongs to us" (prince.10); glory itself is judged by others and denied to Agathocles (prince.03).
- **Religion (C4.30).** Machiavelli separates *politics* from Christian morality, and he is anticlerical, but he does the opposite of privatizing religion. Religion is the public bond of a state, "essential to the maintenance of civil society," to be upheld by rulers "even should they think it untrue" (disc.02, disc.03). The only private belief he points to is the ruler's possible *unbelief*, and that is kept hidden behind public worship.
- **Community (C4.31).** Strongly against. Security depends on "one's own forces," which are "subjects, citizens, or dependents" (prince.11). Greatness comes from "the well-being, not of individuals, but of the community" (disc.05). The founder acts alone, but for "his State and country," and the institutions last only when "entrusted to the care of many" (disc.01). When the country is at stake, "no consideration of what is just or unjust [...] must intervene" (disc.08). Nothing in these texts makes the individual prior to the community.
- **§8.** The performance in *Prince* XVIII is one person deceiving the many in the vocabulary of virtue and religion. That is closer to Bernays (the engineered appearance) than to §8's self-expressed conformity. It shows that a public self crafted for the eye is old (1513), but its direction is top-down, and its idiom is virtue, not self-expression.

**Verdict in one line:** Machiavelli makes the *prince's public face* a craft, but neither the self's nature nor its ends; the self he shows is bounded by fortune, nature, God's share and the *patria*, and is answerable to the eye of the many rather than to God.

## Passages: *The Prince*

### machiavelli-prince.01 · *Prince* VI — "material to mould into the form which seemed best to them"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.25 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VI; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 1065–1078
- **Checked:** text file

> But to come to those who, by their own ability and not through fortune, have risen to be princes, I say that Moses, Cyrus, Romulus, Theseus, and such like are the most excellent examples. [...] And in examining their actions and lives one cannot see that they owed anything to fortune beyond opportunity, which brought them the material to mould into the form which seemed best to them. Without that opportunity their powers of mind would have been extinguished, and without those powers the opportunity would have come in vain.

The great men are valued for their own powers ("ability", probably *virtù*), which supports C4.21's "valued for his own excellence." But what they mould is a people and a state, not themselves, and even they need fortune's "opportunity." The founder makes a world, not a self.

### machiavelli-prince.02 · *Prince* VI, XIV — imitation of great men

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.25 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VI, lines 1043–1048; ch. XIV, lines 2245–2252; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> men, walking almost always in paths beaten by others, and following by imitation their deeds, are yet unable to keep entirely to the ways of others or attain to the power of those they imitate. A wise man ought always to follow the paths beaten by great men, and to imitate those who have been supreme, so that if his ability does not equal theirs, at least it will savour of it.

> above all do as an illustrious man did, who took as an exemplar one who had been praised and famous before him, and whose achievements and deeds he always kept in his mind, as it is said Alexander the Great imitated Achilles, Caesar Alexander, Scipio Cyrus.

The self is formed by a craft, but the craft is *imitation of exemplars*, not originality. Excellence means copying the right models well. This is fashioning from the outside in, the opposite of a self that is its own origin.

### machiavelli-prince.03 · *Prince* VIII — Agathocles: "empire, but not glory"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.25 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 1455–1464
- **Checked:** text file

> Yet it cannot be called talent to slay fellow-citizens, to deceive friends, to be without faith, without mercy, without religion; such methods may gain empire, but not glory. [...] Nevertheless, his barbarous cruelty and inhumanity with infinite wickedness do not permit him to be celebrated among the most excellent men.

Even in *The Prince*, what one achieves alone has a limit: glory is a standard held by others, and it is denied to the man who is "without faith, without mercy, without religion." ("Talent" here probably renders *virtù*; verify against the Italian.) Self-made excellence still answers to a shared judgment of what may be "celebrated."

### machiavelli-prince.04 · *Prince* XV — "how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C4.25 (for)
- **Location:** ch. XV; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 2274–2286
- **Checked:** text file

> it appears to me more appropriate to follow up the real truth of the matter than the imagination of it; for many have pictured republics and principalities which in fact have never been known or seen, because how one lives is so far distant from how one ought to live, that he who neglects what is done for what ought to be done, sooner effects his ruin than his preservation; [...] Hence it is necessary for a prince wishing to hold his own to know how to do wrong, and to make use of it or not according to necessity.

This is the real separation in Machiavelli: political conduct is cut off from the moral teaching of the Church. But what is separated from religion is the *art of rule*, not the self, and the master is "necessity," not the self's own will.

### machiavelli-prince.05 · *Prince* XVIII — "very necessary to appear to have them"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XVIII; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 2575–2590
- **Checked:** text file

> Therefore it is unnecessary for a prince to have all the good qualities I have enumerated, but it is very necessary to appear to have them. And I shall dare to say this also, that to have them and always to observe them is injurious, and that to appear to have them is useful; to appear merciful, faithful, humane, religious, upright, and to be so, but with a mind so framed that should you require not to be so, you may be able and know how to change to the opposite. [...] Therefore it is necessary for him to have a mind ready to turn itself accordingly as the winds and variations of fortune force it, yet, as I have said above, not to diverge from the good if he can avoid doing so, but, if compelled, then to know how to set about it.

The clearest "for" for C4.25: virtue is a performance for others, and appearance is detached from conduct. But it is a ruler's mask over the state's necessities, and what the mind turns with is fortune, not the self's choosing. For §8 it shows that a crafted public face is old. Its vocabulary is virtue and religion, not self-expression, and its author is the one deceiving the many.

### machiavelli-prince.06 · *Prince* XVIII — "men judge generally more by the eye than by the hand"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C4.30 (complicates), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XVIII; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 2611–2626
- **Checked:** text file

> There is nothing more necessary to appear to have than this last quality, inasmuch as men judge generally more by the eye than by the hand, because it belongs to everybody to see you, to few to come in touch with you. Every one sees what you appear to be, few really know what you are, and those few dare not oppose themselves to the opinion of the many, who have the majesty of the state to defend them; [...] because the vulgar are always taken by what a thing seems to be and by what comes of it; and in the world there are only the vulgar, for the few find a place there only when the many have no ground to rest on.

The "last quality" is being religious. Religion is the appearance it is *most* necessary to keep up, which makes it public, the opposite of C4.30's private conscience. The self here exists in the eye of the many and is held in place by "the opinion of the many"; there is no inner court, and no individual prior to that public.

### machiavelli-prince.07 · *Prince* XXI — "using religion as a plea"; "a great and remarkable man"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C4.30 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXI; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 3140–3143, 3156–3157
- **Checked:** text file

> Further, always using religion as a plea, so as to undertake greater schemes, he devoted himself with pious cruelty to driving out and clearing his kingdom of the Moors; nor could there be a more admirable example, nor one more rare.

> And a prince ought, above all things, always endeavour in every action to gain for himself the reputation of being a great and remarkable man.

Ferdinand's religion is a "plea" (a cloak), and Machiavelli admires it. "Great and remarkable" is close to C4.21's individual valued for his excellence, but the value is a *reputation* and it serves rule. It is excellence as others see it.

### machiavelli-prince.08 · *Prince* XXV — "Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXV; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 3443–3454
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not unknown to me how many men have had, and still have, the opinion that the affairs of the world are in such wise governed by fortune and by God that men with their wisdom cannot direct them and that no one can even help them [...] Nevertheless, not to extinguish our free will, I hold it to be true that Fortune is the arbiter of one-half of our actions, but that she still leaves us to direct the other half, or perhaps a little less.

The famous claim for human agency is a claim for *half*, "or perhaps a little less," made "not to extinguish our free will." It is a real bid for self-direction, outside any Reformation frame of answerability (which complicates C4.1). But it explicitly sets a limit.

### machiavelli-prince.09 · *Prince* XXV — "he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (against), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXV; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 3507–3514, 3542–3548
- **Checked:** text file

> But a man is not often found sufficiently circumspect to know how to accommodate himself to the change, both because he cannot deviate from what nature inclines him to do, and also because, having always prospered by acting in one way, he cannot be persuaded that it is well to leave it; [...] I conclude, therefore that, fortune being changeful and mankind steadfast in their ways, so long as the two are in agreement men are successful, but unsuccessful when they fall out. For my part I consider that it is better to be adventurous than cautious, because fortune is a woman, and if you wish to keep her under it is necessary to beat and ill-use her

The key "against" for C4.25: Machiavelli thinks the *self* cannot be refashioned. Its nature is fixed, and that is exactly why fortune defeats it. The mask (.05) can change; the man under it cannot. The advice to be "adventurous" is a choice of temperament for the odds, not self-creation.

### machiavelli-prince.10 · *Prince* XXVI — "that share of glory which belongs to us"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.30 (against), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XXVI; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt` lines 3606–3612
- **Checked:** text file

> Further than this, how extraordinarily the ways of God have been manifested beyond example: the sea is divided, a cloud has led the way, the rock has poured forth water, it has rained manna, everything has contributed to your greatness; you ought to do the rest. God is not willing to do everything, and thus take away our free will and that share of glory which belongs to us.

Human glory is a *share* that God leaves to us, inside providence, and the deed it is for is the liberation of Italy. Rhetorical, perhaps, but in the text the self's achievement is framed by God and aimed at the country.

### machiavelli-prince.11 · *Prince* XIII, XXIV, XX — "depend on yourself": one's own arms are citizens

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIII, lines 2164–2171; ch. XXIV, lines 3432–3434; ch. XX, lines 3092–3093; `texts/machiavelli-prince.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> I conclude, therefore, that no principality is secure without having its own forces [...] And one’s own forces are those which are composed either of subjects, citizens, or dependents; all others are mercenaries or auxiliaries.

> that deliverance is of no avail which does not depend upon yourself; those only are reliable, certain, and durable that depend on yourself and your valour.

> For this reason the best possible fortress is—not to be hated by the people

"Depend on yourself" sounds like self-reliance, but the "self" here is the prince *with his people*: his own forces are his citizens, and his best fortress is their good will. Self-sufficiency is a community's, not an individual's.

## Passages: *Discourses on Livy*

### machiavelli-discourses.01 · *Disc.* I.9 — the founder "must stand alone", for his country

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.9; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 1629–1653
- **Checked:** text file

> we must take it as a rule to which there are very few if any exceptions, that no commonwealth or kingdom ever has salutary institutions given it from the first or has its institutions recast in an entirely new mould, unless by a single person. [...] For this reason the wise founder of a commonwealth who seeks to benefit not himself only, or the line of his descendants, but his State and country, must endeavour to acquire an absolute and undivided authority. [...] Moreover, though it be one man that must give a State its institutions, once given they are not so likely to last long resting for support on the shoulders of one man only, as when entrusted to the care of many, and when it is the business of many to maintain them.

This is the most individualist moment in Machiavelli, one man founding the order, and it is bound at both ends. The founder's end is "his State and country," and his work lasts only in "the care of many." The individual comes before the community only as its maker, and only for its sake.

### machiavelli-discourses.02 · *Disc.* I.11 — religion "essential to the maintenance of civil society"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against), C4.25 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.11; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 1836–1840, 1876–1882
- **Checked:** text file

> He finding the people fierce and turbulent, and desiring with the help of the peaceful arts to bring them to order and obedience, called in the aid of religion as essential to the maintenance of civil society

> this was very necessary to Numa, who feigned to have intercourse with a Nymph by whose advice he was guided in counselling the people. And this, because desiring to introduce in Rome new and untried institutions, he feared that his own authority might not effect his end. Nor, indeed, has any attempt ever been made to introduce unusual laws among a people, without resorting to Divine authority, since without such sanction they never would have been accepted.

Religion is public and political, the bond of a city, not a private matter of conscience. Against C4.30 as worded. The founder's own authority is not enough; he has to borrow God's, even by feigning. So no self here founds on itself.

### machiavelli-discourses.03 · *Disc.* I.12 — "even should they think it untrue"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.12; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 1975–1981, 2017–2019
- **Checked:** text file

> For which reason, the rulers of kingdoms and commonwealths should maintain the foundations of the faith which they hold; since thus it will be easy for them to keep their country religious, and, consequently, virtuous and united. To which end they should countenance and further whatsoever tells in favour of religion, even should they think it untrue; and the wiser they are, and the better they are acquainted with natural causes, the more ought they to do so.

> To the Church, therefore, and to the priests, we Italians owe this first debt, that through them we have become wicked and irreligious.

This is the one place where a *private* stance toward religion appears: the wise ruler may privately think it "untrue." But the private disbelief must be hidden behind public support. It is the inverse of C4.30, because the public requires religion and only the few are allowed a private exit. Anticlerical, not anti-religious.

### machiavelli-discourses.04 · *Disc.* II.2 — "our religion glorifies men of a humble and contemplative [...] life"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.30 (complicates), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** II.2; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 5930–5934, 5946–5967
- **Checked:** text file

> For our religion, having revealed to us the truth and the true path, teaches us to make little account of worldly glory; whereas, the Gentiles, greatly esteeming it, and placing therein their highest good, displayed a greater fierceness in their actions.

> Moreover, under the old religions none obtained divine honours save those who were loaded with worldly glory, such as captains of armies and rulers of cities; whereas our religion glorifies men of a humble and contemplative, rather than of an active life. [...] But should it seem that the world has grown effeminate and Heaven laid aside her arms, this assuredly results from the baseness of those who have interpreted our religion to accord with indolence and ease rather than with valour. For were we to remember that religion permits the exaltation and defence of our country, we would see it to be our duty to love and honour it, and would strive to be able and ready to defend it.

This is the best support for the "Renaissance" half of C4.21: a deliberate revaluation of worldly glory and the active life against Christian humility. But the glory is civic ("captains of armies and rulers of cities"), and the fix is to reinterpret religion so that it serves "our country," not to leave religion behind. The revalued excellence belongs to the citizen-soldier.

### machiavelli-discourses.05 · *Disc.* II.2 — "the well-being, not of individuals, but of the community"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** II.2; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 5862–5869
- **Checked:** text file

> The cause, however, is not far to seek, since it is the well-being, not of individuals, but of the community which makes a State great; and, without question, this universal well-being is nowhere secured save in a republic. For a republic will do whatsoever makes for its interest; and though its measures prove hurtful to this man or to that, there are so many whom they benefit, that these are able to carry them out, in spite of the resistance of the few whom they injure.

This says directly that the community's good, not the individual's, is what counts. It is the opposite of C4.31's "individuals who are prior to it."

### machiavelli-discourses.06 · *Disc.* II.29 — "may interweave their threads with her web, but cannot break it"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** II.29; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 8930–8935, 8959–8962
- **Checked:** text file

> And hence it is that those who experience the extremes whether of good or of evil fortune, are, commonly, little deserving either of praise or blame; since it is apparent that it is from Heaven having afforded them, or denied them opportunities for acting worthily, that they have been brought to their greatness or to their undoing.

> But once more I repeat what, from all history, may be seen to be most true, that men may aid Fortune, but not withstand her; may interweave their threads with her web, but cannot break it

This is more fatalist than *Prince* XXV. Great success and great ruin are Heaven's work, so they are "little deserving either of praise or blame." It supports C4.1's pattern that early forms come bounded. It also bears on §7: in Machiavelli, praise and blame shrink as fortune's share grows. He does not make the self the cause.

### machiavelli-discourses.07 · *Disc.* III.9 — "we cannot act in opposition to the bent of our nature"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (against), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** III.9; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 10730–10733, 10748–10751
- **Checked:** text file

> And hence it comes that a commonwealth endures longer, and has a more sustained good fortune than a princedom, because from the diversity in the characters of its citizens, it can adapt itself better than a prince can to the diversity of times.

> As to why such changes are impossible, two reasons may be given. One is that we cannot act in opposition to the bent of our nature. The other, that when a man has been very successful while following a particular method, he can never be convinced that it is for his advantage to try some other.

This repeats .09: the individual cannot remake his nature. What adapts is the *republic*, through the variety of its citizens (Fabius for one time, Scipio for another). Difference among persons is valued, but as a resource of the community. That is relational difference in SPEC §2's sense, not a self-made self.

### machiavelli-discourses.08 · *Disc.* III.41 — "no consideration of what is just or unjust [...] must intervene"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.41; `texts/machiavelli-discourses.txt` lines 13349–13355
- **Checked:** text file

> Now this incident deserves to be noted and pondered over by every citizen who is called on to advise his country; for when the entire safety of our country is at stake, no consideration of what is just or unjust, merciful or cruel, praiseworthy or shameful, must intervene. On the contrary, every other consideration being set aside, that course alone must be taken which preserves the existence of the country and maintains its liberty.

The highest value overrides even the individual's conscience, and it is the country, not God and not the self. Machiavelli's separation from Christian morality yields the *patria* as the absolute, which is the reverse of the separated individual.
