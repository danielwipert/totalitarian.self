## Passages

### locke-toleration.01 · p. 11 — "the just bounds" between civil government and religion

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** p. 11; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 1181–1186
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized

> I esteem it above all things necessary to distinguish exactly the business of civil government from that of religion, and to settle the just bounds that lie between the one and the other.

The programme of the *Letter* in one sentence. Note what is separated: two *businesses* (jurisdictions), not the person from religion.

### locke-toleration.02 · pp. 13–15 — the care of souls is not the magistrate's

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C4.3 (for)
- **Location:** pp. 13–15; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 1276–1312, 1352–1358
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; OCR corrected ("3 18 not" → "is not", "conmitied" → "committed", "Guts" → "souls")

> First, Because the care of souls is not committed to the civil magistrate, any more than to other men. It is not committed unto him, I say, by God; because it appears not that God has ever given any such authority to one man over another, as to compel any one to his religion. Nor can any such power be vested in the magistrate by the consent of the people; because no man can so far abandon the care of his own salvation, as blindly to leave it to the choice of any other, whether prince or subject, to prescribe to him what faith or worship he shall embrace. For no man can, if he would, conform his faith to the dictates of another. All the life and power of true religion consists in the outward [sic] and full persuasion of the mind; and faith is not faith without believing. [...] In the second place, The care of souls cannot belong to the civil magistrate, because his power consists only in outward force; but true and saving religion consists in the inward persuasion of the mind, without which nothing can be acceptable to God.

Belief is made private because it is inward and cannot be compelled. "Outward" in the first sentence is as the file reads; the next paragraph's "inward persuasion" suggests an OCR or printing error. Check against a scan before quoting. The test of true religion is still what is "acceptable to God."

### locke-toleration.03 · pp. 18–19 — "one truth, one way to heaven"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 18–19, "In the third place"; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 1531–1544
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized

> For, there being but one truth, one way to heaven, what hopes is there that more men would be led into it, if they had no other rule to follow but the religion of the court, and were put under a necessity to quit the light of their own reason, to oppose the dictates of their own consciences, and blindly to resign up themselves to the will of their governors, and to the religion, which either ignorance, ambition, or superstition had chanced to establish in the countries where they were born?

Conscience and reason are each person's "own," but they are means to a single shared end, "one truth, one way to heaven." Locke privatizes the *search*, not the truth.

### locke-toleration.04 · pp. 19–21 — "a voluntary society"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** pp. 19–21; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 1632–1666
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "effeftual" → "effectual"

> A church then I take to be a voluntary society of men, joining themselves together of their own accord, in order to the public worshipping of God, in such a manner as they judge acceptable to him, and effectual to the salvation of their souls. [...] it is a free and voluntary society. No body is born a member of any church; otherwise the religion of parents would descend unto children, by the same right of inheritance as their temporal estates, and every one would hold his faith by the same tenure he does his lands; than which nothing can be imagined more absurd.

Direct support for the second half of C4.30, in Locke's own words. Inherited membership is the thing denied: religion is cut from birth and family, not from God.

### locke-toleration.05 · pp. 22, 28–29 — the church makes laws and may expel

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 22 and pp. 28–29; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 1764–1768, 2052–2068
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "fjirft, 1" → "first, I"

> it necessarily follows, that the right of making its laws can belong to none but the society itself [...] no church is bound by the duty of Toleration to retain any such person in her bosom, as after admonition continues obstinately to offend against the laws of the society. For these being the condition of communion, and the bond of the society, if the breach of them were permitted without any animadversion, the society would immediately be thereby dissolved.

The voluntary church is still a community with laws, admonition and excommunication. Joining is free; belonging is bounded.

### locke-toleration.06 · p. 33 (approx.) — "the Supreme Judge of all men"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** p. 33 (approx.); `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 2322–2336
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "Confan-tinople" → "Constantinople", "alfo" → "also", "ef: the:" → "of the"

> nor is there any judge, either at Constantinople, or elsewhere upon earth, by whose sentence it can be determined. The decision of that question belongs only to the Supreme Judge of all men, to whom also alone belongs the punishment of the erroneous.

No earthly judge of religious truth, because there is a heavenly one. Toleration rests on answerability to God, not on its absence.

### locke-toleration.07 · p. 45 — "The care therefore of every man's soul belongs unto himself"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** p. 45; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 2783–2805
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "man' s foul" → "man's soul"

> But a charitable care, which consists in teaching, admonishing, and persuading, cannot be denied unto any man. The care therefore of every man's soul belongs unto himself, and is to be left unto himself.

The brief's "care of souls belonging to each person," verified. In the same sentence others keep "a charitable care" of teaching and admonishing: the person is responsible, not solitary.

### locke-toleration.08 · pp. 88–90 — "the supreme and absolute authority of judging for himself"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for and complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** pp. 88–90; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 4886–4949
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "Firf" → "First", "dave" → "have"

> Every man has an immortal soul, capable of eternal happiness or misery, whose happiness depending upon his believing and doing those things in this life, which are necessary to the obtaining of God's favour, and are prescribed by God to that end; it follows from thence; First, That the observance of these things is the highest obligation that lies upon mankind [...] therefore the care of each man's salvation belongs only to himself. [...] Every man, in that, has the supreme and absolute authority of judging for himself. And the reason is, because nobody else is concerned in it, nor can receive any prejudice from his conduct therein.

The strongest wording of private religion in the *Letter* ("supreme and absolute authority of judging for himself"), and it is derived *from* God's commands, the "highest obligation." The authority is over *who judges* among men, not over *what is true*; God prescribes.

### locke-toleration.09 · pp. 119–120 — "accountable to God only"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** pp. 119–120; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 6306–6324
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized

> the occasion for which they are thus treated, does not at all belong to the jurisdiction of the magistrate, but intirely to the conscience of every particular man; for the conduct of which he is accountable to God only

Conscience is taken from the magistrate and given to the person, and the person is "accountable to God only." This is the institutional separation with the answerable Self intact: exactly the C4.30 pressure point.

### locke-toleration.10 · pp. 102–103 — no toleration for a church bound to a foreign prince

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 102–103; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 5564–5615
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "effe it" left as in file (word lost in OCR), "Mahumetan" kept, "Conflantinople" → "Constantinople"

> That church can have no right to be tolerated by the magistrate, which is constituted upon such a bottom, that all those who enter into it, do thereby ipso facto, deliver themselves up to the protection and service of another prince. [...] It is ridiculous for any one to profess himself to be a Mahumetan only in his religion, but in every thing else a faithful subject to a Christian magistrate, whilst at the same time he acknowledges himself bound to yield blind obedience to the Mufti of Constantinople [...]

The usual reading is that this aims at Catholics (allegiance to the Pope) through the Muslim example; Locke does not name them here. The limit is political loyalty: religion is private only so long as it does not carry a rival public allegiance.

### locke-toleration.11 · pp. 103–104 — "not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against, for the Self; for, for the institution)
- **Location:** pp. 103–104; `texts/locke-letter-toleration-1800.txt` lines 5629–5660
- **Checked:** text file; ſ modernized; "ih. facto" in .10 and "pre-rence" here corrected to "ipso facto" and "pretence"

> Lastly, Those are not at all to be tolerated who deny the being of a God. Promises, covenants, and oaths, which are the bonds of human society, can have no hold upon an atheist. The taking away of God, though but even in thought, dissolves all. Besides also, those that by their atheism undermine and destroy all religion, can have no pretence of religion whereupon to challenge the privilege of a toleration.

The decisive limit. A Self separated from God is not tolerated, because God is what makes the self's word binding on others. Religion becomes private; belief in God stays a public condition of membership in society.

## Summary for C4.30

Locke supports the claim's institutional half almost word for word: belief is a matter of inward persuasion (locke-toleration.02), the church "a voluntary society" (.04), the care of each soul "belongs unto himself" (.07, .08). But the Self that gains this authority is "accountable to God only" (.09), judged by "the Supreme Judge of all men" (.06), and an atheist is not tolerated at all (.11). The church separates from the state; the Self does not separate from God.
