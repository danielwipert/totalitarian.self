# Samuel Smiles, *Self-Help* (1859)

- **Edition used:** original English (revised edition), "Popular Edition", John Murray, London, 1897 (text of the 1866 revision; preface dated May 1866, introduction September 1859)
- **Text file:** `texts/smiles-self-help.txt` (clean; Project Gutenberg #935)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): Oxford World's Classics, ed. Sinnema (2002)
- **Citation form:** chapter, plus this file's line
- **Claims to test:** C5.14, C5.9, C6.*, C7.1–C7.4

**Caution on dating:** this is the 1866 revision. Passages may be later than 1859 (the preface lists new ones; Andrew Johnson as "the present President" in ch. I is post-1865). Check any passage that matters for dating against the 1859 first edition.

## Summary of the reading (2026-10-23)

Smiles keeps the frame. The self-helping Self answers to duty, conscience, character ("moral order embodied in the individual"), God and Providence, the nation, and "a system of mutual dependencies"; it is the "fruit" of "all the foregoing centuries", not prior to them (.05, .06, .12, .14, .15). What it does *not* do is answer to institutions: government, law and outside help are pushed aside as causes of anyone's condition (.03). So the shift Smiles shows is not self-origination but a moving of *causes*: the individual becomes the near-sole cause of his own fortune while the moral frame stays in place, and in fact guarantees the result ("individuals, nations, and races, will obtain just so much as they deserve", .15).

The strongest evidence for C5.14 is the cult of will in ch. VIII ("it is the Man himself"; "a savour of omnipotence", .07, .09) and the "habit of happy thought" (.18), which point toward mind-cure. Both come bounded: will "joined to the Divine", "everything depends upon right direction".

The strongest evidence for C7.4 is chapters IX–X (.10, .11): failure is laid at the individual's door, with God and Providence still in charge. That cuts against the essay's *explanation* of C7.4 (frames deflected, §7.1): personalized failure comes *before* frame loss, and the frame itself drives it.

## Passages

### smiles-selfhelp.01 · Preface (1866) — "the very opposite" of "a eulogy of selfishness"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Preface, May 1866; `texts/smiles-self-help.txt` lines 95–107, 113–119
- **Checked:** text file

> In one respect the title of the book, which it is now too late to alter, has proved unfortunate, as it has led some, who have judged it merely by the title, to suppose that it consists of a eulogy of selfishness: the very opposite of what it really is,—or at least of what the author intended it to be. Although its chief object unquestionably is to stimulate youths to apply themselves diligently to right pursuits,—sparing neither labour, pains, nor self-denial in prosecuting them,—and to rely upon their own efforts in life, rather than depend upon the help or patronage of others, it will also be found [...] that the duty of helping one's self in the highest sense involves the helping of one's neighbours. [...] It is, however, shown in the following pages that Failure is the best discipline of the true worker, by stimulating him to renewed efforts, evoking his best powers, and carrying him onward in self-culture, self-control, and growth in knowledge and wisdom.

Smiles's own statement of intent: self-help is a *duty*, aimed at "right pursuits", and it includes the neighbour. Self-reliance is set against "patronage", not against relation. Failure is a discipline, not yet a verdict on the person (contrast .10).

### smiles-selfhelp.02 · Introduction (1859) — "individual duty, which is the glory of manly character"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Introduction to the First Edition, Sept. 1859; lines 228–234
- **Checked:** text file

> citing examples of what other men had done, as illustrations of what each might, in a greater or less degree, do for himself; and pointing out that their happiness and well-being as individuals in after life, must necessarily depend mainly upon themselves—upon their own diligent self-culture, self-discipline, and self-control—and, above all, on that honest and upright performance of individual duty, which is the glory of manly character.

The 1859 core message: well-being depends "mainly" (not wholly) on oneself, and the summit is *duty*. Answerability is kept, made individual. The book grew out of a mutual-improvement class where "those who knew a little taught those who knew less" (lines 209–210): a communal origin for "self-help".

### smiles-selfhelp.03 · Ch. I — "Help from without is often enfeebling"; social evils as "the outgrowth of man's own perverted life"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I, "Self-Help—National and Individual"; lines 612–621, 638–642, 658–668
- **Checked:** text file

> "HEAVEN helps those who help themselves" is a well-tried maxim [...]. Help from without is often enfeebling in its effects, but help from within invariably invigorates. [...] no laws, however stringent, can make the idle industrious, the thriftless provident, or the drunken sober. Such reforms can only be effected by means of individual action, economy, and self-denial; by better habits, rather than by greater rights. [...] National progress is the sum of individual industry, energy, and uprightness, as national decay is of individual idleness, selfishness, and vice. What we are accustomed to decry as great social evils, will, for the most part, be found to be but the outgrowth of man's own perverted life

The clearest early statement of C7.4's pattern: social evils are traced back to individual conduct, and institutions are demoted. The hedge "for the most part" matters ("never" is not Smiles's word). For C5.14: the individual is prior to *institutions* (the nation is "an aggregate", lines 653–654), but the opening maxim still puts Heaven above self-help.

### smiles-selfhelp.04 · Ch. I — Mill quoted, with Mill's God-clause cut

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates: influence route), C4.20 (complicates, as reception)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 680–685 (epigraph from Mill at lines 604–605). Compare Mill, *On Liberty* III, `texts/mill-on-liberty.txt` lines 2771–2774
- **Checked:** text file (both)

> The solid foundations of liberty must rest upon individual character; which is also the only sure guarantee for social security and national progress. John Stuart Mill truly observes that "even despotism does not produce its worst effects so long as individuality exists under it; and whatever crushes individuality _is_ despotism, by whatever name it be called."

Mill's sentence goes on: "and whether it professes to be enforcing the will of God or the injunctions of men" (mill-on-liberty.txt line 2774). Smiles stops before it. This is a documented route from philosophy to the popular book (Mill is the opening epigraph and is quoted again in ch. IX, line 8469), and the route *narrows* Mill: "individuality" becomes "individual character", and the clause naming God as a possible despot goes. Emerson is never named in the book; Carlyle appears only in an anecdote about his burnt manuscript (lines 3569–3576).

### smiles-selfhelp.05 · Ch. I — "manly dependence and manly independence"; "their own best helpers"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.4 (complicates), C5.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I (closing); lines 1303–1311, 1356–1364
- **Checked:** text file

> it must at the same time be acknowledged that the help which we derive from others in the journey of life is of very great importance. The poet Wordsworth has well said that "these two things, contradictory though they may seem, must go together—manly dependence and manly independence, manly reliance and manly self-reliance." From infancy to old age, all are more or less indebted to others for nurture and culture [...]. In fine, human character is moulded by a thousand subtle influences; by example and precept; by life and literature; by friends and neighbours; by the world we live in as well as by the spirits of our forefathers, whose legacy of good words and deeds we inherit. But great, unquestionably, though these influences are acknowledged to be, it is nevertheless equally clear that men must necessarily be the active agents of their own well-being and well-doing; and that, however much the wise and the good may owe to others, they themselves must in the very nature of things be their own best helpers.

Chapter I ends by naming dependence on others, the world and forefathers, so the Self comes *after* its world, not before it. "Best helpers" is a claim about agency, not origin. The structure, though, is "acknowledged [...] But", and the "But" carries the weight: that is the pattern C7.4 will need.

### smiles-selfhelp.06 · Ch. II — "Honourable industry travels the same road with duty"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against)
- **Location:** ch. II, "Leaders of Industry"; lines 1398–1407
- **Checked:** text file

> Honourable industry travels the same road with duty; and Providence has closely linked both with happiness. [...] Labour is not only a necessity and a duty, but a blessing: only the idler feels it to be a curse. The duty of work is written on the thews and muscles of the limbs, the mechanism of the hand, the nerves and lobes of the brain

Work is a duty set by Providence and written into the body: a frame outside the Self's choosing. Close to the Puritan calling Weber describes (C4.4), but without election.

### smiles-selfhelp.07 · Ch. VIII — energy of will "is the Man himself"; will "joined to the Divine"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VIII, "Energy and Courage"; lines 7207–7210, 7278–7283; cf. 7179–7184
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not eminent talent that is required to ensure success in any pursuit, so much as purpose,—not merely the power to achieve, but the will to labour energetically and perseveringly. Hence energy of will may be defined to be the very central power of character in a man—in a word, it is the Man himself. [...] It is will,—force of purpose,—that enables a man to do or be whatever he sets his mind on being or doing. A holy man was accustomed to say, "Whatever you wish, that you are: for such is the force of our will, joined to the Divine, that whatever we wish to be, seriously, and with a true intention, that we become. No one ardently wishes to be submissive, patient, modest, or liberal, who does not become what he wishes."

The book's closest approach to the self-originating Self: the man *is* his will, and will makes him "whatever he sets his mind on being". But the source quoted joins the will "to the Divine", and its examples of becoming are virtues (submissive, patient, modest). Self-*making* inside a moral and religious frame, not self-origination. The chapter opens with a Norseman's "I put my sole trust in my own strength of body and soul" (line 7181), framed as racial ("Teuton") inheritance.

### smiles-selfhelp.08 · Ch. VIII — free will: "the only thing that is wholly ours"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C7.4 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 7291–7311
- **Checked:** text file

> Whatever theoretical conclusions logicians may have formed as to the freedom of the will, each individual feels that practically he is free to choose between good and evil—that he is not as a mere straw thrown upon the water to mark the direction of the current, but that he has within him the power of a strong swimmer [...]. Without this where would be responsibility?—and what the advantage of teaching, advising, preaching, reproof, and correction? [...] In every moment of our life, conscience is proclaiming that our will is free. It is the only thing that is wholly ours, and it rests solely with ourselves individually, whether we give it the right or the wrong direction. Our habits or our temptations are not our masters, but we of them.

Free will is asserted for the sake of responsibility, the forensic link of C4.10–C4.12 (Nietzsche's charge that free will was made up to make guilt attributable fits this passage closely). For C7.1: the accountability is there, but so is conscience, and "the right or the wrong direction" is still measured against a standard. Blame is not yet cut loose from God. Note the hedge: "directing *to a great extent*".

### smiles-selfhelp.09 · Ch. VIII — "a savour of omnipotence", checked by "right direction" and Napoleon

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C5.9 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 7337–7350, 7372–7377
- **Checked:** text file

> As will, considered without regard to direction, is simply constancy, firmness, perseverance, it will be obvious that everything depends upon right direction and motives. Directed towards the enjoyment of the senses, the strong will may be a demon [...]; but directed towards good, the strong will is a king [...]. "Where there is a will there is a way," is an old and true saying. He who resolves upon doing a thing, by that very resolution often scales the barriers to it, and secures its achievement. To think we are able, is almost to be so—to determine upon attainment is frequently attainment itself. Thus, earnest resolution has often seemed to have about it almost a savour of omnipotence. [...] for Napoleon's intense selfishness was his ruin, and the ruin of France, which he left a prey to anarchy. His life taught the lesson that power, however energetically wielded, without beneficence, is fatal to its possessor and its subjects

"To think we are able, is almost to be so" is the seed of mind-cure and positive thinking (compare james-trine, coue-bernays). It is a popular text, not a philosophical one, which fits C5.9's "two stages". But every claim is hedged ("often", "almost", "frequently", "seemed"), and will cut off from the good is condemned in the very same pages. Smiles plants the seed but keeps the fence. A precursor, not the event.

### smiles-selfhelp.10 · Ch. IX — "it was generally by his own fault that he failed of success"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C6.* (see note)
- **Location:** ch. IX, "Men of Business"; lines 8555–8580; cf. 8582–8587 (Washington Irving)
- **Checked:** text file

> Those who fail in life are however very apt to assume a tone of injured innocence, and conclude too hastily that everybody excepting themselves has had a hand in their personal misfortunes. An eminent writer [...] came to the conclusion that the real cause of his ill-success in life was the money-worshipping spirit of the age. [...] Again, some consider themselves born to ill luck, and make up their minds that the world invariably goes against them without any fault on their own part. [...] There is however a Russian proverb which says that Misfortune is next door to Stupidity; and it will often be found that men who are constantly lamenting their luck, are in some way or other reaping the consequences of their own neglect, mismanagement, improvidence, or want of application. Dr. Johnson [...] has honestly said, "All the complaints which are made of the world are unjust; I never knew a man of merit neglected; it was generally by his own fault that he failed of success."

The direct ancestor of C7.4: a social explanation ("the money-worshipping spirit of the age") is mocked, and failure goes back to the person. Still hedged ("often", "generally"). The next paragraph adds an American voice, Washington Irving: talk of "modest merit being neglected" is "too often a cant" (lines 8582–8584). This is the only America-specific support in the book, and it is a single quotation.

### smiles-selfhelp.11 · Ch. X — the labouring class "the ordinance of God"; its misery "solely" man's own fault

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C5.14 (against)
- **Location:** ch. X, "Money—Its Use and Abuse"; lines 9302–9309; cf. Cobden quoted at 9274–9282, "the law of nature and of Providence" at 9280
- **Checked:** text file

> What some men are, all without difficulty might be. Employ the same means, and the same results will follow. That there should be a class of men who live by their daily labour in every state is the ordinance of God, and doubtless is a wise and righteous one; but that this class should be otherwise than frugal, contented, intelligent, and happy, is not the design of Providence, but springs solely from the weakness, self-indulgence, and perverseness of man himself.

The key passage for the whole reading. The frame is *fully* kept: class is God's ordinance, and Providence has a design. Yet unhappiness within the class is laid "solely" on the individual. So personalizing failure does not need frame loss; here the theological frame is what licenses it. (It also faintly echoes the sanctified station of C4.2, now with self-improvement *inside* the station, "contented".) This is the strongest single text for C7.4's pattern and against its proposed cause.

### smiles-selfhelp.12 · Ch. XI — "To respect myself, to develop myself—this is my true duty", owed "to society and to its Author"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XI, "Self-Culture"; lines 10351–10369
- **Checked:** text file

> At least we ought to be able to say, as Richter did, "I have made as much out of myself as could be made of the stuff, and no man should require more;" for it is every man's duty to discipline and guide himself, with God's help, according to his responsibilities and the faculties with which he has been endowed. [...] The humblest may say, "To respect myself, to develop myself—this is my true duty in life. An integral and responsible part of the great system of society, I owe it to society and to its Author not to degrade or destroy either my body, mind, or instincts. [...] And as I respect myself, so am I equally bound to respect others, as they on their part are bound to respect me." Hence mutual respect, justice, and order, of which law becomes the written record and guarantee.

The plainest answer to the key question. Self-development is a *duty owed* to society and God, done "with God's help", within "the stuff" and "faculties [...] endowed" (a given nature, not a self-made one). The Self is "an integral and responsible part of the great system of society". The vocabulary ("develop myself") is the later one; the frame is the old one.

### smiles-selfhelp.13 · Ch. XI — against "Byronism" and "Wertherism"; Channing on America

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C4.15 (complicates), C6.* (weak; see note)
- **Location:** ch. XI; lines 9990–9998
- **Checked:** text file

> It is perhaps to the neglect of physical exercise that we find amongst students so frequent a tendency towards discontent, unhappiness, inaction, and reverie,—displaying itself in contempt for real life and disgust at the beaten tracks of men,—a tendency which in England has been called Byronism, and in Germany Wertherism. Dr. Channing noted the same growth in America, which led him to make the remark, that "too many of our young men grow up in a school of despair." The only remedy for this green-sickness in youth is physical exercise—action, work, and bodily occupation.

The popular self-help book sets itself *against* the Romantic singular Self that despises "the beaten tracks of men" and treats it as a bodily ailment. So the two strands the essay may want to join (Romantic singularity and the self-made man) are opposed in the source. Channing, Emerson's Unitarian forerunner, is cited only as a witness to the same malaise in America.

### smiles-selfhelp.14 · Ch. XII — "No individual in the universe stands alone"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C5.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XII, "Example—Models"; lines 11367–11376
- **Checked:** text file

> There is, indeed, an essence of immortality in the life of man, even in this world. No individual in the universe stands alone; he is a component part of a system of mutual dependencies; and by his several acts he either increases or diminishes the sum of human good now and for ever. As the present is rooted in the past, and the lives and examples of our forefathers still to a great extent influence us, so are we by our daily acts contributing to form the condition and character of the future. Man is a fruit formed and ripened by the culture of all the foregoing centuries

The opposite of the Self "prior to its world" (C5.6, SPEC §2 "Singularity"): man is the *fruit* of the centuries, part of "a system of mutual dependencies". Even the self-helper's answerability is to "the sum of human good". Strong against.

### smiles-selfhelp.15 · Ch. XIII — Character is "moral order embodied in the individual"; races "obtain just so much as they deserve"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.4 (for)
- **Location:** ch. XIII, "Character—The True Gentleman"; lines 11959–11969
- **Checked:** text file

> Character is human nature in its best form. It is moral order embodied in the individual. Men of character are not only the conscience of society, but in every well-governed State they are its best motive power [...]. Laws and institutions are but its outgrowth. In the just balance of nature, individuals, nations, and races, will obtain just so much as they deserve, and no more. And as effect finds its cause, so surely does quality of character amongst a people produce its befitting results.

Two findings in one passage. (a) The final ideal, Character, is defined by a moral order *outside* the person: frame kept. (b) A just-world principle: what anyone, any nation or race gets is what it deserves. This is C7.4 in metaphysical form, and again the frame (the "just balance of nature") is what produces the personal attribution, not its absence.

### smiles-selfhelp.16 · Ch. XIII — Horner's character "formed [...] by himself"; Erskine "leave the consequence to God"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C4.4 (complicates, as later echo)
- **Location:** ch. XIII; lines 11997–12002, 12044–12051
- **Checked:** text file

> By what, then, was it? Merely by sense, industry, good principles, and a good heart—qualities which no well-constituted mind need ever despair of attaining. It was the force of his character that raised him; and this character not impressed upon him by nature, but formed, out of no peculiarly fine elements, by himself. [...] "It was a first command and counsel of my earliest youth," he said, "always to do what my conscience told me to be a duty, and to leave the consequence to God. [...] I have found it, on the contrary, the road to prosperity and wealth, and I shall point out the same path to my children for their pursuit."

Self-formation is real in Smiles ("formed [...] by himself", not by nature), but its materials are "good principles" and its rule is conscience and duty, with outcomes left to God. Erskine's last sentence is the practical-syllogism shape of C4.4 in lay form: duty done, prosperity follows, and that is the path to teach.

### smiles-selfhelp.17 · Ch. XIII — the gentleman is forbearing toward "those whose advantages in life have not been equal to his own"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XIII; lines 12684–12691
- **Checked:** text file

> Gentleness is indeed the best test of gentlemanliness. A consideration for the feelings of others, for his inferiors and dependants as well as his equals, and respect for their self-respect, will pervade the true gentleman's whole conduct. He will rather himself suffer a small injury, than by an uncharitable construction of another's behaviour, incur the risk of committing a great wrong. He will be forbearant of the weaknesses, the failings, and the errors, of those whose advantages in life have not been equal to his own.

A counterweight inside the book: unequal "advantages" are admitted, and the ideal man is told *not* to put an uncharitable construction on others' failings. Smiles's moral ideal checks the blame his economics licenses (.10, .11). So C7.4's "never" is false even in Smiles.

### smiles-selfhelp.18 · Ch. XIII — habits as self-woven "chains"; "the habit of happy thought"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C7.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIII; lines 12175–12182, 12209–12213
- **Checked:** text file

> Self-respect, self-help, application, industry, integrity—all are of the nature of habits, not beliefs. Principles, in fact, are but the names which we assign to habits [...]. It thus happens that as we grow older, a portion of our free activity and individuality becomes suspended in habit; our actions become of the nature of fate; and we are bound by the chains which we have woven around ourselves. [...] And we possess the power, to a great extent, of so exercising the will as to direct the thoughts upon objects calculated to yield happiness and improvement rather than their opposites. In this way the habit of happy thought may be made to spring up like any other habit.

Two links toward the later culture. (a) "Self-help" becomes a *habit*, not a creed: the Self makes its own fate, but as a limit ("chains"), not a power. (b) Steering thought toward happiness by will is mind-cure in miniature, still "to a great extent" and framed as habit-training. For C7.3: the self that weaves its own chains is the beginning of a burden carried alone, but Smiles offers it as a relief.

### smiles-selfhelp.19 · Ch. XII — Franklin from Cotton Mather; Drew from Franklin

- **Claims:** C6.4 (complicates), C4.4 (complicates, as reception)
- **Location:** ch. XII; lines 11640–11645
- **Checked:** text file

> Franklin was accustomed to attribute his usefulness and eminence to his having early read Cotton Mather's 'Essays to do Good'—a book which grew out of Mather's own life. And see how good example draws other men after it, and propagates itself through future generations in all lands. For Samuel Drew avers that he framed his own life, and especially his business habits, after the model left on record by Benjamin Franklin.

The one America-to-Britain route the book itself traces runs Puritan → Franklin → English artisan, and it runs through *doing good* (Mather), not through Emersonian self-reliance. For C6.4 (Protestant inheritance without a counterweight): the inheritance Smiles takes from America is still a Puritan ethic of service. Franklin appears 11 times, Emerson never.
