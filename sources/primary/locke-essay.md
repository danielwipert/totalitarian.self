# Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding* (1689; II.xxvii added in the 2nd edition, 1694)

- **Edition used:** Project Gutenberg text, based on a later edition
- **Text files:** `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` (Books I–II), `texts/locke-essay-vol2.txt` (III–IV), both clean
- **Standard modern edition** (for final citations): ed. Nidditch (Oxford, 1975)
- **Citation form:** book.chapter.section, e.g. II.xxvii.26.
- **Numbering warning:** in II.xxvii this edition's section numbers run **1–2 higher than Nidditch's** (this edition's §28 "Person a forensic Term" is Nidditch §26). Always give the Nidditch number in the essay; until each is checked against Nidditch, mark it "verify."
- **Claims to test:** C4.9, C4.10, C4.11, C4.12, C4.14, C7.1

## Passages

### locke-essay.01 · II.xxvii.26 (Nidditch) — "Person a forensic Term"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (for), C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** this edition §28; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` line 12192
- **Checked:** text file

> PERSON, as I take it, is the name for this self. Wherever a man finds what he calls himself, there, I think, another may say is the same person. It is a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit; and so belongs only to intelligent agents, capable of a law, and happiness, and misery. This personality extends itself beyond present existence to what is past, only by consciousness,—whereby it becomes concerned and accountable; owns and imputes to itself past actions, just upon the same ground and for the same reason as it does the present. All which is founded in a concern for happiness, the unavoidable concomitant of consciousness [...]

Strong support for C4.10. Note also the last line: the person is "founded in a concern for happiness." The forensic Self is accountable *and* self-concerned, which may help §7 (guilt and narcissism from the same root).

### locke-essay.02 · II.xxvii.25 (Nidditch, verify) — "the same continued consciousness"

> In all which account of self, the same numerical SUBSTANCE is not considered a making the same self; but the same continued CONSCIOUSNESS, in which several substances may have been united, and again separated from it [...]

Verifies the spec's quotation (§4). Identity rests on consciousness, explicitly not on substance.

### locke-essay.04 · II.xxvii.15 (Nidditch, verify) — the prince and the cobbler

- **Claims:** C4.9 (complicates), C4.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** this edition §17, "The body, as well as the soul, goes to the making of a Man"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 11926–11940
- **Checked:** text file

> For should the soul of a prince, carrying with it the consciousness of the prince’s past life, enter and inform the body of a cobbler, as soon as deserted by his own soul, every one sees he would be the same PERSON with the prince, accountable only for the prince’s actions: but who would say it was the same MAN? The body too goes to the making the man [...]

Locke splits *man* (a living body) from *person* (consciousness). He doesn't discard the body; he assigns it to a different concept. The essay should say that Locke's *person*, not Locke's *man*, is the ancestor of the Self.

### locke-essay.05 · II.i.2 — "white paper"

- **Claims:** C4.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.i.2, "All Ideas come from Sensation or Reflection"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` line 3039
- **Checked:** text file

> Let us then suppose the mind to be, as we say, white paper, void of all characters, without any ideas:—How comes it to be furnished? [...] To this I answer, in one word, from EXPERIENCE.

Locke's own image is "white paper." The phrase *tabula rasa* does not appear anywhere in the *Essay* (searched both volumes). Also, the claim is about *ideas*, not the whole person: "cuts it loose from inherited nature" (C4.11) goes further than this text does.

### locke-essay.06 · I.iii.3 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "Nature, I confess, has put into man a desire of happiness"

- **Claims:** C4.11 (against), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book I ch. iii §3, "Objection: though Men deny them in their Practice [...]"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 1622–1634
- **Checked:** text file

> Nature, I confess, has put into man a desire of happiness and an aversion to misery: these indeed are innate practical principles which (as practical principles ought) DO continue constantly to operate and influence all our actions without ceasing: these may be observed in all persons and all ages, steady and universal; but these are INCLINATIONS OF THE APPETITE to good, not impressions of truth on the understanding. I deny not that there are natural tendencies imprinted on the minds of men [...] but this makes nothing for innate characters on the mind, which are to be the principles of knowledge regulating our practice.

This decides the Book I question. Locke's attack is aimed at innate *knowledge* (principles "on the understanding"). He openly grants innate appetites and "natural tendencies." Book I does not cut the Self loose from inherited nature.

### locke-essay.07 · I.iii.22 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "white paper receives any characters"

- **Claims:** C4.11 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book I ch. iii §22, "How men commonly come by their Principles"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 2178–2186
- **Checked:** text file

> doctrines that have been derived from no better original than the superstition of a nurse, or the authority of an old woman, may, by length of time and consent of neighbours, grow up to the dignity of PRINCIPLES in religion or morality. For such, who are careful (as they call it) to principle children well, [...] instil into the unwary, and as yet unprejudiced, understanding, (for white paper receives any characters,) those doctrines they would have them retain and profess.

Here Locke uses his own white-paper image against the Self's independence. Because the page is blank, nurse, neighbours and custom can write anything on it. "Built from experience" therefore means built largely *by others*. That suits §5 (screens) better than it suits a self-made Self. See also §25, line 2229: "custom, a greater power than nature".

### locke-essay.08 · I.iv.24–25 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "Men must think and know for themselves"

- **Claims:** C4.13 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** Book I ch. iv §§24–25; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 2912, 2925–2930, 2956–2967
- **Checked:** text file

> perhaps we should make greater progress in the discovery of rational and contemplative knowledge, if we sought it in the fountain, IN THE CONSIDERATION OF THINGS THEMSELVES; and made use rather of our own thoughts than other men’s to find it. For I think we may as rationally hope to see with other men’s eyes, as to know by other men’s understandings. [...] it was of no small advantage to those who affected to be masters and teachers, to make this the principle of principles,—THAT PRINCIPLES MUST NOT BE QUESTIONED. [...] Nor is it a small power it gives one man over another, to have the authority to be the dictator of principles, and teacher of unquestionable truths; and to make a man swallow that for an innate principle which may serve to his purpose who teacheth them.

This is the strongest text for C4.13. The denial of innate principles is presented as freeing people from "dictators of principles," which is close to "acknowledging difference against orders that denied it." Note that it concerns *judgement and knowledge*, not feelings or desires.

### locke-essay.09 · II.xxxiii.7 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — antipathies "truly natural [...] born with us"

- **Claims:** C4.11 (against)
- **Location:** II.xxxiii §7, "Some Antipathies an Effect of it"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 14159–14163
- **Checked:** text file

> I say most of the antipathies, I do not say all; for some of them are truly natural, depend upon our original constitution, and are born with us; but a great part of those which are counted natural, would have been known to be from unheeded, though perhaps early, impressions, or wanton fancies at first [...]

Locke grants an inborn "original constitution" that shapes our likes and dislikes. His point is that we should *shrink* our estimate of it, not deny it. (This chapter is a 4th-edition addition; see the Epistle, line 635–636.) Also compare II.xi.2, line 5087: "hastiness and precipitancy, natural to some tempers."

### locke-essay.10 · II.xxvii.17–18 (Nidditch, verify) — "concerned for itself" / "all the right and justice of reward and punishment"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (for), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** this edition §§19–20; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 11976–11979, 11998–12001
- **Checked:** text file

> SELF is that conscious thinking thing,—whatever substance made up of, [...] which is sensible or conscious of pleasure and pain, capable of happiness or misery, and so is concerned for itself, as far as that consciousness extends. [...] In this personal identity is founded all the right and justice of reward and punishment; happiness and misery being that for which every one is concerned for HIMSELF [...]

This confirms C4.10 outside §26/28: the person exists to ground reward and punishment, and it is defined by self-concern. For C7.1, though, Locke's self-concern is the *motive* that makes accountability work. He does not present it as a source of guilt.

### locke-essay.11 · II.xxvii.22 (Nidditch, verify) — the drunkard and "the Great Day"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (for), C7.1 (against)
- **Location:** this edition §24 (no heading in this edition); `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 12087–12098
- **Checked:** text file

> But is not a man drunk and sober the same person? why else is he punished for the fact he commits when drunk, though he be never afterwards conscious of it? [...] Human laws punish both, with a justice suitable to THEIR way of knowledge;—because, in these cases, they cannot distinguish certainly what is real, what counterfeit [...] But in the Great Day, wherein the secrets of all hearts shall be laid open, it may be reasonable to think, no one shall be made to answer for what he knows nothing of; but shall receive his doom, his conscience accusing or excusing him.

Locke's forensic person *limits* guilt: in true justice no one answers for what consciousness cannot reach. The final judge is God, not the Self or society. This works against reading the forensic Self as the source of boundless guilt. It also shows the framework is explicitly theological, which matters for C7.1's "unrelated to original sin."

### locke-essay.12 · II.xxvii.26 (Nidditch) — rest of "Person a forensic Term": "CREATED miserable"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (for), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** this edition §28 (continues locke-essay.01); `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 12205–12220
- **Checked:** text file

> And therefore whatever past actions it cannot reconcile or APPROPRIATE to that present self by consciousness, it can be no more concerned in than if they had never been done: and to receive pleasure or pain, i.e. reward or punishment, on the account of any such action, is all one as to be made happy or miserable in its first being, without any demerit at all. For, supposing a MAN punished now for what he had done in another life, whereof he could be made to have no consciousness at all, what difference is there between that punishment and being CREATED miserable? And therefore, conformable to this, the apostle tells us, that, at the great day, when every one shall ‘receive according to his doings, the secrets of all hearts shall be laid open.’ The sentence shall be justified by the consciousness all persons shall have, that THEY THEMSELVES [...] are the SAME that committed those actions, and deserve that punishment for them.

The forensic chapter ends at the Last Judgement. Locke's person answers to God, and it is shielded from punishment for anything it cannot own (an implicit refusal of inherited guilt). A secular, self-administered guilt (C7.1) must be a *later* change to this frame, not Locke's own position.

### locke-essay.13 · II.xxvii.25 (Nidditch, verify) — "the more probable opinion" is an immaterial substance

- **Claims:** C4.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** this edition §27; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 12156–12157 (same section as locke-essay.02)
- **Checked:** text file

> I agree, the more probable opinion is, that this consciousness is annexed to, and the affection of, one individual immaterial substance.

C4.9 stands, but it should say that Locke does not deny the soul. He holds that an immaterial substance is probably the bearer of consciousness. He only denies that sameness of substance is what makes the same *person*. The claim should read "not on *sameness of* substance."

### locke-essay.14 · II.xxi.22 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "willing to shift off from himself [...] all thoughts of guilt"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C4.10 (for)
- **Location:** II.xxi §22, "In respect of willing, a Man is not free"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 8608–8612
- **Checked:** text file

> But the inquisitive mind of man, willing to shift off from himself, as far as he can, all thoughts of guilt, though it be by putting himself into a worse state than that of fatal necessity, is not content with this: freedom, unless it reaches further than this, will not serve the turn [...]

Locke's picture of human nature runs the other way from C7.1: people try to *escape* guilt, even by denying their own freedom. The free-will debate itself is read as a flight from accountability.

### locke-essay.15 · II.xxi.56 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "The mind has a different relish"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.xxi §56, "All men seek happiness, but not of the same sort"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 9388–9391, 9405–9415
- **Checked:** text file

> The mind has a different relish, as well as the palate; and you will as fruitlessly endeavour to delight all men with riches or glory [...] as you would to satisfy all men’s hunger with cheese or lobsters [...] If, therefore, men in this life only have hope; if in this life only they can enjoy, it is not strange nor unreasonable, that they should seek their happiness by avoiding all things that disease them here, and by pursuing all that delight them; wherein it will be no wonder to find variety and difference. [...] Men may choose different things, and yet all choose right; supposing them only like a company of poor insects [...]

This is the closest text to the §2 definition of the Self: desires really do differ between people. But Locke puts the relativism under a condition ("if in this life only"). With an afterlife, not every choice is "right." The healthy Self acknowledges difference; the conclusion that every choice is right belongs to a world with no beyond.

### locke-essay.16 · II.xxi.71 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "men can MAKE things or actions more or less pleasing to themselves"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (complicates), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.xxi §71, "We can change the Agreeableness or Disagreeableness in Things"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 9770–9781
- **Checked:** text file

> it will be possibly entertained as a paradox, if it be said, that men can MAKE things or actions more or less pleasing to themselves; and thereby remedy that, to which one may justly impute a great deal of their wandering. Fashion and the common opinion having settled wrong notions, and education and custom ill habits, the just values of things are misplaced, and the palates of men corrupted. Pains should be taken to rectify these; and contrary habits change our pleasures [...] and when happiness is lost, and misery overtakes him, he will confess he did amiss in neglecting it, and condemn himself for it; and I ask every one, whether he has not often done so?

For Locke, one's desires are neither simply given nor simply one's own: fashion and custom corrupt them, and the person is responsible for re-forming them. That cuts against C4.1's "already present." It also extends responsibility, and self-condemnation, to one's own *feelings*. That could seed C7.1, but Locke treats it as a remedy, not a disorder.

### locke-essay.17 · II.xxviii.12 (this edition; Nidditch verify) — "the LAW OF FASHION"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.xxviii §12, "Its Inforcement is Commendation and Discredit"; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 12528–12547
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] the greatest part whereof we shall find to govern themselves chiefly, if not solely, by this LAW OF FASHION; and so they do that which keeps them in reputation with their company, little regard the laws of God, or the magistrate. [...] But no man escapes the punishment of their censure and dislike, who offends against the fashion and opinion of the company he keeps, and would recommend himself to. Nor is there one of ten thousand, who is stiff and insensible enough, to bear up under the constant dislike and condemnation of his own club. [...] nobody that has the least thought or sense of a man about him, can live in society under the constant dislike and ill opinion of his familiars [...]

Locke puts the blame that actually rules people in the *group* ("his own club"), not in the self-accusing individual. That fits §8 (belonging, False Groups) better than §7.1. Any Lockean root for C7.1 must explain how this outward censure turned inward.

### locke-essay.18 · Epistle to the Reader — "one whole new chapter concerning Identity"

- **Claims:** C4.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Epistle to the Reader (the note on the 2nd edition); title page; `texts/locke-essay-vol1.txt` lines 51, 396–403, 408–418; cf. II.xxi.31 line 8788–8789
- **Checked:** text file

> In the Second Edition I added as followeth:—
>
> The bookseller will not forgive me if I say nothing of this New Edition, which he has promised, by the correctness of it, shall make amends for the many faults committed in the former. He desires too, that it should be known that it has one whole new chapter concerning Identity, and many additions and amendments in other places. [...] I must only except the alterations I have made in Book II. chap. xxi. [...] I have found reason somewhat to alter the thoughts I formerly had concerning that which gives the last determination to the Will in all voluntary actions.

This settles from Locke's own words that the Identity chapter (II.xxvii) first appeared in the **2nd edition**. The text does not give that edition's year, so "1694" still needs an outside source. The uneasiness doctrine in II.xxi (locke-essay.14–16; see "upon second thoughts," line 8788) is also a 2nd-edition revision. The title page of the first edition is dated "MDCXC" (1690), not 1689. So 1689 is also not a date this text supports, even for the 1st edition.
