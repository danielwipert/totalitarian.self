# Emerson, "Self-Reliance" and "The Over-Soul" (*Essays: First Series*, 1841)

- **Edition used:** original English (Gutenberg #2944)
- **Text file:** `texts/emerson-essays-first-series.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): *Collected Works of Ralph Waldo Emerson*, vol. II (Harvard)
- **Citation form:** essay title + paragraph, plus this file's line
- **Paragraph numbering:** counted in this file, from the first prose paragraph after the essay's title (epigraph poems excluded; numbered sections 1.–4. count as paragraphs; indented verse quotations do not). Check against CW II before final citation.
- **Claims to test:** C4.19, C4.15, C6.*

## Reading summary

The two essays pull in opposite directions, and Emerson knows it. "Self-Reliance" makes nonconformity a duty, makes imitation "suicide," and dissolves the community's claim (family, charity, church, covenant, party, inheritance) into the "integrity of your own mind." But when it asks *who* is trusted ("Who is the Trustee?", ¶21), the answer is not the individual: it is a universal "Spontaneity or Instinct" of which persons are "organs" and "a passage." "The Over-Soul" says this outright: the individual who "would be something of himself" is the weakness (¶4), and genius is "more like and not less like other men" (¶24).

So the §4 limits are not simply **dropped**. God is **absorbed** (moved inside: "God is here within," SR ¶28; the worshipper "becomes God," OS ¶28), and the community is **replaced** by an impersonal "common nature" that is explicitly "not social" (OS ¶11). Difference is valued, but as the way "the general soul incarnates itself" in each person ("Spiritual Laws" ¶14), not as singularity. What is dropped is every *external, social* check; what remains is an internal one that only the Self can hear. That is closer to the C1.1 pattern ("kept the structure, dropped the limit") than to C4.19 as written, but it is not the singular Self of SPEC §2.

## Passages

### emerson-selfreliance.01 · "Self-Reliance" ¶2–3 — imitation is suicide; trust thyself, accept your place

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: difference as duty; complicates: grounded in God and providence), C4.15 (for), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶2–3; `texts/emerson-essays-first-series.txt` lines 875–897
- **Checked:** text file

> There is a time in every man’s education when he arrives at the conviction that envy is ignorance; that imitation is suicide; that he must take himself for better for worse as his portion; [...] The power which resides in him is new in nature, and none but he knows what that is which he can do, nor does he know until he has tried. [...] We but half express ourselves, and are ashamed of that divine idea which each of us represents. It may be safely trusted as proportionate and of good issues, so it be faithfully imparted, but God will not have his work made manifest by cowards.
>
> Trust thyself: every heart vibrates to that iron string. Accept the place the divine providence has found for you, the society of your contemporaries, the connection of events.

The turn to difference as duty is here ("new in nature", "imitation is suicide"). But the difference is "that divine idea which each of us represents", owed to God, and "Trust thyself" comes paired with *accepting* providence's place and one's age. Answerability before God is the frame difference sits in, not something it replaced.

### emerson-selfreliance.02 · "Self-Reliance" ¶6–7 — society a conspiracy; "No law can be sacred to me but that of my nature"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C4.1 (for: the God-limit is offered and declined), C5.3 (for)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶6–7; lines 944–965
- **Checked:** text file

> Society everywhere is in conspiracy against the manhood of every one of its members. [...] The virtue in most request is conformity. Self-reliance is its aversion. It loves not realities and creators, but names and customs.
>
> Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist. [...] Nothing is at last sacred but the integrity of your own mind. Absolve you to yourself, and you shall have the suffrage of the world. [...] On my saying, “What have I to do with the sacredness of traditions, if I live wholly from within?” my friend suggested,—“But these impulses may be from below, not from above.” I replied, “They do not seem to me to be such; but if I am the Devil’s child, I will live then from the Devil.” No law can be sacred to me but that of my nature. Good and bad are but names very readily transferable to that or this; the only right is what is after my constitution; the only wrong what is against it.

The strongest "for" text in either essay. Conformity is named the vice in so many words. The limit (impulses might come "from below", that is, sin) is put to Emerson directly, and he refuses it: the self's own "constitution" becomes the measure of right.

### emerson-selfreliance.03 · "Self-Reliance" ¶7 — "I shun father and mother"; "Are they *my* poor?"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: the community limit dropped), C1.1 pattern (for), C7.8 (for, as an early ideological source; textual only)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶7; lines 979–996
- **Checked:** text file

> Your goodness must have some edge to it,—else it is none. [...] I shun father and mother and wife and brother when my genius calls me. I would write on the lintels of the door-post, _Whim_. I hope it is somewhat better than whim at last, but we cannot spend the day in explanation. [...] Then again, do not tell me, as a good man did to-day, of my obligation to put all poor men in good situations. Are they _my_ poor? [...] There is a class of persons to whom by all spiritual affinity I am bought and sold; for them I will go to prison if need be; but your miscellaneous popular charities; [...]—though I confess with shame I sometimes succumb and give the dollar, it is a wicked dollar which by and by I shall have the manhood to withhold.

The biblical form survives (Luke 14:26, leaving father and mother; the lintels of Exodus 12) but "genius" stands where God stood: the pattern note in miniature. Obligation is cut back to elective "spiritual affinity", which is a chosen community, not none at all.

### emerson-selfreliance.04 · "Self-Reliance" ¶12–14 — consistency as "terror"; "A foolish consistency"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C4.1 (complicates), C4.10 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶12–14; lines 1076–1098
- **Checked:** text file

> The other terror that scares us from self-trust is our consistency; a reverence for our past act or word because the eyes of others have no other data for computing our orbit than our past acts, and we are loath to disappoint them.
>
> But why should you keep your head over your shoulder? Why drag about this corpse of your memory, lest you contradict somewhat you have stated in this or that public place? Suppose you should contradict yourself; what then? [...]
>
> A foolish consistency is the hobgoblin of little minds, adored by little statesmen and philosophers and divines. With consistency a great soul has simply nothing to do. [...] Speak what you think now in hard words and to-morrow speak what to-morrow thinks in hard words again, though it contradict every thing you said to-day.

Here Emerson rejects Locke's structure itself (the person answerable to others, through memory, for past acts) and not just its limits. This complicates "kept the structure". He moves continuity into "nature" instead (¶15: "no man can violate his nature"; "A character is like an acrostic").

### emerson-selfreliance.05 · "Self-Reliance" ¶21 — "Who is the Trustee?"; "organs of its activity"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (complicates), C5.3 (against), C5.6 (for: prior to "tuitions")
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶21; lines 1213–1234
- **Checked:** text file

> Who is the Trustee? What is the aboriginal Self, on which a universal reliance may be grounded? [...] The inquiry leads us to that source, at once the essence of genius, of virtue, and of life, which we call Spontaneity or Instinct. We denote this primary wisdom as Intuition, whilst all later teachings are tuitions. [...] We lie in the lap of immense intelligence, which makes us receivers of its truth and organs of its activity. When we discern justice, when we discern truth, we do nothing of ourselves, but allow a passage to its beams.

This is the hinge of the essay. The trusted "Self" is "aboriginal" and *prior to* all teaching (for C5.6), but it is not the person's own: we are "receivers", "organs", "a passage". By SPEC §5's own imagery this is experience flowing *through* the Self, not *from* it, so it is against C5.3 as a reading of Emerson.

### emerson-selfreliance.06 · "Self-Reliance" ¶22, ¶25 — "means, teachers, texts, temples fall"; "exclude example and experience"

- **Claims:** C5.6 (for), C5.7 (for: history), C4.1 (for: mediation falls away)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶22 (lines 1256–1271) and ¶25 (lines 1309–1311)
- **Checked:** text file

> Whenever a mind is simple and receives a divine wisdom, old things pass away,—means, teachers, texts, temples fall; it lives now, and absorbs past and future into the present hour. [...] The centuries are conspirators against the sanity and authority of the soul. Time and space are but physiological colors which the eye makes, but the soul is light: where it is, is day; where it was, is night; and history is an impertinence and an injury if it be any thing more than a cheerful apologue or parable of my being and becoming.
>
> [¶25] [...] the way, the thought, the good shall be wholly strange and new. It shall exclude example and experience. You take the way from man, not to man.

The clearest text for the Self as *prior to* experience and history. The list "means, teachers, texts, temples" names exactly the mediations Luther kept (word, preaching, congregation; C4.3). Here they all fall. Note that the agent is still "a divine wisdom" received, not the person's own.

### emerson-selfreliance.07 · "Self-Reliance" ¶30–32 — "no covenants but proximities"; the two confessionals

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: the limits absorbed), C1.1 pattern (for), C6.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶30 (lines 1390–1394), ¶31 (lines 1413–1425), ¶32 (lines 1427–1430)
- **Checked:** text file

> Be it known unto you that henceforward I obey no law less than the eternal law. I will have no covenants but proximities. I shall endeavour to nourish my parents, to support my family, to be the chaste husband of one wife,—but these relations I must fill after a new and unprecedented way. I appeal from your customs. I must be myself.
>
> The populace think that your rejection of popular standards is a rejection of all standard, and mere antinomianism; [...] But the law of consciousness abides. There are two confessionals, in one or the other of which we must be shriven. You may fulfil your round of duties by clearing yourself in the _direct_, or in the _reflex_ way. Consider whether you have satisfied your relations to father, mother, cousin, neighbor, town, cat, and dog; whether any of these can upbraid you. But I may also neglect this reflex standard and absolve me to myself. I have my own stern claims and perfect circle. [...] If any one imagines that this law is lax, let him keep its commandment one day.
>
> And truly it demands something godlike in him who has cast off the common motives of humanity and has ventured to trust himself for a taskmaster. High be his heart, faithful his will, clear his sight, that he may in good earnest be doctrine, society, law, to himself, [...]

The single best text for the pattern. The *structure* of answerability is kept in full (confessional, being "shriven", duty, "commandment"); the community's standard ("father, mother, cousin, neighbor, town") is the one dropped; and the self becomes "doctrine, society, law, to himself". That is absorption, not abolition. "I will have no covenants" names and refuses the Puritan covenant tradition, which shows that tradition was still there to refuse (cf. the C6.4 pressure point).

### emerson-selfreliance.08 · "Self-Reliance" ¶42–43 — the American artist; "Every great man is a unique"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C4.15 (for), C5.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶42 (lines 1583–1589), ¶43 (lines 1591–1605)
- **Checked:** text file

> And why need we copy the Doric or the Gothic model? Beauty, convenience, grandeur of thought and quaint expression are as near to us as to any, and if the American artist will study with hope and love the precise thing to be done by him, considering the climate, the soil, the length of the day, the wants of the people, the habit and form of the government, he will create a house in which all these will find themselves fitted, and taste and sentiment will be satisfied also.
>
> Insist on yourself; never imitate. [...] That which each can do best, none but his Maker can teach him. No man yet knows what it is, nor can, till that person has exhibited it. [...] Every great man is a unique. The Scipionism of Scipio is precisely that part he could not borrow. [...] Not possibly will the soul, all rich, all eloquent, with thousand-cloven tongue, deign to repeat itself; [...]

Uniqueness as the norm, stated as plainly as anywhere ("Every great man is a unique"). But the originality of the paragraph just before is *relational*: fitted to "climate, soil, [...] the wants of the people, [...] the government". That is SPEC §2's healthy, relational difference, not singularity. Even the unique gift is taught by "his Maker".

### emerson-selfreliance.09 · "Self-Reliance" ¶49 — property, inheritance, conventions; "Is not a man better than a town?"

- **Claims:** C6.2 (complicates), C6.5 (complicates), C4.19 (for: community dropped)
- **Location:** "Self-Reliance" ¶49; lines 1677–1704
- **Checked:** text file

> And so the reliance on Property, including the reliance on governments which protect it, is the want of self-reliance. [...] Especially he hates what he has if he see that it is accidental,—came to him by inheritance, or gift, or crime; [...] But that which a man is, does always by necessity acquire, and what the man acquires is living property, which does not wait the beck of rulers, or mobs, or revolutions, or fire, or storm, or bankruptcies, but perpetually renews itself wherever the man breathes. [...] The political parties meet in numerous conventions; [...] the young patriot feels himself stronger than before by a new thousand of eyes and arms. In like manner the reformers summon conventions and vote and resolve in multitude. Not so, O friends! will the God deign to enter and inhabit you, but by a method precisely the reverse. It is only as a man puts off all foreign support and stands alone that I see him to be strong and to prevail. He is weaker by every recruit to his banner. Is not a man better than a town?

This is evidence for the *ideology*, not the *conditions*. 1841 America, as Emerson describes it, is thick with parties, conventions and reform societies (Tocqueville's associations). The checks were present, and Emerson attacks them. Inheritance is despised and acquisition praised ("living property"), which fits C6.5's spirit, but reliance on property is itself condemned.

### emerson-oversoul.01 · "The Over-Soul" ¶1–2 — "a stream whose source is hidden"; "not a cause"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (against), C4.19 (complicates), C5.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶1 (lines 5025–5029), ¶2 (lines 5031–5036)
- **Checked:** text file

> Man is a stream whose source is hidden. Our being is descending into us from we know not whence. [...] I am constrained every moment to acknowledge a higher origin for events than the will I call mine.
>
> As with events, so is it with thoughts. When I watch that flowing river, which, out of regions I see not, pours for a season its streams into me, I see that I am a pensioner; not a cause, but a surprised spectator of this ethereal water; that I desire and look up and put myself in the attitude of reception, but from some alien energy the visions come.

The plainest denial that the Self is a source: "not a cause", "a pensioner", "a higher origin [...] than the will I call mine". The dependence on God that C5.8 says the later Self dropped is here kept.

### emerson-oversoul.02 · "The Over-Soul" ¶3 — "every man's particular being is contained and made one with all other"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶3; lines 5038–5046
- **Checked:** text file

> The Supreme Critic on the errors of the past and the present, and the only prophet of that which must be, is that great nature in which we rest as the earth lies in the soft arms of the atmosphere; that Unity, that Over-soul, within which every man’s particular being is contained and made one with all other; that common heart of which all sincere conversation is the worship, to which all right action is submission; that overpowering reality which confutes our tricks and talents, and constrains every one to pass for what he is, and to speak from his character and not from his tongue, [...]

A limit that is moved inward, not dropped: a "Supreme Critic" to which "all right action is submission". Particular being is "contained and made one with all other", which is the opposite of singularity. Yet it is this same power that "constrains every one to pass for what he is", so uniqueness and unity are one doctrine for Emerson.

### emerson-oversoul.03 · "The Over-Soul" ¶4 — "The weakness of the will begins when the individual would be something of himself"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (against), C5.3 (against)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶4; lines 5080–5093
- **Checked:** text file

> From within or from behind, a light shines through us upon things and makes us aware that we are nothing, but the light is all. [...] When it breathes through his intellect, it is genius; when it breathes through his will, it is virtue; when it flows through his affection, it is love. And the blindness of the intellect begins when it would be something of itself. The weakness of the will begins when the individual would be something of himself. All reform aims in some one particular to let the soul have its way through us; in other words, to engage us to obey.

The strongest "against" text. Wanting to be "something of himself" is named as *the* weakness, and the duty is obedience. If C4.19 says "trust in one's own nature is the virtue", this is where Emerson says whose nature he means: not the individual's.

### emerson-oversoul.04 · "The Over-Soul" ¶11 — "That third party or common nature is not social; it is impersonal; is God"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: community dropped; complicates: replaced by God, not by the Self), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶11; lines 5201–5207
- **Checked:** text file

> Persons are supplementary to the primary teaching of the soul. In youth we are mad for persons. Childhood and youth see all the world in them. But the larger experience of man discovers the identical nature appearing through them all. Persons themselves acquaint us with the impersonal. In all conversation between two persons tacit reference is made, as to a third party, to a common nature. That third party or common nature is not social; it is impersonal; is God.

This is exactly where the community limit goes. It is kept as a bond between persons, but explicitly not "social": the common thing is God. Community is dropped as an *authority*, but persons are still bound to one another through God. Difference ("persons") is "supplementary"; identity is primary.

### emerson-oversoul.05 · "The Over-Soul" ¶24 — "genius is religious [...] more like and not less like other men"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (against), C4.15 (against, for Emerson)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶24; lines 5424–5426
- **Checked:** text file

> But genius is religious. It is a larger imbibing of the common heart. It is not anomalous, but more like and not less like other men.

Read this against selfreliance.08 ("Every great man is a unique"). In "The Over-Soul" greatness is *more likeness*, not more unlikeness. Emerson does not make unlikeness a value in itself.

### emerson-oversoul.06 · "The Over-Soul" ¶28 — "becomes God"; "not a valve, not a wall"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: God absorbed into the Self; complicates: no singular Self remains to absorb it)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶28; lines 5500–5503, 5532–5535
- **Checked:** text file

> Ineffable is the union of man and God in every act of the soul. The simplest person who in his integrity worships God, becomes God; yet for ever and ever the influx of this better and universal self is new and unsearchable. [...] And this because the heart in thee is the heart of all; not a valve, not a wall, not an intersection is there anywhere in nature, but one blood rolls uninterruptedly an endless circulation through all men, as the water of the globe is all one sea, and, truly seen, its tide is one.

The worshipper who "becomes God" is the absorption C4.19 predicts, taken as far as it can go. But the absorbing "self" is "universal" and has no wall against other persons. If a later reader keeps "becomes God" and loses "the heart of all", that is the pattern note's dropped limit, but that loss happens in reception, not in this text.

### emerson-oversoul.07 · "The Over-Soul" ¶29–30 — "go into his closet"; "what can Calvin or Swedenborg say?"; "I become public and human"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (for), C4.1 (for: mediation dropped), C5.3 (complicates), C4.19 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Over-Soul" ¶29 (lines 5537–5550), ¶30 (lines 5552–5558, 5575–5590)
- **Checked:** text file

> Let man then learn the revelation of all nature and all thought to his heart; this, namely; that the Highest dwells with him; that the sources of nature are in his own mind, if the sentiment of duty is there. But if he would know what the great God speaketh, he must ‘go into his closet and shut the door,’ as Jesus said. God will not make himself manifest to cowards. He must greatly listen to himself, withdrawing himself from all the accents of other men’s devotion. [...] When I rest in perfect humility, when I burn with pure love, what can Calvin or Swedenborg say?
>
> It makes no difference whether the appeal is to numbers or to one. The faith that stands on authority is not faith. [...] Great is the soul, and plain. It is no flatterer, it is no follower; it never appeals from itself. It believes in itself. [...] Behold, it saith, I am born into the great, the universal mind. I, the imperfect, adore my own Perfect. [...] More and more the surges of everlasting nature enter into me, and I become public and human in my regards and actions. [...] He will cease from what is base and frivolous in his life and be content with all places and with any service he can render.

The Protestant inheritance taken to its end (C6.4): the private closet against congregation, numbers, authority and Calvin himself. "[T]he sources of nature are in his own mind" is as close as Emerson comes to C5.3, but it is conditional ("if the sentiment of duty is there"). The essay ends with the self becoming "public and human" and "content [...] with any service", which echoes Luther's free lord and dutiful servant (C4.3 pressure point).

### emerson-spirituallaws.01 · "Spiritual Laws" ¶14 — "Each man has his own vocation [...] the mode in which the general soul incarnates itself in him"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: difference as the only call; complicates: grounded in the general soul), C4.15 (for), C6.4 (for), C4.4 (for, as later evidence: calling kept, election dropped)
- **Location:** "Spiritual Laws" ¶14; lines 2670–2687
- **Checked:** text file

> Each man has his own vocation. The talent is the call. [...] This talent and this call depend on his organization, or the mode in which the general soul incarnates itself in him. He inclines to do something which is easy to him and good when it is done, but which no other man can do. He has no rival. For the more truly he consults his own powers, the more difference will his work exhibit from the work of any other. [...] Every man has this call of the power to do somewhat unique, and no man has any other call. The pretence that he has another call, a summons by name and personal election and outward “signs that mark him extraordinary, and not in the roll of common men,” is fanaticism, and betrays obtuseness to perceive that there is one mind in all the individuals, and no respect of persons therein.

This passage decides the question. Difference is the *only* calling ("no man has any other call"), so C4.19's "duty" holds. But difference is how "the general soul incarnates itself", and it rests on "one mind in all the individuals". The Protestant vocabulary is kept and transformed: "calling" survives, while Calvinist "personal election" and "signs" are dismissed as "fanaticism". That is the §4 pattern applied directly to C4.4.
