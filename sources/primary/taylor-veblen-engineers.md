## Summary of the reading

- **C9.6, "claim objectivity":** yes, both. Taylor's whole book is a claim that management is "a true science" (taylor.02) and that questions once bargained over (a fair day's work, fair pay, what is good for the worker) can be settled by "impartial scientific investigation" (taylor.10, .11). Veblen's engineers are "driven by no commercial bias" (veblen-engineers.02) and serve a technology that is "impersonal and dispassionate" (.08).
- **C9.6, "deny their own values":** no, as written. Both state their values openly and often. Taylor calls his foundation a "firm conviction" (taylor.03), names prosperity, harmony, justice and the rights of "the whole people" (taylor.13), moralizes about drink and thrift (taylor.10), and says the change he wants is a revolution in "mental attitude" (taylor.14). Veblen's engineers have workmanship, the common good and "Live and Let Live" (veblen-engineers.04, .07). What the texts *do* show is narrower: **values presented as findings.** Taylor says the wage that is good for the man was found by experiment (taylor.10); the "one best method" is best by the single measure of speed, which is a choice he never argues (taylor.05). That is a real version of C9.6, but it is a confusion of value with fact, not a denial of having values.
- **C9.6, "in-group / out-group":** yes, both, and stated openly. Taylor: "engineers and managers" against labor agitators and "sentimentalists" (taylor.04), his own class position (taylor.08), and contempt for the worker as ox-like (taylor.07). Veblen: the technicians become "class-conscious" (veblen-engineers.04); everyone trained in business is to be excluded from positions of trust (.10). This part of C9.6 is supported, but it is avowed, not hidden.
- **C9.6, "nihilists":** against. Neither author resents belief. Taylor is an optimist and a moralist; Veblen's engineers are defined by an instinct of workmanship and a common decency. The one thing close to the claim is Veblen's positivism: for the engineers "nothing is quite real" that cannot be measured, so ownership and credit are "make-believe" (veblen-engineers.06). That disenchants *others'* beliefs; it is not the engineers' disgust at belief as such.
- **The individual and the system:** Taylor puts "the system" first and the man second (taylor.01), moves knowledge from the worker into management (taylor.06), and has workers "moved ... very much as chessmen" (see To do). This is the *opposite* of the Totalitarian Self in the worker. But Taylor answers the "wooden man" charge and claims each man keeps "his own individuality" (taylor.12, .14). And the Self, if anywhere, would be in the planner, not in the system: the management assumes the whole of the judgment. §9's claim that technocrats *carry* the Totalitarian Self is not supported by these founders; what they carry is the subordination of the person to a system run by experts.
- **C9.8:** mostly against. Taylor puts value in "the whole people," whose rights are "greater than those of either employer or employee" (taylor.13). Veblen locates value in a "joint stock of knowledge" held "in common by the civilized peoples" (veblen-engineers.03), says technology's end is "to serve human needs" (.08), and would abolish corporate ownership as absentee ownership (.11). The founding technocrat is anti-corporate; the system serves an environment, it is not prior to it.
- **C8.7:** Taylor supplies a disguise word for obedience: being "a high-priced man" means doing "exactly as he's told" (taylor.07), and "cooperation" names what he himself calls "enforced cooperation" (taylor.09, .14). Veblen supplies a 1921 witness to the mechanism in the business class: absentee ownership as "the idol of every true American heart" (veblen-engineers.12).
- **Veblen is not a technocrat speaking for himself.** He writes as an outsider ("a layman," veblen-engineers ch. III end, line 2692), with heavy irony, and predicts the engineers will *not* act: they are "harmless and docile" and "pretty well commercialized" (.09). And he counts Taylor's "efficiency engineers" on the commercial side (.05). So "technocrats" in 1911–21 are not one group.

## Passages: Taylor

### taylor.01 · Introduction — "In the past the man has been first; in the future the system must be first"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C9.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Introduction; lines 98–108
- **Checked:** text file. The wording in the reader brief is exact.

> In the future it will be appreciated that our leaders must be trained right as well as born right, and that no great man can (with the old system of personal management) hope to compete with a number of ordinary men who have been properly organized so as efficiently to cooperate. In the past the man has been first; in the future the system must be first. This in no sense, however, implies that great men are not needed. On the contrary, the first object of any good system must be that of developing first-class men; and under systematic management the best man rises to the top more certainly and more rapidly than ever before.

The system is put before the man, the opposite of the Self as origin. But Taylor's system is prior to the *man*, not to its environment, and its stated "first object" is developing men. The target is the self-made "Captains of industry" (line 96), not the worker.

### taylor.02 · Introduction — management "a true science," for churches and government too

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: claims science), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** Introduction; lines 120–127, 133–139
- **Checked:** text file

> Third. To prove that the best management is a true science, resting upon clearly defined laws, rules, and principles, as a foundation. And further to show that the fundamental principles of scientific management are applicable to all kinds of human activities, from our simplest individual acts to the work of our great corporations, which call for the most elaborate cooperation. [...] It is hoped, however, that it will be clear to other readers that the same principles can be applied with equal force to all social activities: to the management of our homes; the management of our farms; the management of the business of our tradesmen, large and small; of our churches, our philanthropic institutions our universities, and our governmental departments.

The claim to scientific authority, stated as the book's purpose, and stretched to all social life. A method that admits no frame but its own fits C9.8's last clause, but the totalizing thing is a *method*, not an organization.

### taylor.03 · ch. I — the foundation is "the firm conviction" that interests are one

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against: values stated, not denied)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 147–149, 171–179
- **Checked:** text file

> The principal object of management should be to secure the maximum prosperity for the employer, coupled with the maximum prosperity for each employee. [...] The majority of these men believe that the fundamental interests of employees and employers are necessarily antagonistic. Scientific management, on the contrary, has for its very foundation the firm conviction that the true interests of the two are one and the same; that prosperity for the employer cannot exist through a long term of years unless it is accompanied by prosperity for the employee, and vice versa; and that it is possible to give the workman what he most wants--high wages--and the employer what he wants--a low labor cost--for his manufactures.

Taylor does not hide a belief behind science: he names it a "conviction" and puts it at the foundation. The belief (harmony of interests) is contestable, which is exactly why it matters that he states it.

### taylor.04 · ch. I — "As engineers and managers, we are ... best fitted to lead"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: in-group / out-group)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 373–380
- **Checked:** text file

> As engineers and managers, we are more intimately acquainted with these facts than any other class in the community, and are therefore best fitted to lead in a movement to combat this fallacious idea by educating not only the workmen but the whole of the country as to the true facts. And yet we are practically doing nothing in this direction, and are leaving this field entirely in the hands of the labor agitators (many of whom are misinformed and misguided), and of sentimentalists who are ignorant as to actual working conditions.

A class ("we," engineers and managers) claims the facts and the right to educate the country; the out-group is agitators and sentimentalists. This supports the tribal part of C9.6, but openly, in the first person.

### taylor.05 · ch. I, ch. II — "this one best method," best meaning quickest

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: a value built into the "science"), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 554–563. ch. II; lines 2962–2967
- **Checked:** text file. The phrase "one best way" is not in this text; Taylor writes "one best method" (lines 559, 2816).

> Now, among the various methods and implements used in each element of each trade there is always one method and one implement which is quicker and better than any of the rest. And this one best method and best implement can only be discovered or developed through a scientific study and analysis of all of the methods and implements in use, together with accurate, minute, motion and time study. This involves the gradual substitution of science for rule of thumb throughout the mechanic arts. [...] This one new method, involving that series of motions which can be made quickest and best, is then substituted in place of the ten or fifteen inferior series which were formerly in use. This best method becomes standard, and remains standard, to be taught first to the teachers (or functional foremen) and by them to every workman in the establishment until it is superseded by a quicker and better series of movements.

The strongest textual case for C9.6: "best" is defined by one value, speed ("quicker and better"), and the choice of that value is never argued; it is presented as what study discovers. The disguise word is "science" (C8.7).

### taylor.06 · ch. II — the management takes over the workman's knowledge and judgment

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C9.6 (for)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 833–837, 875–879, 887–890, 3450–3452
- **Checked:** text file

> The managers assume, for instance, the burden of gathering together all of the traditional knowledge which in the past has been possessed by the workmen and then of classifying, tabulating, and reducing this knowledge to rules, laws, and formulae which are immensely helpful to the workmen in doing their daily work. [...] The development of a science, on the other hand, involves the establishment of many rules, laws, and formulae which replace the judgment of the individual workman and which can be effectively used only after having been systematically recorded, indexed, etc. [...] Thus all of the planning which under the old system was done by the workman, as a result of his personal experience, must of necessity under the new system be done by the management in accordance with the laws of the science [...] Third. It was not due to this man's initiative or originality that he did his big day's work, but to the knowledge of the science of pig-iron handling developed and taught him by some one else.

The management's monopoly of knowledge, stated plainly. The source of productive value is moved from the person to the organization's records; that is near C9.8's "from which all value is imagined to flow," but inside the shop only, and the worker's knowledge is collected first.

### taylor.07 · ch. II — Schmidt: "a high-priced man has to do exactly as he's told"; "the type of the ox"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C9.6 (for: out-group contempt)
- **Location:** ch. II (pig-iron illustration); lines 1088–1089, 1098–1099, 1105–1110, 1425–1428, 1432–1435
- **Checked:** text file

> "Now, hold on, hold on. You know just as well as I do that a high-priced man has to do exactly as he's told from morning till night. [...] Now a high-priced man does just what he's told to do, and no back talk. [...] This seems to be rather rough talk. And indeed it would be if applied to an educated mechanic, or even an intelligent laborer. With a man of the mentally sluggish type of Schmidt it is appropriate and not unkind, since it is effective in fixing his attention on the high wages which he wants and away from what, if it were called to his attention, he probably would consider impossibly hard work. [...] Now one of the very first requirements for a man who is fit to handle pig iron as a regular occupation that he shall be so stupid and so phlegmatic that he more nearly resembles in his mental make-up the ox than any other type. [...] He is so stupid that the word "percentage" has no meaning to him, and he must consequently be trained by a man more intelligent than himself into the habit of working in accordance with the laws of this science before he can be successful.

Obedience is renamed as rank: being "a high-priced man" means doing as told (C8.7). Taylor says openly that the talk is meant to steer Schmidt's attention away from how hard the work is. The ranking of men by intelligence is the sharpest out-group in the book.

### taylor.08 · ch. II — Midvale: "working on the side of the management"; trusted because he "happened not to be of working parents"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: in-group; complicates: motive is to end the war)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 1187–1189, 1213–1221, 1252–1255
- **Checked:** text file

> The writer told them plainly that he was now working on the side of the management, and that he proposed to do whatever he could to get a fair day's work out of the lathes. This immediately started a war [...] The writer had two advantages, however, which are not possessed by the ordinary foreman, and these came, curiously enough, from the fact that he was not the son of a working man. First, owing to the fact that he happened not to be of working parents, the owners of the company believed that he had the interest of the works more at heart than the other workmen, and they therefore had more confidence in his word than they did in that of the machinists who were under him. [...] For any right-minded man, however, this success is in no sense a recompense for the bitter relations which he is forced to maintain with all of those around him. Life which is one continuous struggle with other men is hardly worth living.

Taylor names his side and the class basis of the owners' trust. But the stated motive for the whole system is to end this "war" (lines 1264–1267: interests "should become the same, instead of antagonistic"). In-group thinking is the starting point he wants to escape, by his own account.

### taylor.09 · ch. II — "enforced standardization ... and enforced cooperation"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for, but stated openly), C9.6 (against: no disguise here)
- **Location:** ch. II (bricklaying); lines 2037–2044, 2047–2049
- **Checked:** text file. The text's "enforcing-this" is read as "enforcing this".

> No one bricklayer, then, can work much faster than the one next to him. Nor has any one workman the authority to make other men cooperate with him to do faster work. It is only through enforced standardization of methods, enforced adoption of the best implements and working conditions, and enforced cooperation that this faster work can be assured. And the duty of enforcing the adoption of standards and of enforcing this cooperation rests with the management alone. [...] All of those who, after proper teaching, either will not or cannot work in accordance with the new methods and at the higher speed must be discharged by the management.

"Cooperation" is the book's value word for conformity to the standard, but here Taylor says in plain words that it is enforced and that refusers are discharged. The word dresses the conformity elsewhere (taylor.14); this passage undresses it.

### taylor.10 · ch. II — what pay is good for a man, "demonstrated" by experiment

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: value presented as finding)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 1817–1828, 3461–3465
- **Checked:** text file

> A long series of experiments, coupled with close observation, had demonstrated the fact that when workmen of this caliber are given a carefully measured task, which calls for a big day's work on their part, and that when in return for this extra effort they are paid wages up to 60 per cent beyond the wages usually paid, that this increase in wages tends to make them not only more thrifty but better men in every way; that they live rather better, begin to save money, become more sober, and work more steadily. When, on the other hand, they receive much more than a 60 per cent increase in wages, many of them will work irregularly and tend to become more or less shiftless, extravagant, and dissipated. Our experiments showed, in other words, that it does not do for most men to get rich too fast. [...] Fifth. As is explained (page 74), the 60 per cent increase in pay which he received was not the result of an arbitrary judgment of a foreman or superintendent, it was the result of a long series of careful experiments impartially made to determine what compensation is really for the man's true and best interest when all things are considered.

The closest Taylor comes to C9.6: a moral judgment (thrift and sobriety are better; too much money corrupts) and a wage decision are presented as the output of impartial experiment. The values are visible; what is denied is that they are *values* rather than findings.

### taylor.11 · ch. II — "justice for all three parties through impartial scientific investigation"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: objectivity claimed for a value question; against: justice named openly)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 3489–3499, 3586–3588
- **Checked:** text file

> And the means which the writer firmly believes will be adopted to bring about, first, efficiency both in employer and employs and then an equitable division of the profits of their joint efforts will be scientific management, which has for its sole aim the attainment of justice for all three parties through impartial scientific investigation of all the elements of the problem. For a time both sides will rebel against this advance. The workers will resent any interference with their old rule-of-thumb methods, and the management will resent being asked to take on new duties and burdens; but in the end the people through enlightened public opinion will force the new order of things upon both employer and employee. [...] What constitutes a fair day's work will be a question for scientific investigation, instead of a subject to be bargained and haggled over.

Justice is the stated aim, and Taylor says he "firmly believes." The move C9.6 is after is real here: a question of fairness is taken out of bargaining and handed to investigation. Not denial of value, but removal of value questions from the parties into expertise.

### taylor.12 · ch. II — the "wooden man" objection, and Taylor's answer

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 3137–3143, 3182–3187, 3201–3204
- **Checked:** text file

> Now, when through all of this teaching and this minute instruction the work is apparently made so smooth and easy for the workman, the first impression is that this all tends to make him a mere automaton, a wooden man. As the workmen frequently say when they first come under this system, "Why, I am not allowed to think or move without some one interfering or doing it for me!" The same criticism and objection, however, can be raised against all other modern subdivision of labor. [...] What really happens is that, with the aid of the science which is invariably developed, and through the instructions from his teachers, each workman of a given intellectual capacity is enabled to do a much higher, more interesting, and finally more developing and more profitable kind of work than he was before able to do. [...] It is true that with scientific management the workman is not allowed to use whatever implements and methods he sees fit in the daily practice of his work.

Taylor records the workers' own complaint and answers it with the surgeon analogy (lines 3143–3168). He claims the system develops the person; he does not claim the person is nothing. The subordination is admitted ("not allowed"), not hidden.

### taylor.13 · ch. II — "The rights of the people are therefore greater than those of either employer or employee"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 3406–3415
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not fair, however, to form any final judgment until all of the elements in the case have been considered. At the first glance we see only two parties to the transaction, the workmen and their employers. We overlook the third great party, the whole people,--the consumers, who buy the product of the first two and who ultimately pay both the wages of the workmen and the profits of the employers. The rights of the people are therefore greater than those of either employer or employee. And this third great party should be given its proper share of any gain.

The founding text of management puts the source of right outside the firm, in the public. The organization is not prior to its environment here; it answers to it.

### taylor.14 · ch. II — "a complete revolution in the mental attitude"; "Cooperation, not individualism"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C9.6 (against: a creed, stated as one), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 3286–3290, 3517–3534
- **Checked:** text file. The phrase "mental revolution" is not in this book (it comes from Taylor's 1912 testimony to Congress, not in our texts); the book says "a complete revolution in the mental attitude".

> On the other hand, the really great problem involved in a change from the management of "initiative and incentive" to scientific management consists in a complete revolution in the mental attitude and the habits of all of those engaged in the management, as well of the workmen. [...] It is no single element, but rather this whole combination, that constitutes scientific management, which may be summarized as: Science, not rule of thumb. Harmony, not discord. Cooperation, not individualism. Maximum output, in place of restricted output. The development of each man to his greatest efficiency and prosperity. The writer wishes to again state that: "The time is fast going by for the great personal or individual achievement of any one man standing alone and without the help of those around him. And the time is coming when all great things will be done by that type of cooperation in which each man performs the function for which he is best suited, each man preserves his own individuality and is supreme in his particular function, and each man at the same time loses none of his originality and proper personal initiative, and yet is controlled by and must work harmoniously with many other men."

A credo in five lines, with a conversion of mind as its condition: these are beliefs, announced. "Cooperation, not individualism" is the value word; "controlled by" is the substance, stated in the same sentence as "preserves his own individuality." Anti-individualist in so many words, which cuts against reading Taylorism as a carrier of the Self.

## Passages: Veblen

### veblen-engineers.01 · ch. III, p. 52 — the industrial system "of an impersonal nature, after the fashion of the material sciences"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: objectivity claimed), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III "The Captains of Finance and the Engineers," p. 52; lines 1726–1731, 1737–1744
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> In more than one respect the industrial system of today is notably different from anything that has gone before. It is eminently a system, self-balanced and comprehensive; and it is a system of interlocking mechanical processes, rather than of skilful manipulation. [...] It is of an impersonal nature, after the fashion of the material sciences, on which it constantly draws. It runs to "quantity production" of specialized and standardized goods and services. For all these reasons it lends itself to systematic control under the direction of industrial experts, skilled technologists, who may be called "production engineers," for want of a better term.

The argument for expert rule runs from the system's impersonality. The system is "self-balanced," almost a self, but it is a mechanism, and Veblen never gives it ends of its own.

### veblen-engineers.02 · ch. III, pp. 53–55 — a "free hand ... regardless of any national pretensions or any vested interests"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: the expert as without bias)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 53–55; lines 1779–1790, 1822–1826
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> And for the due working of this inclusive going concern it is essential that that corps of [p. 54] technological specialists who by training, insight, and interest make up the general staff of industry must have a free hand in the disposal of its available resources, in materials, equipment, and man power, regardless of any national pretensions or any vested interests. [...] Politics and investment are still allowed to decide matters of industrial policy which should plainly be left to the discretion of the general staff of production engineers driven by no commercial bias.

The technocratic claim at its plainest: policy taken from politics and given to experts with "no commercial bias." But note "by training, insight, and interest": Veblen grants the engineers an interest of their own.

### veblen-engineers.03 · ch. III, pp. 68–69 — a "joint stock of knowledge"; the engineers "alone are competent"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against), C9.6 (for: exclusive competence)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 68–69; lines 2250–2254, 2268–2283
- **Checked:** text file (OCR). "industrial -arts" read as "industrial arts".

> The technology — the state of the industrial arts — which takes effect in this mechanical industry is in an eminent sense a joint stock of knowledge and experience held in common by the civilized peoples. [...] These expert men, technologists, engineers, or whatever name may best suit them, make up the indispensable General Staff of the industrial system; and without their immediate and unremitting guidance and correction the industrial system will not work. [...] The material welfare of the community is unreservedly bound up with the due working of this industrial system, and therefore with its unreserved control by the engineers, who alone are competent to manage it.

The source of value is the community's common knowledge (the experts too are "born, bred, and trained at the cost of the community," lines 2264–2265), and the end is "material welfare." That is the reverse of C9.8. What it does claim is that only one class is competent to run it.

### veblen-engineers.04 · ch. III, p. 71 — "uneasily 'class-conscious'"; "damage to the common good"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: in-group; against: values named)
- **Location:** ch. III, p. 71; lines 2329–2343
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> Right lately these technologists have begun to become uneasily "class-conscious" and to reflect that they together constitute the indispensable General Staff of the industrial system. Their class consciousness has taken the immediate form of a growing sense of waste and confusion in the management of industry by the financial agents of the absentee owners. They are beginning to take stock of that all-pervading mismanagement of industry that is inseparable from its control for commercial ends. All of which brings home a realization of their own shame and of damage to the common good. So the engineers are beginning to draw together and ask themselves, "What about it?"

The in-group is named outright, in the Marxist word. Its content is moral: shame and the common good. Tribal, yes; value-denying, no.

### veblen-engineers.05 · ch. III, p. 72 — Taylor's "efficiency engineers" counted on the commercial side

- **Claims:** C9.1 (complicates), C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III, p. 72; lines 2370–2379
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> The case of the efficiency engineers, or scientific-management experts, is somewhat similar. They too have set out to appraise, exhibit, and correct the commercial short-comings of the ordinary management of those industrial establishments which they investigate, to persuade the business men in charge how they may reasonably come in for larger net earnings by a more closely shorn exploitation of the industrial forces at their disposal.

Veblen classes Taylorism with the business interest, not with the technicians. The two founding texts of American technocracy do not form one group; any §9 account of technocrats has to say which ones.

### veblen-engineers.06 · ch. III, p. 75 — "nothing is quite real that cannot be stated in terms of tangible performance"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates: nearest thing to "resent belief")
- **Location:** ch. III, p. 75; lines 2455–2461
- **Checked:** text file (OCR). OCR "ac£ordingly_coming to. understand _that" read as "accordingly coming to understand that"; "o f" as "of".

> So also, to these men who are trained in the stubborn logic of technology, nothing is quite real that cannot be stated in terms of tangible performance; and they are accordingly coming to understand that the whole fabric of credit and corporation finance is a tissue of make-believe.

A positivist criterion of the real, turned against legal and financial belief ("make-believe"). This is the passage closest to SPEC's nihilist; but it is aimed at the owners' institutions, and Veblen attributes it to training, not to disgust or resentment.

### veblen-engineers.07 · ch. III, pp. 80–81 — "the sense of workmanship" and "the rule of Live and Let Live"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against: not nihilists)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 80–81; lines 2629–2634, 2639–2641, 2647–2650
- **Checked:** text file (OCR). The page break falls inside "ob-structive".

> To these men, soberly trained in a spirit of tangible performance and endowed with something more than an even share of the sense of workmanship, and endowed also with the common heritage of partiality for the rule of Live and Let Live, the disallowance of an outworn and ob-[p. 81]structive right of absentee ownership is not likely to seem a shocking infraction of the sacred realties. [...] For all the purposes of that scheme of tangible performance that goes to make up the technologist's world, it is without form and void.

Veblen's engineers hold values (workmanship, live and let live) and use them to judge ownership. "Sacred realties" is Veblen's pun: what is sacred to others is to them merely property. Disbelief in the owners' creed, not in belief.

### veblen-engineers.08 · ch. V, p. 132 — technology "impersonal and dispassionate, and its end is very simply to serve human needs"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: impersonality claimed), C9.8 (against: system serves needs)
- **Location:** ch. V "On the Circumstances which Make for a Change," pp. 132–133; lines 4232–4237, 4245–4248, 4256–4259
- **Checked:** text file (OCR). "a massive body by technological knowledge" is in the text (for "of").

> in spite of obstructive national frontiers and patriotic animosities; for the mechanical technology is impersonal and dispassionate, and its end is very simply to serve human needs, without fear or favor or respect of persons, prerogatives, or politics. [...] The foundation and driving force of it all is a massive body by technological knowledge, of a highly impersonal and altogether unbusinesslike nature [...] Any defect or hindrance in its technical administration, any intrusion of nontechnical considerations, any failure or obstruction at any point

Both halves of the test in one sentence: the claim to impersonality (for C9.6), and an end openly named, "to serve human needs" (against C9.8 and against value-denial). Non-technical considerations are cast as "intrusion": the closest Veblen comes to ruling values out of the question.

### veblen-engineers.09 · ch. V, pp. 135, 137 — "a harmless and docile sort"; "a 'safe and sane' lot"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against, for 1919: not a ruling class), C9.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V, pp. 135, 137; lines 4338–4343, 4409–4415
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> By settled habit the technicians, the engineers and industrial experts, are a harmless and docile sort, well fed on the whole, and somewhat placidly content with the "full dinner-pail" which the lieutenants of the Vested Interests habitually allow them. [...] The technicians are a "safe and sane" lot, on the whole; and they are pretty well commercialized, particularly the older generation, who speak with authority and conviction, and to whom the younger generation of engineers defer, on the whole, with such a degree of filial piety as should go far to reassure all good citizens.

Veblen's own verdict on the engineers of his day: employees, not rulers. Whatever technocracy later became, its founding portrait is of a subordinate class with no will to power.

### veblen-engineers.10 · ch. VI, pp. 146–148 — business-trained men excluded; "well-intentioned deaf-mute blind men"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: out-group exclusion)
- **Location:** ch. VI "A Memorandum on a Practicable Soviet of Technicians," pp. 146–148; lines 4682–4687, 4693–4696, 4733–4741
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> To avoid persistent confusion and prospective defeat, it will be necessary to exclude from all positions of trust and executive responsibility all persons who have been trained for business or who have had experience in business undertakings of the larger sort. [...] What is wanted is training in the ways and means of productive industry, not in the ways and means of salesmanship and profitable investment. [...] For the purposes of this projected new order of production, therefore, the experienced and capable business men are at the [p. 148] best to be rated as well-intentioned deaf-mute blind men.

A whole class barred from trust by its training, and called well-meaning but blind. The out-group is explicit and justified openly by an argument about bias (lines 4698–4703), not hidden.

### veblen-engineers.11 · ch. VI, pp. 156–157, 163 — corporate ownership is absentee ownership; the technicians speak for the system

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C9.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VI, pp. 156–157, 163; lines 5009–5013, 5030–5034, 5234–5241
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> By absentee ownership, as the term applies in this connection, is here to be understood the ownership of an industrially useful article by any person or persons who are not habitually employed in the industrial use of it. [...] Collective ownership, of the corporate form, that is to say ownership by a collectivity instituted ad hoc, also falls away as being unavoidably absentee ownership, within the meaning of the term. [...] In this two-cleft, or bi-cameral, administration of industry, the technicians may be said to represent the community at large in its industrial capacity, or in other words the industrial system as a going concern; whereas the business men speak for the commercial interest of the absentee owners, as a body which holds the industrial community in usufruct.

The founding technocrat would cancel corporate ownership. C9.8 pairs technocrats with corporatism; Veblen sets them against each other. The "going concern" the technicians speak for is the community "in its industrial capacity," not the firm.

### veblen-engineers.12 · ch. VI, pp. 138, 161–162 — "in an objective way"; "no intention here to argue the moral merits"; "the idol of every true American heart"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates: value-neutrality professed, ironically), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VI, pp. 138, 161–162; lines 4431–4437, 5156–5173, 5180–5182, 5208–5212
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> It is the purpose of this memorandum to show, in an objective way, that under existing circumstances there need be no fear, and no hope, of an effectual revolutionary overturn in America, such as would unsettle the established order and unseat those Vested Interests that now control the country's industrial system. [...] There is no intention here to argue the moral merits of this contemplated disallowance of absentee ownership; or to argue for or against such a move, on moral or other grounds. Absentee ownership is legally sound today. [...] There is, in fact, not much more to be said about it; except that, in the last resort, the economic moralities wait on the economic necessities. The economic-moral sense of the American community today runs unequivocally to the effect that absentee ownership is fundamentally and eternally right and good; and it should seem reasonable to believe that it will continue to run to that effect for some time yet. [...] apprehensive persons should not lose sight of the main fact that absentee ownership after all is the idol of every true American heart. [...] The most tenacious factor in any civilization is a settled popular frame of mind, and to this abiding American frame of mind absentee ownership is the controlling center of all the economic realities.

Here the form of C9.6 appears: an objective stance, an explicit refusal to argue morals, and a claim that morals follow necessity. But it is irony; the same pages call ownership "something for nothing" (line 5194) and "make-believe." Veblen's disclaimer hides nothing from the reader. For C8.7, he names the business class's value word: absentee ownership as idol, "right and good."

### veblen-engineers.13 · ch. VI, p. 166 — "a self-selected, but inclusive, Soviet of technicians"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for: self-selected expert rule), C9.8 (against: bounded by the arts and the population)
- **Location:** ch. VI, p. 166; lines 5319–5332
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> In short, so far as regards the technical requirements of the case, the situation is ready for a self-selected, but inclusive, Soviet of technicians to take over the economic affairs of the country and to allow and disallow what they may agree on; provided always that they live within the requirements of that state of the industrial arts whose keepers they are, and provided that their pretensions continue to have the support of the industrial rank and file; which comes near saying that their Soviet must consistently and effectually take care of the material welfare of the underlying population.

The plan for rule by a self-chosen in-group. But it is doubly bounded: by the state of the arts, of which they are only "keepers," and by the consent of the rank and file. Not a body prior to its environment.
