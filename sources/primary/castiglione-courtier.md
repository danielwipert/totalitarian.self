# Castiglione, *The Book of the Courtier* (1528)

- **Edition used:** trans. Leonard Eckstein Opdycke (1901), Gutenberg #67799
- **Text file:** `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (clean)
- **Citation form:** Book.section, using Opdycke's numbered sections (e.g. I.26). These match the standard Italian division (Cian, Maier); verify before final citation.
- **Claims to test:** C4.21, C4.25, C8.1, C8.2, C8.4; also C4.1, C4.30, C4.31
- **Read:** Book I.1, I.14–I.28, I.40–I.42 (birth, grace, nonchalance); Book II.7–II.36 and II.41 (self-presentation, the prince, dress, reputation); Book IV.4–IV.13, IV.23, IV.46–IV.47, IV.65–IV.72 (the courtier's end; Bembo's ascent). Book III (the court lady) not read.

**Opdycke's word for *sprezzatura*** is **"nonchalance"** (I.26, line 1919, where the Count offers it "to use possibly a new word"). Opdycke's note 73 (line 14387) gives the Italian and says Castiglione "may, however, have been among the first to use it [...] in its modified sense of unconcern or nonchalance." Opdycke also uses "nonchalant ease" (line 1946) and "nonchalant simplicity" (line 2715). The other key word, *grazia*, is "grace" throughout.

**Dialogue form.** These are speakers' positions, not Castiglione's in his own voice: Count Ludovico da Canossa (Book I), messer Federico Fregoso (Book II), my lord Ottaviano Fregoso (Book IV.4–47), Pietro Bembo (IV.50–71). Objections are built in (Pietro da Napoli: "a noble flatterer"; Calmeta: princes favour only the presumptuous; my lady Emilia's tug at Bembo's robe). They are recorded below where they bear on a claim.

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

Castiglione gives the rival view its best text, and the essay's §8 its oldest precursor so far. But the self he fashions is bounded at every level.

- **Self as craft and performance (C4.25): for, with limits.** The courtier is made: by imitating masters until he can "transform himself into his master" and by stealing grace "from all who seem to possess it" (.04). He is made *for the eyes of others*: deeds done "before the very eyes of his king" (.10), dress chosen for "how he wishes to seem" (.13), every act adjusted to "in whose presence" (.09). The method is to hide the making: "that to be true art which does not appear to be art" (.05). This is the most explicit text yet for a self fashioned as performance.
- **But the material is given, not self-originated.** Grace is first "the gift of nature and of heaven"; the courtier is "nobly born" and "endowed by nature" (.02). Study can only "polish and amend." He must "understand himself and his own powers" and not imitate what does not suit his nature (.11). He is to be "an honest and upright man" underneath (.08), and his whole life must be "accordant with his own self" (.09). The craft works on a nature; it does not create one.
- **Bounded by the prince, by honour, by God (C4.21, C4.31, C4.30: against).** He must "mould his wishes, habits and all his ways to his prince's liking" (.11). He may refuse only "dishonourable things," and even that limit is qualified at once (.12). Book IV turns the whole art into a means. The courtier is good "not however simply and in himself, but in respect to the end to which he may be directed," which is to tell the prince the truth and lead him to virtue (.15). The prince in turn is God's steward, answerable to "render Him an account" (.16), and that is answerability inside a humanist text (C4.1, for). Bembo's ascent turns *inward* ("let him have recourse to himself") only to leave the self behind. The prayer asks that "we, being severed from ourselves," may "transform ourselves into the beloved," as the fathers' souls were made to "unite with God" (.17, .18). Nothing here is valued "for his own excellence" alone, and nothing is its own origin.
- **§8 (C8.1, C8.2, C8.4).** This is the strongest pressure yet on §8's originality, and it should be stated plainly. In 1528 Castiglione already describes:
  1. a social standard formed by **imitation**, copied piece by piece from admired models (.04);
  2. performed as **natural ease** that hides the copying (.05, .07);
  3. a **failed copy**, where the sign of ease is imitated without what it signified and turns into "affectation" (.06, Roberto's dropped cloak);
  4. **opinion that converges** on the prince's favour, so that "the whole court will seem to admire" a dolt (.14);
  5. **fashion set by use, not reason** (.01, .13).

  What Castiglione does *not* have is the **idiom of self-expression**. The performance is never said to express an inner self. The inner self (goodness) is kept apart from the display (.08), and the display's stated end is favour, then virtue, then God. His idiom is *nature* and *grace* (seeming natural), which is the same idiom C8.2 finds in Wilde (conformity "passed off as 'natural'"). So C8.2 as revised survives: the mechanism is old (now 1528, not 1859), and the idiom of self-expression is still the new part. But C8.1's process, and C8.4's claim that the engine is the wish to belong, both face a precursor. Castiglione's engine is the wish for **favour and esteem at a court**, which is belonging and distinction at once ("to excel the rest", .10).

**Verdict in one line:** Castiglione makes the self a craft and a performance for others' eyes (C4.25 for), but the craft works on a nature given by birth and heaven, serves the prince, and is finally subordinated to virtue and to God (C4.21 against as worded). He is a strong precursor of §8's *mechanism* but not of its *idiom*.

## Passages

### castiglione.01 · I.1 — "use rather than reason has power to introduce new things"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.1 (dedication to Alfonso Ariosto); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 767–771, 778–785
- **Checked:** text file

> You ask me then to write what is to my thinking the form of Courtiership most befitting a gentleman who lives at the court of princes, by which he may have the ability and knowledge perfectly to serve them in every reasonable thing, winning from them favour, and praise from other men

> For custom often makes the same thing pleasing and displeasing to us; whence it sometimes follows that customs, habits, ceremonies and fashions that once were prized, become vulgar, and contrariwise the vulgar become prized. Thus it is clearly seen that use rather than reason has power to introduce new things among us, and to do away with the old; and he will often err who seeks to determine which are perfect.

The book's purpose is stated in the first section: a "form" of the person that serves princes and wins "favour, and praise from other men." The self is fashioned for others from the start. The second block describes fashion as movement of custom without reason, a stage on which §8's "convergence" can happen. Castiglione treats it as the medium he works in, not as a corruption.

### castiglione.02 · I.14 — grace "the gift of nature and of heaven"; study can only "polish and amend"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (complicates), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** I.14 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 1411–1414, 1439–1449; see also I.24, lines 1830–1837
- **Checked:** text file

> It is true that, by favour of the stars or of nature, some men are endowed at birth with such graces that they seem not to have been born, but rather as if some god had formed them with his very hands and adorned them with every excellence of mind and body.

> I say that there is a middle state between perfect grace on the one hand and senseless folly on the other; and those who are not thus perfectly endowed by nature, with study and toil can in great part polish and amend their natural defects. Besides his noble birth, then, I would have the Courtier favoured in this regard also, and endowed by nature not only with talent and beauty of person and feature, but with a certain grace and (as we say) air that shall make him at first sight pleasing and agreeable to all who see him

Self-fashioning starts from given material: birth, nature, "favour of the stars," "some god." Craft only amends it. This is against a self-originating self, and it bounds C4.25. Gaspar Pallavicino objects (I.15) that nobility is "the merit of our predecessors than our own." The case against inherited worth is put inside the book, and the Count keeps birth anyway, on grounds of "universal opinion" (I.16).

### castiglione.03 · I.18 — the art of self-praise: things "let fall so naturally"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.18 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 1608–1613
- **Checked:** text file

> Among the ancient authors there is no lack of those who have taught it; but to my thinking, the whole art consists in saying things in such a way that they shall not seem to be said to that end, but let fall so naturally that it was impossible not to say them, and while seeming always to avoid self-praise, yet to achieve it

The self is advertised by concealing the advertisement. A calculated display is made to seem involuntary ("impossible not to say them"). This is §8's structure, a performance passed off as spontaneity, in 1528. The idiom is "naturally," not "self-expression."

### castiglione.04 · I.26 — "transform himself into his master"; "steal this grace"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates), C4.21 (against), C4.25 (for)
- **Location:** I.26 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 1895–1904
- **Checked:** text file

> Therefore he who wishes to be a good pupil, besides performing his tasks well, must put forth every effort to resemble his master, and, if it were possible, to transform himself into his master. And when he feels that he has made some progress, it will be very profitable to observe different men of the same calling, and governing himself with that good judgment which must ever be his guide, to go about selecting now this thing from one and that thing from another. And as the bee in the green meadows is ever wont to rob the flowers among the grass, so our Courtier must steal this grace from all who seem to possess it, taking from each that part which shall most be worthy praise

The method of self-fashioning is imitation. Selective, guided by "good judgment," but imitation all the same. This is the clearest early text for §8's "attachment" stage: one copies the admired, piece by piece, in order to be admired. It is against C4.21's self "valued for his own excellence," since the excellence is assembled from others'.

### castiglione.05 · I.26 — "nonchalance"; "true art which does not appear to be art"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (for), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.26 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 1913–1936
- **Checked:** text file

> I find one universal rule concerning it, which seems to me worth more in this matter than any other in all things human that are done or said: and that is to avoid affectation to the uttermost and as it were a very sharp and dangerous rock; and, to use possibly a new word, to practise in everything a certain nonchalance that shall conceal design and show that what is done and said is done without effort and almost without thought. [...] Accordingly we may affirm that to be true art which does not appear to be art; nor to anything must we give greater care than to conceal art, for if it is discovered, it quite destroys our credit and brings us into small esteem. And I remember having once read that there were several very excellent orators of antiquity, who among their other devices strove to make everyone believe that they had no knowledge of letters; and hiding their knowledge they pretended that their orations were composed very simply and as if springing rather from nature and truth than from study and art

This is the founding text of art that hides art (his words: "to conceal art"). The learned performance is presented "as if springing rather from nature and truth." It supports C8.2 as revised: the mechanism (artifice passed off as nature) is old. The idiom is nature, not the self. Castiglione says openly what §8 says is hidden from the performer: it is a technique, and the courtier knows it.

### castiglione.06 · I.27 — Roberto's nonchalance "is really affectation"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.27 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 1964–1971 (Bibbiena's description at lines 1953–1960)
- **Checked:** text file

> Do you not perceive that what you call nonchalance in messer Roberto, is really affectation? For it is clearly seen that he is striving with all his might to seem to be taking no thought, and this is taking too much thought; and since it passes the true limits of moderation, his nonchalance is affected and unbecoming; and it is a thing that works precisely the reverse of the effect intended, that is the concealment of art.

Roberto lets his cloak and slippers fall while dancing to *show* that he is not thinking about it. The sign of ease has been copied without the ease. This is close to §8's "perversion" and "accessory" stages: the outward mark kept, the thing it marked gone, worn to be seen. Castiglione already names and mocks it. §8 needs to acknowledge this precursor.

### castiglione.07 · I.28, I.40 — nonchalance makes bystanders think he "knows much more than he does"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** I.28, lines 2021–2028; I.40, lines 2715–2716; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (the Count)
- **Checked:** text file

> Thus this excellence, which is the opposite of affectation and which for the present we call nonchalance, besides being the true fountain from which grace springs, carries with it another ornament, which, in accompanying any human action whatever and however trifling it be, not only at once reveals the knowledge of him who performs it, but often leads us to rate his knowledge as much greater than in fact it is; because it impresses upon the minds of the bystanders the idea that he who does well so easily, knows much more than he does

> This is that nonchalant simplicity most pleasing to the eyes and minds of men, who are ever fearful of being deceived by art.

The audience is the point of nonchalance. It works on "the minds of the bystanders," and it works *because* they fear being "deceived by art." So the performance of naturalness feeds on a demand for authenticity. That is an early form of the dynamic §8 attributes to the self-expression idiom, here in the idiom of "simplicity."

### castiglione.08 · I.41 — "an honest and upright man"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.41 (the Count); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 2747–2753
- **Checked:** text file

> we will in a few words declare it to be enough that he be (as we say) an honest and upright man; for in this are included prudence, goodness, strength and temperance of mind, and all the other qualities that are proper to a name so honoured. And I esteem him alone to be a true moral philosopher, who wishes to be good; and in this regard he needs few other precepts than that wish.

Under the performance there is supposed to be a moral core, but it gets four sentences against dozens of pages on display. The core is classical virtue, not answerability before God. That makes it a second, humanist root of the bounded self, as Montaigne's Ciceronian root was (C4.13), and it complicates C4.1's single Reformation origin.

### castiglione.09 · II.7 — "accordant with his own self"; "in whose presence"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.7 (Federico); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 3732–3740, 3764–3770
- **Checked:** text file

> And let him not only take care that his separate parts and qualities are excellent, but let him order the tenour of his life in such fashion, that the whole may be in keeping with these parts and be seen to be always and in everything accordant with his own self and form one single body of all these good qualities; so that his every act may be the result and compound of all his faculties, as the Stoics say is the duty of him who is wise.

> Next let him consider well what thing it is that he is doing or saying, the place where he is doing it, in whose presence, the cause that impels him, his age, his profession, the object he has in view, and the means that may conduce thereto

There is a self here, "his own self," a unity of faculties, and it is a Stoic ideal of consistency. But it must "be seen" to be consistent, and the second rule makes every act depend on audience and occasion. Morello replies (II.8) that he heard these rules from the friars at confession as "the circumstances," which is a reminder of where the vocabulary came from.

### castiglione.10 · II.8, II.11 — "before the very eyes of his king"; masks let a man "choose a part"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.8, lines 3786–3797; II.11, lines 3915–3921; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (Federico)
- **Checked:** text file

> he ought to arrange discreetly to withdraw from the crowd, and to perform those glorious and brave deeds that he has to do, with as little company as he can, and in sight of all the noblest and most respected men in the army, and especially in the presence and (if it is possible) before the very eyes of his king or of the prince whom he serves; for in truth it is very proper to make the most of one’s good deeds.

> Indeed there is no better way of displaying oneself in such matters at public sports, either armed or unarmed; because disguise carries with it a certain freedom and licence, which among other things enable a man to choose a part for which he feels himself qualified, and to use care and elaboration upon the chief point of the thing wherein he would display himself, and a certain nonchalance as to that which does not count,—which greatly enhances the charm

Even courage is staged for the prince's eye. The mask is praised because it lets a man "choose a part" and display himself better, so a role gives more freedom of display than one's own face. The prince in a mask may "excel the rest not by authority but by merit" (lines 3944–3945). The engine is distinction as well as belonging, which complicates C8.4's contrast between the two.

### castiglione.11 · II.18, II.20 — "mould his wishes, habits and all his ways to his prince's liking"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C4.31 (against), C8.2 (complicates), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** II.18, lines 4188–4192, 4196–4197, 4207–4212; II.20, lines 4349–4359; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (Federico; objection by Pietro da Napoli)
- **Checked:** text file

> I would have the Courtier strive, with all the thoughts and forces of his mind, to love and almost to adore the prince whom he serves, above every other thing, and mould his wishes, habits and all his ways to his prince’s liking.

> We already have enough Courtiers of this kind, for methinks you have in a few words described for us a noble flatterer.

> And I would have the Courtier bend himself to this even if he be by nature alien to it, so that on seeing him his lord shall always feel that he will have something agreeable to say; which will come about if he has the good judgment to perceive what his prince likes, and the wit and prudence to bend himself thereto, and a deliberate purpose to like that which perhaps he by nature dislikes.

> And when we see another man who is pleasing to a prince for any reason, we must not think to reach the same height ourselves by imitating him, for all things are not proper to all men. [...] Hence every man must understand himself and his own powers, and govern himself accordingly, and consider what things he ought to imitate, and what things he ought not.

Self-fashioning here is conformity to another's taste, down to "a deliberate purpose to like that which perhaps he by nature dislikes." That is conformity performed as genuine liking, and it is the nearest thing in these texts to §8's mechanism. Federico limits it to wishes that are "reasonable and right" or indifferent. The last block sets a limit from the other side: one's own nature restricts what one may imitate. The self is bounded by the prince above and by nature below.

### castiglione.12 · II.22–23 — "In dishonourable things we are not bound to obey any man"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.22, lines 4430–4433; II.23, lines 4458–4459, 4466–4476; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (Federico)
- **Checked:** text file

> But if our Courtier should chance to find himself in the service of one who is vicious and malign, let him depart as soon as he discovers it, lest he suffer that keen anguish which all good men feel who serve the wicked.

> In dishonourable things we are not bound to obey any man,

> if he were to command you to commit an act of treachery, not only would you not be bound to do it, but you would be bound not to do it,—both for your own sake and for the sake of not being a minister to your lord’s disgrace. True it is that many things which are evil seem at first sight good, and many seem evil and yet are good. Hence in our lords’ service it is sometimes permitted to kill not one man but ten thousand, and to do many other things that would seem evil to a man who did not rightly consider them, and yet are not evil.

There is a limit on service, but it is honour, not conscience before God, and Federico qualifies it at once ("ten thousand") and then declines to say how to tell real good from apparent good (lines 4483–4485). The bound is real and thin. It is the code of a class, not an individual's own court.

### castiglione.13 · II.27–28 — "how he wishes to seem"; dress as index of "the things within"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C8.2 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.27 (Federico; the Duchess), lines 4626–4627, 4642–4645, 4659–4663; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> for things external often bear witness to the things within.

> if all men go about like that, we must not cast it at them as a fault, since this attire is as fitting and proper to them as it is for the Venetians to wear puffed sleeves, or for the Florentines to wear the hood.

> he ought to consider how he wishes to seem and of what sort he wishes to be esteemed, and to dress accordingly and contrive that his attire shall aid him to be so regarded even by those who neither hear him speak nor witness any act of his.

Dress is chosen for "how he wishes to seem." This is close to the modern idea of dress as a statement of the self, but its language is *esteem* and *regard*, not expression. The Duchess's quip makes local fashion a matter of what "all men" do. Being "fitting and proper" means conforming to the group, which is the root of the accessory.

### castiglione.14 · I.16, II.32 — opinion follows the prince's favour

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** I.16 (the Count), lines 1512–1514; II.32 (Federico), lines 4858–4870; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> And often too they are themselves deceived; but since they always have a host of imitators, their favour begets very great fame, which chiefly guides our judgments

> And on the other hand, if the prince shows favour to a very dolt, who knows neither how to speak nor how to act,—his manners and ways (however silly and uncouth they be) will often be praised by everyone with exclamations and astonishment, and the whole court will seem to admire and respect him, and everyone will appear to laugh at his jests and at certain rustic and stupid jokes that ought to excite rather disgust than laughter: to such degree are men firm and fixed in the opinions that are engendered by the favour and disfavour of lords.

> Therefore I would have our Courtier set off his worth as best he can, with cleverness and skill, and whenever he has to go where he is strange and unknown, let him take care that good opinion of him precedes him

This is convergence of judgment through imitation of a centre, a "False Group" admiring what "ought to excite rather disgust," described in 1528. The engine is outside the self (the prince's favour, a "host of imitators"). The courtier's answer is to manage his reputation, not to resist it.

### castiglione.15 · IV.4–5, IV.10 — good "not however simply and in himself, but in respect to the end"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (against), C4.25 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.4, lines 10467–10474; IV.5, lines 10493–10498, 10509–10512; IV.10, lines 10645–10651; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` (Ottaviano)
- **Checked:** text file

> Hence I think that the perfect Courtier, such as Count Ludovico and messer Federico have described, may be a truly good thing and worthy of praise, not however simply and in himself, but in respect to the end to which he may be directed. For indeed if by being nobly born, graceful, agreeable, and expert in so many exercises, the Courtier brought forth no other fruit than merely being what he is, I should not deem it right for a man to devote so much study and pains to acquiring this perfection of Courtiership

> I think then that the aim of the perfect Courtier, which has not been spoken of till now, is so to win for himself, by means of the accomplishments ascribed to him by these gentlemen, the favour and mind of the prince whom he serves, that he may be able to say, and always shall say, the truth about everything which it is fitting for the prince to know, without fear or risk of giving offence thereby [...] Therefore I think that just as music, festivals, games, and the other pleasant accomplishments are as it were the flower, in like manner to lead or help one’s prince towards right, and to frighten him from wrong, are the true fruit of Courtiership.

> playing upon him with salutary craft; like cunning doctors, who often anoint the edge of the cup with a sweet cordial, when they wish to give some bitter-tasting medicine to sick and over-delicate children. If, therefore, the Courtier put the veil of pleasure to such a use, he will reach his aim

The decisive "against" for C4.21. Book IV denies that the fashioned self is valuable "simply and in himself"; without an end beyond itself it would be "follies and vanities" (line 10477). The performance is kept, but now as a "veil" and "salutary craft" serving truth-telling and the prince's virtue. Book IV re-binds the self to service and virtue.

### castiglione.16 · IV.23 — "placed by God under the ward of princes"; the law of reason "graven upon his own mind"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C1.1 (for), C4.30 (against)
- **Location:** IV.23 (Ottaviano); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 11090–11093, 11104–11110
- **Checked:** text file

> Accordingly men have been placed by God under the ward of princes, who for this reason ought to take diligent care of them, in order to render Him an account of them like good stewards to their lord

> Hence if the prince would perform these duties rightly, he must devote every study and diligence to wisdom; then he must set before himself and follow steadfastly in everything the law of reason (unwritten on paper or metal, but graven upon his own mind), to the end that it may be not only familiar to him, but ingrained in him, and abide with him as a part of himself; so that day and night, in every place and time, it may admonish him and speak inwardly to his heart

Answerability to God ("render Him an account") and an inner law that will "admonish him and speak inwardly to his heart" appear here in a humanist courtesy book written "between the years 1508 and 1516" (Opdycke's title note, lines 115–116) and set in March 1507, before Luther's 1517 theses. That supports C4.1's structure but weakens any claim that it is *Reformation* property. The answerable one is the prince as steward, not every person.

### castiglione.17 · IV.68 — "let him have recourse to himself", to find "God's footstep"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.30 (against), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.68 (Bembo); `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt` lines 12768–12788
- **Checked:** text file

> And thus, instead of going outside himself in thought (as all must needs do who choose to contemplate bodily beauty only), let him have recourse to himself, in order to contemplate that beauty which is seen by the eyes of the mind, which begin to be sharp and clear when those of the body lose the flower of their loveliness. Then the soul,—freed from vice, purged by studies of true philosophy, versed in spiritual life, and practised in matters of the intellect, devoted to the contemplation of her own substance,—as if awakened from deepest sleep, opens those eyes which all possess but few use, and sees in herself a ray of that light which is the true image of the angelic beauty communicated to her [...] thinking that she has found God’s footstep, in the contemplation of which she seeks to rest as in her beatific end.

This is a real inward turn, the soul contemplating "her own substance," in the line of Augustine and Petrarch (C4.8, C4.23). But what she finds inside is not herself: it is "a ray of that light" and "God's footstep." The self is a window, and the faculty is one that "all possess." It is common, not singular.

### castiglione.18 · IV.70–71 — "being severed from ourselves"; "unite with God"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (against), C4.25 (against), C4.30 (against)
- **Location:** IV.70 (Bembo's prayer), lines 12887–12896; IV.71 (my lady Emilia), lines 12905–12906; `texts/castiglione-courtier.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> Accept our souls, which are offered thee in sacrifice; burn them in that living flame which consumes all mortal dross, to the end that, being wholly separated from the body, they may unite with divine beauty by a perpetual and very sweet bond, and that we, being severed from ourselves, may, like true lovers, be able to transform ourselves into the beloved, and rising above the earth may be admitted to the angels’ feast, where, fed on ambrosia and immortal nectar, we may at last die a most happy and living death, as died of old those ancient fathers whose souls thou, by the most glowing power of contemplation, didst ravish from the body and unite with God.

> Have a care, messer Pietro, that with these thoughts your soul, also, does not forsake your body.

The book that teaches self-fashioning ends by praying to be "severed from ourselves" and transformed into God. The phrase "transform himself into his master" (.04) returns here as "transform ourselves into the beloved." Emilia's tug at his robe keeps the body and the company in view, so the ending is framed with irony but not retracted. The rival view's best Renaissance text closes on the self's dissolution into God, not its autonomy.
