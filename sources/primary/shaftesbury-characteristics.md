## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

**C4.29 (the moral self is social): supported for Shaftesbury, with one correction.** The moral self is made of "natural affection" for "the kind or species" (.09), it is "a part of some other system" (.08), and the man cut off from others is pictured as misery, "a private and single being, who is thus divided from everything" (.10). *Sensus communis* is a "sense of public weal" and of "the natural equality there is among those of the same species" (.05). Society is natural ("If eating and drinking be natural, herding is so too", .07), and the compact theory of society is mocked (.06). That is direct evidence *against* C4.31 for this author. **The correction:** Shaftesbury's moral self is not formed by "the eyes of others". Its check is inward, a "reflected sense" in which the mind judges its own affections (.13), and, for the theist, God as "witness and spectator" even in "the deepest solitude" (.11). Company is distrusted (.01). So "social" is right; "formed by the eyes of others" is Smith's and Hume's story, not his.

**Soliloquy: classical, not new.** The self-dialogue makes one "two distinct persons [...] pupil and preceptor" (.01). It splits the self in two, but Shaftesbury derives this explicitly from the ancients: the Delphic "Recognise yourself", read as "Divide yourself, or be two", and the wise man's boast of being "never less alone than when by themselves" (.02); the "doctrine of two persons in one individual self" is drawn from a classical tale (.03). The aim is to "agree with ourselves and be of a piece within" (.02) and to be dependable in friendship and society (.04, paraphrase of a damaged line). This is Stoic and Socratic self-examination, the same classical inner court found in Montaigne (C4.13), not a new inward self. Nor is the self self-grounding: against Descartes he asks "What constitutes the We or I?" and answers "I take my being upon trust" (.12).

**Net for the rival view:** Shaftesbury, the first Enlightenment moralist on our list, does not separate the Self from community. He makes the self social by nature, makes its inner life a classical discipline aimed at harmony with the whole, and keeps God as the spectator. He does give the self a new *dignity of sentiment* (a moral sense of its own, .13; and conscience not defined by fear of God, see To do), which is a real Enlightenment strand, but it is framed by "the kind", "the system" and "Nature".

### shaftesbury.01 · *Soliloquy, or Advice to an Author* I.1 — "he becomes two distinct persons. He is pupil and preceptor"

- **Claims:** C4.13 (for), C4.21 (complicates), C4.29 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Soliloquy* Part I, §1; Robertson vol. I, pp. 105–106; `texts/shaftesbury-characteristics-vol1.txt` lines 6529–6553, 6561–6567
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "aort" → "sort", "self-dissection-" → "self-dissection.", "eames" → "earnest", "fidl fort«" → "full force"; "be" restored). The objection just before ("who can thus multiply himself in to two persons and be his own subject?", lines 6526–6527) is not quoted because a word is lost at line 6529.

> Go to the poets, and they will present you with many instances. Nothing is more common with them, than this sort of soliloquy. A person of profound parts, or perhaps of ordinary capacity, happens on some occasion to commit a fault. He is concerned for it. He comes alone upon the stage; looks about him to see if anybody be near; then takes himself to task, without sparing himself in the least. You would wonder to hear how close he pushes matters, and how thoroughly he carries on the business of self-dissection. By virtue of this soliloquy he becomes two distinct persons. He is pupil and preceptor. He teaches, and he learns. [...] For whether the practice [be] natural or no, in respect of common custom and usage, I take upon me to assert, that it is an honest and laudable practice; and that if already it be not natural to us, we ought however to make it so, by study and application.

The inner self-division is real and deliberate, but it is a moral exercise ("takes himself to task" after "a fault"), a practice to be learnt, not a discovered inner essence. A few lines on (6563–6565, damaged at the margin) company is called a provocation to fancy that soliloquy remedies: the self is corrected away from others, not formed by them.

> [...] the only way of composing matters in our breast, and establishing that subordinacy which alone could make us agree with ourselves and be of a piece within. They esteemed this a more religious work than any prayers, or other duty in the temple. [...] This was, among the ancients, that celebrated Delphic inscription, Recognise yourself; which was as much as to say, Divide yourself, or be two. For if the division were rightly made, all within would of course, they thought, be rightly understood and prudently managed. Such confidence they had in this home-dialect of soliloquy. For it was accounted the peculiar of philosophers and wise men, to be able to hold themselves in talk. And it was their boast on this account, "that they were never less alone than when by themselves." A knave, they thought, could never be by himself.

Shaftesbury names his source himself: the Delphic maxim and "the ancients". The "never less alone" boast is classical (Cicero gives it to Scipio, *De Officiis* III.1; verify). The goal is inner order and unity, "of a piece within", not difference.

> "Oh sir!" replied the youth, "well am I now satisfied that I have in reality within me two distinct separate souls. This lesson of philosophy I have learnt from that villainous sophister Love. For 'tis impossible to believe that, having one and the same soul, it should be actually both good and bad, passionate for virtue and vice, desirous of contraries. ### shaftesbury.05 · *Sensus Communis* III.1 (verify) — "sense of public weal, and of the common interest"

> Some of the most ingenious commentators, however, interpret this very differently from what is generally apprehended. > 'Tis ridiculous to say there is any obligation on man to act sociably or honestly in a formed government, and not in that which is commonly called the state of nature. ### shaftesbury.08 · *Inquiry Concerning Virtue* I.ii.1 — "a part of some other system"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (for), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** *An Inquiry Concerning Virtue or Merit*, Book I, Part II, §1; Robertson vol. I, pp. 245–246; `texts/shaftesbury-characteristics-vol1.txt` lines 12765–12769, 12852–12856
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "i>eyond" → "beyond", "ha\-c" → "have", "aniniEd" → "animal", "haa" → "has", "bim" → "him")

> If therefore in the structure of this or any other animal, there be anything which points beyond himself, and by which he is plainly discovered to have relation to some other being or nature besides his own, then will this animal undoubtedly be esteemed a part of some other system. [...] So that in a sensible creature that which is not done through any affection at all makes neither good nor ill in the nature of that creature, who then only is supposed good when the good or ill of the system to which he has relation is the immediate object of some passion or affection moving him.

Goodness is defined by relation to "the system": species, then the earth, then "a universal nature" (lines 12813–12821). The self is a part; it is good only when its affection aims at the whole.

### shaftesbury.09 · *Inquiry* II.ii.1 — "the least able to bear solitude"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (for), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** *Inquiry* Book II, Part II, §1; `texts/shaftesbury-characteristics-vol1.txt` lines 15063–15067, 16225–16230
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "iflections" → "affections", "md in a^m^thy" → "and in sympathy"; the word before "misery" is lost at the margin, line 15067)

> That to have the natural affections (such as are founded in love, complacency, good-will, and in sympathy with the kind or species) is to have the chief means and power of self-enjoyment; and that to want them is [...] misery and ill. [...] How unfortunate must it be for a creature whose dependence on society is greater than any others, to lose that natural affection by which he is prompted to the good and interest of his species and community? Man's "dependence on society is greater than any others": a direct denial of an individual prior to society. Self-enjoyment itself comes through affection for the kind.

### shaftesbury.10 · *Inquiry* II.ii (unnatural affections; section verify) — "a private and single being, who is thus divided from everything"

> What enjoyment or rest is there for one who is not conscious of the merited affection or love, but, on the contrary, of the ill-will and hatred of every human soul? [...] Wherever such a creature turns himself, whichever way he cast his eye, everything around must appear ghastly and horrid; everything hostile and, as it were, bent against a private and single being, who is thus divided from everything, and at defiance and war with the rest of Nature. Shaftesbury describes, as the endpoint of vice, something close to SPEC §2's singularity, the self "divided from everything", and he calls it "inward banishment". The founding Enlightenment moralist describes the separated self as a pathology.

### shaftesbury.11 · *Inquiry* I.iii.3 — "a witness and spectator of human life"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.29 (complicates), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Inquiry* Book I, Part III, §3; `texts/shaftesbury-characteristics-vol1.txt` lines 13789–13798
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "\or" → "Nor", "i>crfcct" → "perfect", "su peri n tendency" → "superintendency", "[X;i-fectest" → "perfectest")

> [...] the theistical belief is entire and perfect, there must be a steady opinion of the superintendency of a supreme Being, a witness and spectator of human life, and conscious of whatsoever is felt or acted in the universe; so that in the perfectest recess or deepest solitude there must be One still presumed remaining with us, whose presence singly must be of more moment than that of the most august assembly on earth. In such a presence, 'tis evident that as the shame of guilty actions must be the greatest of any, so must the honour be of well-doing, even under the unjust censure of a world.

The spectator who matters is God, not other people: His presence outweighs "the most august assembly on earth" and holds "even under the unjust censure of a world". The self stays answerable before God (C4.1), and its moral check is not the eyes of others (against that part of C4.29).

> 'Twill not, in this respect, be sufficient for us to use the seeming logic of a famous modern, and say, "We think, therefore we are." Which is a notably invented saying, after the model of that like philosophical proposition, that "What is, is." Miraculously argued! "If I am, I am." Nothing more certain! For the Ego or I being established in the first part of the proposition, the ergo, no doubt, must hold it good in the latter. But the question is, "What constitutes the We or I?" and "whether the I of this instant be the same with that of any instant preceding or to come?" [...] To the force of this reasoning I confess I must so far su[b]mit [...] as to de[c]lare that, for my own part, I take my being upon trus[t]. Against Descartes, Shaftesbury refuses to make the "I" a self-certifying foundation. Morals rest on conduct "given out and received", not on a self-grounded ego. Another Enlightenment philosopher who does not make the Self its own origin (C5.9).

### shaftesbury.13 · *Inquiry* I.ii.3 — "by means of this reflected sense"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Inquiry* Book I, Part II, §3; `texts/shaftesbury-characteristics-vol1.txt` lines 13033–13041
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "offei'" → "offer", "afiection" → "affection", "hut" → "but", "tliemselvea" → "themselves", "Ho" → "So", "Uiosc" → "those", "whidi" → "which")

> In a creature capable of forming general notions of things, not only the outward beings which offer themselves to the sense are the objects of the affection, but the very actions themselves, and the affections of pity, kindness, gratitude, and their contraries, being brought into the mind by reflection, become objects. So that, by means of this reflected sense, there arises another kind of affection towards those very affections themselves, which have been already felt, and are now become the subject of a new liking or dislike.

The strongest "for" text here for an Enlightenment inward self: morality arises from the mind's reflection on its own affections, a moral sense within each person, with no reference to Church or Scripture. But the affections it judges are social ones ("pity, kindness, gratitude"), and Shaftesbury holds this sense to be common to every man. It is an inward faculty everyone shares, not a separated self. Kant later counts such "moral feelings" as heteronomy (kant-groundwork.06).
