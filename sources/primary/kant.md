## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

**C4.28 is right in its wording and wrong in its implication.** The motto is verified in the German: "Habe Mut, dich deines eigenen Verstandes zu bedienen!" (aufklaerung.01), and the *Groundwork* does make the will the author of its own law (groundwork.01, .02), even ranking the Gospels' "Holy One" below reason's own ideal and calling a morality grounded on God's will heteronomy (groundwork.05, .06). That is the strongest evidence anywhere in the corpus so far for a self that is its own lawgiver and that stands apart from religious authority.

**But the lawgiving self is not the individual Self of SPEC §2.** Three things bound it:

1. **The law is universal.** It is "of his own giving, though at the same time [...] universal" (groundwork.02); the kingdom of ends is reached only "if we abstract from the personal differences of rational beings" (groundwork.03); wit, imagination and humour have mere "fancy value" (groundwork.04). The "proper self" is the rational intelligence; the man of feelings and desires is "only the appearance of himself" (groundwork.07). Autonomy is the opposite of singularity: the pressure point on C4.28 holds.
2. **Enlightenment is public and collective.** Few individuals manage it alone; "ein Publikum" enlightens itself (aufklaerung.03); reason's free use is its use before "dem ganzen Publikum der Leserwelt", as a member of "der Weltbürgergesellschaft" (aufklaerung.04); the test of law is what "ein Volk" could impose on itself (aufklaerung.05). In private office one obeys (aufklaerung.04, .07).
3. **It is answerability, not self-origination.** Immaturity is "selbstverschuldet", caused by "Faulheit und Feigheit" (aufklaerung.01, .02): Kant blames the self for not using what it already has. The structure is the Reformation's (each person answerable on his own, with his own conscience, aufklaerung.02, .06), turned from God toward reason.

**Unsocial sociability (C4.31) goes against the rival claim.** The individual is not prior to society. The "tendency to individualise himself by isolation" is real (history.03), but it is a means Nature uses to make men social and lawful; capacities develop "only in the species and not in the individual" (history.01); trees "apart from one another [...] grow stunted and crooked" (history.05); man "requires a master to break his self-will" (history.06). The one passage that does make man self-made (history.02, "owe it only to himself") is about the species, and it is what "Nature has willed".

**Net for the rival view:** the Enlightenment here takes off as *autonomy*, a real break with Church and tutor, but autonomy is universal reason legislating for all, inside a public, under Nature's plan and (still) a "wise Creator". It supports a separation of *institutions* (church, tutor, prince) from the thinking person, not a separation of the Self from community or from a common frame.

## Passages

### kant-aufklaerung.01 · AA 8:35 (verify) — "Sapere aude!"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for, wording; complicates, scope), C4.1 (complicates), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** opening paragraph; Cassirer ed. p. 169; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 58–64
- **Checked:** text file (spaced-type markers dropped)

> Aufklärung ist der Ausgang des Menschen aus seiner selbstverschuldeten Unmündigkeit. Unmündigkeit ist das Unvermögen, sich seines Verstandes ohne Leitung eines anderen zu bedienen. Selbstverschuldet ist diese Unmündigkeit, wenn die Ursache derselben nicht am Mangel des Verstandes, sondern der Entschließung und des Mutes liegt, sich seiner ohne Leitung eines andern zu bedienen. *Sapere aude!* Habe Mut, dich deines eigenen Verstandes zu bedienen! ist also der Wahlspruch der Aufklärung.

(Our translation: Enlightenment is man's exit from his self-incurred immaturity. Immaturity is the inability to use one's understanding without another's guidance. It is self-incurred when its cause lies not in a lack of understanding but of resolve and courage to use it without another's guidance. Sapere aude! Have courage to use your *own* understanding! is the motto of Enlightenment.)

C4.28's quotation is verified, but it concerns *Verstand* (understanding), not law; self-legislation comes in the *Groundwork*. "Selbstverschuldet" makes the self culpable for its own state: the structure is answerability (C4.1), now owed to reason, not God.

### kant-aufklaerung.02 · AA 8:35 (verify) — "einen Seelsorger, der für mich Gewissen hat"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for), C4.1 (complicates), C4.30 (for, institutional)
- **Location:** second paragraph; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 66–73
- **Checked:** text file

> Faulheit und Feigheit sind die Ursachen, warum ein so großer Teil der Menschen, nachdem sie die Natur längst von fremder Leitung freigesprochen (*naturaliter maiorennes*), dennoch gerne zeitlebens unmündig bleiben; und warum es anderen so leicht wird, sich zu deren Vormündern aufzuwerfen. Es ist so bequem, unmündig zu sein. Habe ich ein Buch, das für mich Verstand hat, einen Seelsorger, der für mich Gewissen hat, einen Arzt, der für mich die Diät beurteilt usw., so brauche ich mich ja nicht selbst zu bemühen.

(Our translation: Laziness and cowardice are the reasons why so many remain immature all their lives [...] If I have a book that has understanding for me, a pastor who has a conscience for me, a doctor who judges my diet for me, I need not trouble myself.)

Delegated conscience is the first target, as it was Luther's. The fault is moral (laziness, cowardice): a charge of failed answerability, not a celebration of a distinct self.

### kant-aufklaerung.03 · AA 8:36 (verify) — "Daß aber ein Publikum sich selbst aufkläre"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.15 (for), C4.28 (complicates)
- **Location:** third–fourth paragraphs; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 97–107
- **Checked:** text file

> Daher gibt es nur wenige, denen es gelungen ist, durch eigene Bearbeitung ihres Geistes sich aus der Unmündigkeit herauszuwickeln und dennoch einen sicheren Gang zu tun. Daß aber ein Publikum sich selbst aufkläre, ist eher möglich; ja es ist, wenn man ihm nur Freiheit läßt, beinahe unausbleiblich. Denn da werden sich immer einige Selbstdenkende, sogar unter den eingesetzten Vormündern des großen Haufens finden, welche, nachdem sie das Joch der Unmündigkeit selbst abgeworfen haben, den Geist einer vernünftigen Schätzung des eigenen Werts und des Berufs jedes Menschen, selbst zu denken, um sich verbreiten werden.

(Our translation: So only a few have worked themselves out of immaturity by their own effort. But that a public should enlighten itself is more possible [...] the spirit of a rational estimate of one's own worth and of the vocation of every human being to think for himself.)

The subject of enlightenment is a *public*, not a lone individual. Thinking for oneself is every person's calling ("des Berufs jedes Menschen, selbst zu denken": the vocation of every man to think for himself; our translation): a common vocation, not a distinction.

### kant-aufklaerung.04 · AA 8:37 (verify) — public and private use of reason

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.28 (complicates)
- **Location:** fifth paragraph; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 130–150
- **Checked:** text file (spaced-type markers dropped)

> Der öffentliche Gebrauch seiner Vernunft muß jederzeit frei sein, und der allein kann Aufklärung unter Menschen zustande bringen; der Privatgebrauch derselben aber darf öfters sehr enge eingeschränkt sein, ohne doch darum den Fortschritt der Aufklärung sonderlich zu hindern. Ich verstehe aber unter dem öffentlichen Gebrauche seiner eigenen Vernunft denjenigen, den jemand als Gelehrter von ihr vor dem ganzen Publikum der Leserwelt macht. [...] Sofern sich aber dieser Teil der Maschine zugleich als Glied eines ganzen gemeinen Wesens, ja sogar der Weltbürgergesellschaft ansieht, mithin in der Qualität eines Gelehrten, der sich an ein Publikum im eigentlichen Verstande durch Schriften wendet, kann er allerdings räsonnieren, ohne daß dadurch die Geschäfte leiden, zu denen er zum Teile als passives Glied angesetzt ist.

(Our translation: The public use of one's reason must always be free [...] I mean by it the use a person makes of it as a scholar before the whole reading public. [...] Insofar as this part of the machine regards himself as a member of a whole commonwealth, even of cosmopolitan society [...] he may certainly argue.)

Free reason is reason addressed to everyone, exercised as a "Glied" (member) of a world-society; in office the same person is a "passives Glied" who must obey. The free self is a member, not a separated individual.

### kant-aufklaerung.05 · AA 8:39 (verify) — "ob ein Volk sich selbst wohl ein solches Gesetz auferlegen könnte?"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (complicates), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** seventh paragraph; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 210–216, 238–241
- **Checked:** text file

> Das wäre ein Verbrechen wider die menschliche Natur, deren ursprüngliche Bestimmung gerade in diesem Fortschreiten besteht; [...] Der Probierstein alles dessen, was über ein Volk als Gesetz beschlossen werden kann, liegt in der Frage: ob ein Volk sich selbst wohl ein solches Gesetz auferlegen könnte? [...] Was aber nicht einmal ein Volk über sich selbst beschließen darf, das darf noch weniger ein Monarch über das Volk beschließen; denn sein gesetzgebendes Ansehen beruht eben darauf, daß er den gesamten Volkswillen in dem seinigen vereinigt.

(Our translation: That would be a crime against human nature, whose original vocation lies in this progress [...] The touchstone of whatever can be decreed as law for a people lies in the question whether a people could impose such a law on itself.)

Self-legislation appears in this essay, but its subject is *ein Volk* and its will is "der gesamte Volkswille". The individual is not the lawgiver here; the people is, and "human nature" sets the vocation.

### kant-aufklaerung.06 · AA 8:40–41 (verify) — "in allem, was Gewissensangelegenheit ist"; "vorzüglich in Religionssachen"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for, institutional), C4.28 (for), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ninth and tenth paragraphs; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 269–277, 290–295
- **Checked:** text file (spaced-type markers dropped)

> Ein Fürst, der es seiner nicht unwürdig findet zu sagen, daß er es für Pflicht halte, in Religionsdingen den Menschen nichts vorzuschreiben, sondern ihnen darin volle Freiheit zu lassen, der also selbst den hochmütigen Namen der Toleranz von sich ablehnt, ist selbst aufgeklärt und verdient von der dankbaren Welt und Nachwelt als derjenige gepriesen zu werden, der zuerst das menschliche Geschlecht der Unmündigkeit, wenigstens von seiten der Regierung, entschlug und jedem frei ließ, sich in allem, was Gewissensangelegenheit ist, seiner eigenen Vernunft zu bedienen. [...] Ich habe den Hauptpunkt der Aufklärung, d. i. des Ausganges der Menschen aus ihrer selbstverschuldeten Unmündigkeit, vorzüglich in Religionssachen gesetzt, weil in Ansehung der Künste und Wissenschaften unsere Beherrscher kein Interesse haben, den Vormund über ihre Untertanen zu spielen, überdem auch jene Unmündigkeit, so wie die schädlichste, also auch die entehrendste unter allen ist.

(Our translation: [...] who left each free to use his own reason in all matters of conscience. [...] I have placed the main point of enlightenment chiefly in matters of religion, because [...] that immaturity is the most harmful and the most dishonouring of all.)

Enlightenment's centre is religion: freedom of conscience from clerical and state tutelage. This is a separation of conscience from *institutions*, in the Reformation's own terms (Gewissen), not a separation of the self from religion as such.

### kant-aufklaerung.07 · AA 8:41–42 (verify) — "Räsonniert, soviel ihr wollt [...]; nur gehorcht!"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (complicates), C4.31 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** closing paragraph; `texts/kant-was-ist-aufklaerung-de.txt` lines 307–321
- **Checked:** text file (spaced-type markers and footnote marker dropped)

> Räsonniert, soviel ihr wollt, und worüber ihr wollt; nur gehorcht! [...] Ein größerer Grad bürgerlicher Freiheit scheint der Freiheit des Geistes des Volks vorteilhaft und setzt ihr doch unübersteigliche Schranken; ein Grad weniger von jener verschafft hingegen diesem Raum, sich nach allem seinen Vermögen auszubreiten. Wenn denn die Natur unter dieser harten Hülle den Keim, für den sie am zärtlichsten sorgt, nämlich den Hang und Beruf zum freien Denken, ausgewickelt hat: so wirkt dieser allmählich zurück auf die Sinnesart des Volks, (wodurch dies der Freiheit zu handeln nach und nach fähiger wird), und endlich auch sogar auf die Grundsätze der Regierung, die es ihr selbst zuträglich findet, den Menschen, der nun mehr als Maschine ist, seiner Würde gemäß zu behandeln.

(Our translation: Argue as much as you like and about whatever you like; only obey! [...] when Nature has unfolded the seed she tends most tenderly, the inclination and vocation to free thinking, it works back on the people's way of thinking [...] and at last on government, which finds it to its own advantage to treat man, who is now more than a machine, in keeping with his dignity.)

Free thought is a seed planted by Nature, it grows under obedience, and its result is the dignity of "den Menschen", man in general. Freedom of the mind is bounded by civil obedience and is held for everyone alike.

### kant-groundwork.01 · AA 4:431 (verify) — the will "as itself giving the law"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for)
- **Location:** Second Section, third formula; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 1806–1815
- **Checked:** text file

> Hence follows the third practical principle of the will, which is the ultimate condition of its harmony with universal practical reason, viz.: the idea of the will of every rational being as a universally legislative will. On this principle all maxims are rejected which are inconsistent with the will being itself universal legislator. Thus the will is not subject simply to the law, but so subject that it must be regarded as itself giving the law and, on this ground only, subject to the law (of which it can regard itself as the author).

This is C4.28's core and it holds: the will is "author" of the law it obeys. Note that it is "the will of every rational being" and "universal legislator", not one will's own law.

### kant-groundwork.02 · AA 4:432–433 (verify) — "only those of his own giving, though at the same time they are universal"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for; complicates), C4.15 (for)
- **Location:** Second Section; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 1865–1883
- **Checked:** text file

> Looking back now on all previous attempts to discover the principle of morality, we need not wonder why they all failed. It was seen that man was bound to laws by duty, but it was not observed that the laws to which he is subject are only those of his own giving, though at the same time they are universal, and that he is only bound to act in conformity with his own will; a will, however, which is designed by nature to give universal laws. [...] I will therefore call this the principle of autonomy of the will, in contrast with every other which I accordingly reckon as heteronomy.

Kant claims novelty here: every earlier morality was heteronomy. That supports the "take-off" half of C4.21. But the self-given law is by definition universal and "designed by nature": autonomy is a common frame.

### kant-groundwork.03 · AA 4:433 (verify) — the kingdom of ends: "abstract from the personal differences of rational beings"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for), C4.28 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** Second Section; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 1891–1912
- **Checked:** text file

> By a kingdom I understand the union of different rational beings in a system by common laws. Now since it is by laws that ends are determined as regards their universal validity, hence, if we abstract from the personal differences of rational beings and likewise from all the content of their private ends, we shall be able to conceive all ends combined in a systematic whole [...] Hence results a systematic union of rational being by common objective laws, i.e., a kingdom which may be called a kingdom of ends, since what these laws have in view is just the relation of these beings to one another as ends and means. It is certainly only an ideal. A rational being belongs as a member to the kingdom of ends when, although giving universal laws in it, he is also himself subject to these laws.

The moral community is reached by setting aside "personal differences". The self-legislator is a "member", giving and receiving the same law: autonomy is constitutively shared, the opposite of singularity.

### kant-groundwork.04 · AA 4:434–435 (verify) — dignity versus "fancy value"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** Second Section; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 1945–1965
- **Checked:** text file

> In the kingdom of ends everything has either value or dignity. Whatever has a value can be replaced by something else which is equivalent; whatever, on the other hand, is above all value, and therefore admits of no equivalent, has a dignity. [...] Now morality is the condition under which alone a rational being can be an end in himself, since by this alone is it possible that he should be a legislating member in the kingdom of ends. Thus morality, and humanity as capable of it, is that which alone has dignity. Skill and diligence in labour have a market value; wit, lively imagination, and humour, have fancy value; on the other hand, fidelity to promises, benevolence from principle (not from instinct), have an intrinsic worth.

What is irreplaceable in a person is the capacity everyone shares, not what is peculiar to him. The individual excellences that C4.21 credits to the Renaissance (wit, imagination) rank as mere "fancy value".

### kant-groundwork.05 · AA 4:408 (verify) — "Even the Holy One of the Gospels must first be compared with our ideal"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for), C4.30 (for), C4.1 (against), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Section; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 869–885
- **Checked:** text file

> Nor could anything be more fatal to morality than that we should wish to derive it from examples. For every example of it that is set before me must be first itself tested by principles of morality, whether it is worthy to serve as an original example, i.e., as a pattern; but by no means can it authoritatively furnish the conception of morality. Even the Holy One of the Gospels must first be compared with our ideal of moral perfection before we can recognise Him as such [...] But whence have we the conception of God as the supreme good? Simply from the idea of moral perfection, which reason frames a priori and connects inseparably with the notion of a free will. Imitation finds no place at all in morality

The strongest "for" text so far for a self no longer bounded by God: reason judges Christ and even supplies the concept of God. Against C4.1's "bounded by God" for Kant. It complicates C5.9: philosophy here makes *reason* the origin of morality, though not the individual Self.

### kant-groundwork.06 · AA 4:441–443 (verify) — the will of God as heteronomy

- **Claims:** C4.28 (for), C4.30 (for), C4.1 (against)
- **Location:** Second Section, "Heteronomy of the Will as the Source of all spurious Principles of Morality"; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 2196–2201, 2232–2238, 2289–2297
- **Checked:** text file

> If the will seeks the law which is to determine it anywhere else than in the fitness of its maxims to be universal laws of its own dictation, consequently if it goes out of itself and seeks this law in the character of any of its objects, there always results heteronomy. [...] All principles which can be taken from this point of view are either empirical or rational. The former, drawn from the principle of happiness, are built on physical or moral feelings; the latter, drawn from the principle of perfection, are built either on the rational conception of perfection as a possible effect, or on that of an independent perfection (the will of God) as the determining cause of our will. [...] it is nevertheless preferable to the theological view, first, because we have no intuition of the divine perfection and can only deduce it from our own conceptions, the most important of which is that of morality, and our explanation would thus be involved in a gross circle; and, in the next place, if we avoid this, the only notion of the Divine will remaining to us is a conception made up of the attributes of desire of glory and dominion, combined with the awful conceptions of might and vengeance, and any system of morals erected on this foundation would be directly opposed to morality.

A morality grounded on God's will is "directly opposed to morality". Here Calvin's frame ("We are not our own", see `calvin-institutes.md`) is explicitly reversed. Note that "moral feelings" (Shaftesbury's and Hutcheson's moral sense) are also heteronomy: Kant cuts the self from sentiment as well as from God.

### kant-groundwork.07 · AA 4:457 (verify) — "his proper self [...] being as man only the appearance of himself"

- **Claims:** C4.28 (complicates), C4.15 (for), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Third Section, "Of the Extreme Limits of all Practical Philosophy"; Abbott; `texts/kant-groundwork.txt` lines 2856–2870
- **Checked:** text file

> The causality of such actions lies in him as an intelligence and in the laws of effects and actions [which depend] on the principles of an intelligible world, of which indeed he knows nothing more than that in it pure reason alone independent of sensibility gives the law; moreover since it is only in that world, as an intelligence, that he is his proper self (being as man only the appearance of himself), those laws apply to him directly and categorically, so that the incitements of inclinations and appetites (in other words the whole nature of the world of sense) cannot impair the laws of his volition as an intelligence. Nay, he does not even hold himself responsible for the former or ascribe them to his proper self, i.e., his will

The self that gives the law is pure reason. SPEC §2's Self (one's feelings, will and desires as distinct from the collective) is here mere "appearance". It also *limits* answerability: one is not responsible for one's inclinations, only for yielding to them, a bound like Locke's.

### kant-history.01 · Second Proposition, AA 8:18–19 (verify) — "only in the species and not in the individual"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Hastie, *Principles of Politics*, I, Second and Third Propositions, pp. 6–9; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 1635–1640, 1746–1750
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; line-break hyphens joined)

> In Man, as the only rational creature on earth, those natural capacities which are directed towards the use of his Reason, could be completely developed only in the species and not in the individual. [...] one species of animals was destined to possess Reason, and that, forming a class of rational beings mortal in all the individuals but immortal in the species, it was yet to attain to a complete development of its capacities.

The individual is not the unit of development. Reason is completed across "an almost inconceivable series of generations" (line 1660); the individual is a stage in the species.

### kant-history.02 · Third Proposition, AA 8:19–20 (verify) — "owe it only to himself"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C5.9 (complicates), C4.24 (complicates)
- **Location:** Hastie, I, Third Proposition, pp. 7–8; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 1679–1686, 1706–1709, 1714–1723
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "ooodness" → "goodness")

> Nature has willed that Man shall produce wholly out of himself all that goes beyond the mechanical structure and arrangement of his animal existence, and that he shall participate in no other happiness or perfection but what he has procured for himself, apart from Instinct, by his own Reason. [...] as well as all the sources of delight which could make life agreeable, his very insight and prudence, and even the goodness of his Will, all these were to be entirely his own work. [...] as if it had been her will that Man, when he had at last struggled up from the greatest crudeness of life to the highest capability and to internal perfection in his habit of thought, and thereby also — so far as it is possible on earth — to happiness, should claim the merit of it as all his own and owe it only to himself. It thus looks as if Nature had laid more upon his rational self-esteem than upon his mere well-being.

The best text for the rival view: self-making, even of one's goodness, and "rational self-esteem". But the subject is "Man" (the species, see .01), and the self-making is what "Nature has willed": given, like Pico's freedom (C4.24 pressure point), not self-originated.

### kant-history.03 · Fourth Proposition, AA 8:20–21 (verify) — "the unsocial sociability of men"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.29 (complicates)
- **Location:** Hastie, I, Fourth Proposition, pp. 9–11; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 1755–1765, 1767–1798
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; marginal "/ \ {" marks dropped; fixed: "individuo.lise" → "individualise", "amono-" → "among")

> The means which Nature employs to bring about the development of all the capacities implanted in men, is their mutual Antagonism in society, but only so far as this antagonism becomes at length the cause of an Order among them that is regulated by Law. By this Antagonism, I mean the unsocial sociability of men; that is, their tendency to enter into society, conjoined, however, with an accompanying resistance which continually threatens to dissolve this society. The disposition for this lies manifestly in human nature. Man has an inclination to socialise himself by associating with others, because in such a state he feels himself more than a natural man, in the development of his natural capacities. He has, moreover, a great tendency to individualise himself by isolation from others, because he likewise finds in himself the unsocial disposition of wishing to direct everything merely according to his own mind [...] Now it is this resistance or mutual antagonism that awakens all the powers of man, that drives him to overcome all his propensity to indolence, and that impels him through the desire of honour or power or wealth, to strive after rank among his fellow-men — whom he can neither bear to interfere with himself, nor yet let alone.

Hastie's wording is "unsocial sociability". Kant names an individualising drive, which is evidence that the separated self is a real pole. But it is a drive *within* society, it works through the desire for "rank among his fellow-men", and its end is "an Order [...] regulated by Law". It is not prior to society.

- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "wliich" → "which", "hioher" → "higher", "belonored" → "belonged", "J[esire" restored as "[d]esire"; one garbled word after "possession" omitted)

> Without those qualities of an unsocial kind, out of which this Antagonism arises — which viewed by themselves are certainly not amiable but which everyone must necessarily find in the movements of his own selfish propensities — men might have led an Arcadian shepherd life in complete harmony, contentment and mutual love, but in that case all their talents would have for ever remained hidden in their germ. As gentle as the sheep they tended, such men would hardly have won for their existence a higher worth than belonged to their domesticated cattle [...] Thanks be then to Nature for this unsociableness, for this envious jealousy and vanity, for this unsatiable [d]esire of possession [...] even of power! Unsociability is "not amiable", a means used by Nature, and evidence of "a wise Creator". The frame (Nature, providence) stays intact while the individual's self-assertion is put to work.

### kant-history.05 · Fifth Proposition, AA 8:22 (verify) — the trees in a forest

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** Hastie, I, Fifth Proposition, pp. 13–14; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 1897–1918
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; marginal marks dropped)

> But in such a complete growth as the Civil Union, these very inclinations afterwards produce the best effects. It is with them as with the trees in a forest; for just because everyone strives to deprive the other of air and sun, they compel each other to seek them both above, and thus they grow beautiful and straight, whereas those that in freedom and apart from one another shoot out their branches at will, grow stunted and crooked and awry. All the culture and art that adorn humanity, and the fairest social order, are fruits of that unsociableness which is necessitated of itself to discipline itself and which thus constrains man, by compulsive art, to develop completely the germs of his Nature.

Kant's own image answers C4.31: the person who grows "in freedom and apart from one another" grows "crooked". The individual is formed by society and law, not prior to them.

### kant-history.06 · Sixth Proposition, AA 8:23 (verify) — "a master to break his self-will"; "such crooked material"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C1.1 (for), C4.28 (complicates)
- **Location:** Hastie, I, Sixth Proposition, pp. 14–15; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 1927–1955
- **Checked:** text file (OCR fixed: "anvmal" → "animal", "lias" → "has")

> The difficulty which the mere idea of this Problem brings into view, is that man is an animal, and if he lives among others of his kind he has need of a Master. For he certainly misuses his freedom in relation to his fellow-men; and, although as a rational creature, he desires a law which may set bounds to the freedom of all, yet his own selfish animal inclinations lead him wherever he can, to except himself from it. He, therefore, requires a master to break his self-will, and compel him to obey a Will that is universally valid, and in relation to which everyone may be free. [...] Out of such crooked material as man is made of, nothing can be hammered quite straight.

The self that wants to "except himself" from the common law is the problem, not the ideal. "Self-will" must be broken by "a Will that is universally valid": the bound is explicit.

### kant-history.07 · Seventh Proposition, AA 8:26 (verify) — "civilised, even to excess", not yet "moralised"

- **Claims:** C4.29 (complicates), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Hastie, I, Seventh Proposition, pp. 20–21; `texts/kant-principles-of-politics-hastie.txt` lines 2148–2157
- **Checked:** text file

> We are cultivated in a high degree by Science and Art. We are civilised, even to excess, in the way of all sorts of social forms of politeness and elegance. But there is still much to be done before we can be regarded as moralised. The idea of morality certainly belongs to real Culture; but an application of this idea which extends no farther than the likeness of morality in the sense of honour and external propriety, merely constitutes civilisation.

Kant separates a self formed by the eyes of others (honour, propriety: C4.29's social self) from the moral self. The Enlightenment thus contains both pictures; Kant ranks the social one as mere "civilisation".
