# Montaigne, *Essays* (1580; final additions 1595)

- **Edition used:** trans. Charles Cotton, rev. W. C. Hazlitt
- **Text file:** `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): Villey-Saulnier (French); English: Frame (1958) or Screech (1991)
- **Citation form:** Book.essay, e.g. III.13 "Of Experience"
- **Claims to test:** C4.15, C4.1, C4.13, C4.8

**Numbering.** Cotton's Book I runs one lower than the modern (Villey) numbering from I.14 on: Cotton I.19 "That to study philosophy is to learn to die" = modern I.20; I.22 "Of custom" = I.23; I.25 "Of the education of children" = I.26; I.27 "Of friendship" = I.28; I.36 "Of Cato the Younger" = I.37; I.38 "Of solitude" = I.39. Below, Cotton's number comes first and the modern one follows in brackets where they differ.

**Note on "To the Reader."** Cotton omitted it. The text in our file (line 2028) is the editor's translation, marked "[Omitted by Cotton.]". Cite it as Hazlitt's translation, not Cotton's.

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

Montaigne takes the self as his whole subject, and he values the right to judge for oneself and to be judged "every man by himself." But the self he paints is mostly a *sample*, not a singularity: "every man carries the entire form of human condition" (.03). It is changing (.02, .12), bodily (.16), mortal, and bounded by Church, custom and "the common and human model" (.06, .10, .16). Singular fashions are "marks of folly" (.10). Where he does value difference, it is (a) difference in *others*, tolerated and admired (.07), (b) an inborn "governing form" that *every* man has (.05), or (c) a Ciceronian doctrine of keeping one's own nature (.09). None of these is the different Self of C4.15: his feelings are not held to be unlike everyone's *and valuable for that reason*. The real novelty he claims is the *method* (studying oneself, .11), not the uniqueness of the object. He does supply two things the thesis must reckon with: an inner judgment answerable to itself, not to God (.04), and a split between an inner self and social roles (.14, .15), which predates Rousseau (C4.18).

## Passages

### montaigne-essays.01 · "To the Reader" (1580) — "myself am the matter of my book"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** prefatory note, dated 12 June 1580 (Hazlitt's translation; omitted by Cotton); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 2030–2049
- **Checked:** text file

> I have dedicated it to the particular commodity of my kinsfolk and friends, so that, having lost me (which they must do shortly), they may therein recover some traits of my conditions and humours [...] I desire therein to be viewed as I appear in mine own genuine, simple, and ordinary manner, without study and artifice: for it is myself I paint. My defects are therein to be read to the life, and any imperfections and my natural form, so far as public reverence hath permitted me. [...] Thus, reader, myself am the matter of my book: there’s no reason thou shouldst employ thy leisure about so frivolous and vain a subject.

The self is the subject, but presented as "ordinary," for private memory, limited by "public reverence," and called "frivolous and vain." It is a subject, not yet a value; the "different Self" is not claimed here.

### montaigne-essays.02 · III.2 "Of Repentance" — "I do not paint its being, I paint its passage"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C5.6 (for)
- **Location:** III.2; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 36809–36826
- **Checked:** text file

> Others form man; I only report him: and represent a particular one, ill fashioned enough [...] I cannot fix my object; ‘tis always tottering and reeling by a natural giddiness; I take it as it is at the instant I consider it; I do not paint its being, I paint its passage; not a passing from one age to another, or, as the people say, from seven to seven years, but from day to day, from minute to minute, I must accommodate my history to the hour: I may presently change, not only by fortune, but also by intention. [...] whether it be that I am then another self, or that I take subjects by other circumstances and considerations: so it is that I may peradventure contradict myself, but, as Demades said, I never contradict the truth.

The wording "I do not paint its being, I paint its passage" is verified in Cotton. The self is "a particular one" but has no fixed essence to be unique; it exists *in* the flow of experience, the baseline C5.6 says was later lost.

### montaigne-essays.03 · III.2 "Of Repentance" — "every man carries the entire form of human condition"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (against), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.2; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 36830–36840
- **Checked:** text file

> I propose a life ordinary and without lustre: ‘tis all one; all moral philosophy may as well be applied to a common and private life, as to one of richer composition: every man carries the entire form of human condition. Authors communicate themselves to the people by some especial and extrinsic mark; I, the first of any, by my universal being; as Michel de Montaigne, not as a grammarian, a poet, or a lawyer. If the world find fault that I speak too much of myself, I find fault that they do not so much as think of themselves. But is it reason that, being so particular in my way of living, I should pretend to recommend myself to the public knowledge?

The to-do phrase "the whole form of the human condition" is, in Cotton, "every man carries the entire form of human condition." His self is worth reading because it is *common* ("my universal being"), not because it is unlike others. "So particular in my way of living" is offered as an objection to his project, not as its justification. Against C4.15 for Montaigne. It complicates C4.13 because the equality here is the equality of a shared human form, not of standing before God.

### montaigne-essays.04 · III.2 "Of Repentance" — "I have my laws and my judicature"

- **Claims:** C4.13 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.2; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 36932–36942
- **Checked:** text file

> We, who live private lives, not exposed to any other view than our own, ought chiefly to have settled a pattern within ourselves by which to try our actions: and according to that, sometimes to encourage and sometimes to correct ourselves. I have my laws and my judicature to judge of myself, and apply myself more to these than to any other rules: I do, indeed, restrain my actions according to others; but extend them not by any other rule than my own. You yourself only know if you are cowardly and cruel, loyal and devout: others see you not, and only guess at you by uncertain conjectures, and do not so much see your nature as your art; rely not therefore upon their opinions, but stick to your own

This is answerability, but before an inner court ("my laws and my judicature"), and its sources are Cicero and Seneca, not the Reformation. So the answerable Self has a second, classical-humanist root outside the Luther–Calvin line. It is still bounded: "I do, indeed, restrain my actions according to others."

### montaigne-essays.05 · III.2 "Of Repentance" — "a particular and governing form of his own"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C4.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.2; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 37082–37085 (context: "these original qualities are not to be rooted out", line 37066)
- **Checked:** text file

> Look a little into our experience: there is no man, if he listen to himself, who does not in himself discover a particular and governing form of his own, that jostles his education, and wrestles with the tempest of passions that are contrary to it.

This is the passage closest to "one's own nature." Each person has an inborn form "of his own" that resists education. But it is stated of *every* man, as a general fact of human nature. It is not prized because it is unlike others. It is also a counterweight to Locke's white paper (C4.11): nature outlasts education.

### montaigne-essays.06 · III.2 "Of Repentance" — submission to the Church; prayer to God

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** III.2; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 36873–36878 and 37164–37168
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] always adding this clause, not one of ceremony, but a true and real submission, that I speak inquiring and doubting, purely and simply referring myself to the common and accepted beliefs for the resolution. I do not teach; I only relate.

> For my own part, I may desire in general to be other than I am; I may condemn and dislike my whole form, and beg of Almighty God for an entire reformation, and that He will please to pardon my natural infirmity: but I ought not to call this repentance, methinks, no more than the being dissatisfied that I am not an angel or Cato.

The self-portrait is explicitly placed under the Church's "common and accepted beliefs" and under God's pardon. This fits the §4 pattern: the self as subject comes with a limit attached. Whether the submission is sincere is debated, but the text states it as "a true and real submission."

### montaigne-essays.07 · I.36 [I.37] "Of Cato the Younger" — "more easily admit of difference than uniformity"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for, partly), C5.6 (for)
- **Location:** Cotton I.36 (modern I.37); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 11253–11266
- **Checked:** text file

> I am not guilty of the common error of judging another by myself. I easily believe that in another’s humour which is contrary to my own; and though I find myself engaged to one certain form, I do not oblige others to it, as many do; but believe and apprehend a thousand ways of living; and, contrary to most men, more easily admit of difference than uniformity amongst us. I as frankly as any one would have me, discharge a man from my humours and principles, and consider him according to his own particular model. [...] I insinuate myself by imagination into their place, and love and honour them the more for being other than I am. I very much desire that we may be judged every man by himself, and would not be drawn into the consequence of common examples.

This is the strongest evidence *for* C4.15 in Montaigne: difference is affirmed ("a thousand ways of living"), each is judged "by his own particular model," and others are honoured "for being other than I am." But it is difference in *others*, admitted and admired across a relation, not a claim that his own feelings are singular. In SPEC §2 terms this is *relational* difference, the healthy kind (C5.6 baseline).

### montaigne-essays.08 · II.17 "Of Presumption" — "a soul free and entirely its own"; "I turn my sight inwards"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for, partly), C4.18 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.17; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 30074–30080 and 30728–30749
- **Checked:** text file

> I have a soul free and entirely its own, and accustomed to guide itself after its own fashion; having hitherto never had either master or governor imposed upon me: I have walked as far as I would, and at the pace that best pleased myself; this is it that has rendered me unfit for the service of others, and has made me of no use to any one but myself.

> The world looks always opposite; I turn my sight inwards, and there fix and employ it. I have no other business but myself, I am eternally meditating upon myself, considering and tasting myself. [...] for my part, I circulate in myself. [...] the strongest and most general imaginations I have are those that, as a man may say, were born with me; they are natural and entirely my own. I produced them crude and simple [...] I have since established and fortified them with the authority of others and the sound examples of the ancients, whom I have found of the same judgment

Here the self is "entirely its own," turned inward, and has views "born with me." That is a real step toward C4.15. But the inborn views are confirmed by finding the ancients "of the same judgment." What is his own turns out to be shared. He presents his independence as a temperament that makes him "unfit for the service of others," which is a confession, not a virtue.

### montaigne-essays.09 · II.17 "Of Presumption" — Cicero: do not lay aside your own nature

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C4.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.17, quoting Cicero, *De Officiis* I.31; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 30750–30761
- **Checked:** text file

> The reputation that every one pretends to of vivacity and promptness of wit, I seek in regularity; the glory they pretend to from a striking and signal action, or some particular excellence, I claim from order, correspondence, and tranquillity of opinions and manners:
>
> [...] [“If anything be entirely decorous, nothing certainly can be more so than an equability alike in the whole life and in every particular action; which thou canst not possibly observe if, imitating other men’s natures, thou layest aside thy own.”--Cicero, De Of., i. 31.]

Montaigne's warrant for keeping "thy own" nature is Cicero's doctrine of each person's own character (*De Officiis* I.31). So the idea of an individual nature to be kept has a classical root, which neither the §4 groundwork list (C4.8: Paul, Plato, Augustine) nor the Reformation line includes. He also rejects "some particular excellence" as a goal in favour of "regularity."

### montaigne-essays.10 · I.22 [I.23] "Of Custom" — "all singular and particular fashions are rather marks of folly"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (against), C4.1 (for), C4.18 (complicates)
- **Location:** Cotton I.22 (modern I.23); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 6245–6258
- **Checked:** text file

> These considerations, notwithstanding, will not prevail upon any understanding man to decline the common mode; but, on the contrary, methinks, all singular and particular fashions are rather marks of folly and vain affectation than of sound reason, and that a wise man, within, ought to withdraw and retire his soul from the crowd, and there keep it at liberty and in power to judge freely of things; but as to externals, absolutely to follow and conform himself to the fashion of the time. Public society has nothing to do with our thoughts, but the rest, as our actions, our labours, our fortunes, and our lives, we are to lend and abandon them to its service and to the common opinion [...] for it is the rule of rules, the general law of laws, that every one observe those of the place wherein he lives.

Outward singularity is condemned as "folly and vain affectation." Inner freedom of judgment is kept, but it is fenced off from action, which belongs to custom and law. That is a clear §4-style bound (community, custom). The inner/outer split also predates Rousseau (C4.18), though here the outer self is a duty, not a falsehood.

### montaigne-essays.11 · II.6 "Use Makes Perfect" ("Of Practice") — "a new and extraordinary undertaking"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C4.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.6 (Cotton's title "Use makes perfect"; modern "Of Practice"); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 18276–18296
- **Checked:** text file

> Every one, as Pliny says, is a good doctrine to himself, provided he be capable of discovering himself near at hand. Here, this is not my doctrine, ‘tis my study; and is not the lesson of another, but my own; and if I communicate it, it ought not to be ill taken, for that which is of use to me, may also, peradventure, be useful to another. [...] We hear but of two or three of the ancients, who have beaten this path [...] No one since has followed the track: ‘tis a rugged road, more so than it seems, to follow a pace so rambling and uncertain, as that of the soul; to penetrate the dark profundities of its intricate internal windings; to choose and lay hold of so many little nimble motions; ‘tis a new and extraordinary undertaking, and that withdraws us from the common and most recommended employments of the world.

Montaigne claims that the *practice* of self-study is new, not that the self studied is unique. What he learns about himself "may also [...] be useful to another." The self is a sample. For C4.14 this marks a turn inward in 1580, outside the 1517–1694 line.

### montaigne-essays.12 · II.1 "Of the Inconstancy of Our Actions" — "as much difference betwixt us and ourselves as betwixt us and others"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (against), C5.6 (for)
- **Location:** II.1; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 16669–16672
- **Checked:** text file

> We are all lumps, and of so various and inform a contexture, that every piece plays, every moment, its own game, and there is as much difference betwixt us and ourselves as betwixt us and others

This is difference, but it cuts the wrong way for C4.15. If I differ from myself as much as from others, there is no stable "I" whose unlikeness to others could be a value. "We are all" again makes it a general human condition.

### montaigne-essays.13 · I.25 [I.26] "Of the Education of Children" — "absolutely his own [...] his judgment"; the world as "mirror"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C4.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** Cotton I.25 (modern I.26); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 7804–7836 and 8137–8143
- **Checked:** text file

> Let him make him examine and thoroughly sift everything he reads, and lodge nothing in his fancy upon simple authority and upon trust. [...] for, if he embrace the opinions of Xenophon and Plato, by his own reason, they will no more be theirs, but become his own. [...] Truth and reason are common to every one, and are no more his who spake them first, than his who speaks them after [...] Bees cull their several sweets from this flower and that blossom, here and there where they find them, but themselves afterwards make the honey, which is all and purely their own, and no more thyme and marjoram: so the several fragments he borrows from others, he will transform and shuffle together to compile a work that shall be absolutely his own; that is to say, his judgment

> This great world which some do yet multiply as several species under one genus, is the mirror wherein we are to behold ourselves, to be able to know ourselves as we ought to do in the true bias. [...] So many humours, so many sects, so many judgments, opinions, laws, and customs, teach us to judge aright of our own, and inform our understanding to discover its imperfection and natural infirmity

What becomes "absolutely his own" is *judgment*, built from common materials, and "truth and reason are common to every one." Self-knowledge comes from the outside, through the world as "mirror." The self is formed by education and comparison, close to Locke's (C4.11), and it learns its "natural infirmity," not its uniqueness.

### montaigne-essays.14 · I.38 [I.39] "Of Solitude" — "a backshop, wholly our own and entirely free"

- **Claims:** C4.18 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** Cotton I.38 (modern I.39); `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 11748–11757
- **Checked:** text file

> Wives, children, and goods must be had, and especially health, by him that can get it; but we are not so to set our hearts upon them that our happiness must have its dependence upon them; we must reserve a backshop, wholly our own and entirely free, wherein to settle our true liberty, our principal solitude and retreat. And in this we must for the most part entertain ourselves with ourselves, and so privately that no exotic knowledge or communication be admitted there; there to laugh and to talk, as if without wife, children, goods, train, or attendance, to the end that when it shall so fall out that we must lose any or all of these, it may be no new thing to be without them.

The "back shop" wording is verified (Cotton: "backshop"). It is a private inner room, but its purpose is Stoic: preparation for loss, not the expression of a unique nature. The ties ("must be had") stay; the inner room is insurance against losing them. This is an early inner/outer split (C4.18) without Rousseau's charge that the social self is false.

### montaigne-essays.15 · III.10 "Of Managing the Will" — "lend himself to others, and only give himself to himself"

- **Claims:** C4.18 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** III.10; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 45775–45777 and 46143–46161
- **Checked:** text file

> ‘Tis my opinion that a man should lend himself to others, and only give himself to himself.

> We must play our part properly, but withal as a part of a borrowed personage; we must not make real essence of a mask and outward appearance; nor of a strange person, our own [...] The Mayor of Bordeaux and Montaigne have ever been two by very manifest separation.

The person (inner, "given to himself") is separated from the role (public, "lent"). The role is still to be played "properly," so the community bound holds. Montaigne's split protects the inner self *and* honours the office; it does not yet condemn the social self as false (contrast C4.18).

### montaigne-essays.16 · III.13 "Of Experience" — "I study myself [...] 'tis my metaphysic, my physic"; "the common and human model"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (against), C4.1 (for), C5.8 (for), C5.6 (for)
- **Location:** III.13; `texts/montaigne-essays-cotton.txt` lines 48900–48901, 48975–48978 and 50852–50860
- **Checked:** text file

> I study myself more than any other subject; ‘tis my metaphysic, my physic

> If every one would pry into the effects and circumstances of the passions that sway him, as I have done into those which I am most subject to, he would see them coming, and would a little break their impetuosity and career

> ‘Tis an absolute and, as it were, a divine perfection, for a man to know how loyally to enjoy his being. We seek other conditions, by reason we do not understand the use of our own; and go out of ourselves, because we know not how there to reside. ‘Tis to much purpose to go upon stilts, for, when upon stilts, we must yet walk with our legs; and when seated upon the most elevated throne in the world, we are but seated upon our breech. The fairest lives, in my opinion, are those which regularly accommodate themselves to the common and human model without miracle, without extravagance.

The to-do phrase "I study myself more than any other subject" is verified. But self-study is offered as a method "every one" should use, and the essay's last word praises lives fitted "to the common and human model without miracle, without extravagance," with the body as the limit ("seated upon our breech"). (The to-do phrase "living appropriately" is not in Cotton. The nearest wording is "to know how loyally to enjoy his being" here, and at line 50497: "The glorious masterpiece of man is to live to purpose.") This is the clearest statement that his self is bounded and common, and that he resists the mind flying from the body, as C5.8 describes.
