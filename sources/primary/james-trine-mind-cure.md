## Reading summary

**Is this where thought is said to create one's world?** Yes, in so many words, and more plainly than in any §4b or §5b text so far. Trine's epigraph says "Within yourself lies the cause of whatever enters into your life" (trine-infinite.01); his preface says "Each is building his own world" and that through thought "we have creative power, not merely in a figurative sense, but creative power in reality" (.02, .05). James reports the same doctrine as the movement's core: "Thoughts are things", and thoughts of "health, youth, vigor, and success" become "your outward portion" (james-varieties.06). Illness, poverty and adversity are effects of the inner state, and outside conditions are "occasions", not causes (trine-infinite.09, .11). This is SPEC §5's experience "imagined to flow *from* the Self" as an explicit, practical doctrine, sold in volume (James: literature "mechanically produced for the market", james-varieties.02).

**But the Self is not its own origin.** In every passage the creative power is borrowed. Trine's first chapter names the Over-Soul as one of God's names and makes God the source "from which all is continually coming"; man differs from God "in degree", not essence (trine-infinite.03). Man is a "channel", a reservoir fed from the mountain (.04); our creative power is "in like sense" as God's, derived from his (.05); the human will is supreme only "in the degree that [it] is transmuted into the divine" (.07). James's analysis agrees: the mind-curer is "already one with the Divine" through the subconscious (james-varieties.05), and the cure works by *surrender*: "Give up the feeling of responsibility, let go your hold, resign the care of your destiny to higher powers [...] finding that a greater Self is there" (.08). So the frame is kept, as C5.14's pressure point feared. This is Emerson's Over-Soul popularized (C4.19), and Trine says so himself by naming Emerson as its prophet (trine-infinite.06).

**What changed from Emerson** is not the frame but *where the switch sits and what the frame is for*. (1) The self holds the valve: unlike the lotus pond, "You and I have the power [...] to open or to close ourselves to this divine inflow exactly as we choose" (trine-infinite.04); "One need remain in no hell longer than he himself chooses to" (.07). (2) The Infinite is turned into a *law* that delivers on request ("like attracts like"), aimed at health, a job, money (.11). (3) James's footnote moves the greater Self inside: the spirit of the universe "is your own 'subconscious' self" (james-varieties.08). The frame is kept in form but becomes an instrument of the self's conditions. That is a third possibility between C4.19 (channel) and C5.14 (self-origin): **the self as the operator of an infinite power it does not own**.

**What it represses.** Evil, illness and death, explicitly. James names it a "deliberate religious policy" of excluding evil "from its field of vision" (.01); for Christian Science "evil is simply a _lie_" (.10); "disease should never be accepted" (.10); its failure "to accord to sorrow, pain, and death any positive and active attention" makes it "inadequate as a philosophical doctrine" (.12). Trine: "there is no such thing as death" (trine-infinite.10). The repression has a direct cost James does not stress but Trine shows: if every condition has its cause within, then the sick, the poor and the unemployed caused their own state, and to blame the draft is to be "too ignorant or too unfair to look their own weaknesses square in the face" (trine-infinite.09, .11). That is C7.1/C7.4 in 1897.

**Against.** (a) Trine keeps relation and answerability: "all life is one", selfishness is "at the root of all error" (.13); we are "stewards merely, and as stewards we shall be held accountable", and fencing off land as "his" is "absurd" (.12). Difference is not absolute. (b) James defends mind-cure as one valid "system of ideas" among several, none "exhaustive or exclusive" (james-varieties.11): exactly SPEC's healthy screen (C5.2), not a totalitarian one. In 1902 it is still one lens among many. (c) James finds an "element of truth" in its refusal to give evil a place in the whole (.13).

**America.** James names it as American ("poured over America"; "their only decidedly original contribution to the systematic philosophy of life", james-varieties.02) and lists "Emersonianism or New England transcendentalism" among its sources (.03). But he also gives five non-American sources (the Gospels, Berkeley, spiritism, evolutionism, Hinduism), calls it a reaction against the "chronic anxiety" of evangelical England and America, and says the healthy-minded type grows better in Catholic soil than in Protestant (.03, .04). This supports C6.1 (conditions, not cause) and complicates C6.4.

## Passages

### james-varieties.01 · *Varieties* IV–V — systematic healthy-mindedness "deliberately excludes evil from its field of vision"

- **Claims:** C5.7 (for), C5.1 (for), C5.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; `texts/james-varieties.txt` lines 2756–2769, 2789–2794
- **Checked:** text file

> If, then, we give the name of healthy‐mindedness to the tendency which looks on all things and sees that they are good, we find that we must distinguish between a more involuntary and a more voluntary or systematic way of being healthy‐minded. [...] Every abstract way of conceiving things selects some one aspect of them as their essence for the time being, and disregards the other aspects. Systematic healthy‐mindedness, conceiving good as the essential and universal aspect of being, deliberately excludes evil from its field of vision [...]
>
> Refuse to admit their badness; despise their power; ignore their presence; turn your attention the other way; and so far as you yourself are concerned at any rate, though the facts may still exist, their evil character exists no longer. Since you make them evil or good by your own thoughts about them, it is the ruling of your thoughts which proves to be your principal concern.

James describes Burke's terministic screen avant la lettre ("selects [...] and disregards"), and calls the deflection deliberate. But at this level the self makes only the *value* of the facts, not the facts ("though the facts may still exist"). That is a screen that knows it is one, not yet C5.3.

### james-varieties.02 · *Varieties* IV–V — "poured over America"; literature "mechanically produced for the market"; "their only decidedly original contribution"

- **Claims:** C6.1 (for), C5.14 (for: popular literature), C5.5 (for)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 2952–2971, 2992–2999, 3009–3013
- **Checked:** text file

> To my mind a current far more important and interesting religiously than that which sets in from natural science towards healthy‐mindedness is that which has recently poured over America and seems to be gathering force every day,—I am ignorant what foothold it may yet have acquired in Great Britain,—and to which, for the sake of having a brief designation, I will give the title of the “Mind‐cure movement.” [...] It has reached the stage, for example, when the demand for its literature is great enough for insincere stuff, mechanically produced for the market, to be to a certain extent supplied by publishers [...]
>
> The mind‐cure principles are beginning so to pervade the air that one catches their spirit at second‐hand. [...] Complaints of the weather are getting to be forbidden in many households; and more and more people are recognizing it to be bad form to speak of disagreeable sensations [...]
>
> The plain fact remains that the spread of the movement has been due to practical fruits, and the extremely practical turn of character of the American people has never been better shown than by the fact that this, their only decidedly original contribution to the systematic philosophy of life, should be so intimately knit up with concrete therapeutics.

A philosopher in 1902 names the movement American, popular, mass-marketed and already diffusing into manners ("bad form to speak of disagreeable sensations"). This is the strongest outside witness so far for C5.14's claim that the self-originating Self is a cultural event, first visible in popular literature. He explains it by a national trait ("practical turn of character"), which SPEC §6's guardrail tells us not to adopt.

### james-varieties.03 · *Varieties* IV–V — the six sources: "Emersonianism or New England transcendentalism" among them

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: lineage), C6.1 (for), C6.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 2973–2983; fn 44, lines 16700–16706
- **Checked:** text file

> One of the doctrinal sources of Mind‐cure is the four Gospels; another is Emersonianism or New England transcendentalism; another is Berkeleyan idealism; another is spiritism, with its messages of “law” and “progress” and “development”; another the optimistic popular science evolutionism of which I have recently spoken; and, finally, Hinduism has contributed a strain. But the most characteristic feature of the mind‐cure movement is an inspiration much more direct. The leaders in this faith have had an intuitive belief in the all‐saving power of healthy‐minded attitudes as such, in the conquering efficacy of courage, hope, and trust, and a correlative contempt for doubt, fear, worry, and all nervously precautionary states of mind.
>
> [fn 44] Mind‐cure might be briefly called a reaction against all that religion of chronic anxiety which marked the earlier part of our century in the evangelical circles of England and America.

James traces mind-cure to Emerson, but as one source of six, and only two of the six are American. Footnote 44 makes it a reaction *against* the Protestant inheritance (evangelical anxiety) on both sides of the Atlantic. The "contempt for doubt, fear, worry" is the root of the repression in .10.

### james-varieties.04 · *Varieties* IV–V — once-born souls "find a more congenial soil" in Catholicism; "Emerson himself is an admirable example"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (complicates), C4.19 (for: Emerson as once-born)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 2558–2564, 3518–3522
- **Checked:** text file

> In the Romish Church such characters find a more congenial soil to grow in than in Protestantism, whose fashions of feeling have been set by minds of a decidedly pessimistic order. But even in Protestantism they have been abundant enough; and in its recent “liberal” developments of Unitarianism and latitudinarianism generally, minds of this order have played and still are playing leading and constructive parts. Emerson himself is an admirable example.
>
> [...] Protestantism has been too pessimistic as regards the natural man, Catholicism has been too legalistic and moralistic, for either the one or the other to appeal in any generous way to the type of character formed of this peculiar mingling of elements.

C6.4 makes the Protestant inheritance a condition of the Self's growth in America. James says the healthy-minded temperament grows better in Catholic soil and that Protestant feeling is "pessimistic". On his account, mind-cure grows out of the *loosening* of Protestantism (Unitarian, liberal), not out of the Protestantism itself.

### james-varieties.05 · *Varieties* IV–V — "decidedly pantheistic": "through the subconscious part of it we are already one with the Divine"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C5.14 (against as written)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3109–3116
- **Checked:** text file

> Their notion of man’s higher nature is hardly less divergent, being decidedly pantheistic. The spiritual in man appears in the mind‐cure philosophy as partly conscious, but chiefly subconscious; and through the subconscious part of it we are already one with the Divine without any miracle of grace, or abrupt creation of a new inner man. As this view is variously expressed by different writers, we find in it traces of Christian mysticism, of transcendental idealism, of vedantism, and of the modern psychology of the subliminal self.

James's summary is Emerson's absorption (C4.19): God moved inside, no grace needed. The self is one with something larger, not prior to it. He then quotes Trine as the "central point of view" (3120–3152; see trine-infinite.03).

### james-varieties.06 · *Varieties* IV–V — "Thoughts are things": thoughts of success "will also be your outward portion"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C5.14 (for, with complicates: "influx")
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3343–3357
- **Checked:** text file

> This system is wholly and exclusively compacted of optimism: “Pessimism leads to weakness. Optimism leads to power.” “Thoughts are things,” as one of the most vigorous mind‐cure writers prints in bold type at the bottom of each of his pages; and if your thoughts are of health, youth, vigor, and success, before you know it these things will also be your outward portion. [...] Most mind‐curers here bring in a doctrine that thoughts are “forces,” and that, by virtue of a law that like attracts like, one man’s thoughts draw to themselves as allies all the thoughts of the same character that exist the world over. Thus one gets, by one’s thinking, reinforcements from elsewhere for the realization of one’s desires; and the great point in the conduct of life is to get the heavenly forces on one’s side by opening one’s own mind to their influx.

Here thought creates outward conditions (health, success), not just their value as in .01. That is C5.3's "origin of what it sees" in practical form. But the power is "reinforcements from elsewhere" and "influx": the self directs forces it does not originate, and the forces serve "one's desires".

### james-varieties.07 · *Varieties* IV–V (quoting Henry Wood) — "The soul’s real world is that which it has built of its thoughts"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C5.6 (for), C4.19 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3611–3617, 3626–3636; source fn 63 (Henry Wood, *Ideal Suggestion through Mental Photography*, 1899)
- **Checked:** text file

> “The soul’s real world is that which it has built of its thoughts, mental states, and imaginations. If we _will_, we can turn our backs upon the lower and sensuous plane, and lift ourselves into the realm of the spiritual and Real, and there gain a residence. [...] At such favorable seasons the outside world, with all its current of daily events, is barred out, and one goes into the silent sanctuary of the inner temple of soul to commune and aspire. [...] There is soul‐contact with the Parent‐Soul, and an influx of life, love, virtue, health, and happiness from the Inexhaustible Fountain.”

The plainest sentence in James for SPEC §5 ("the soul's real world is that which it has built of its thoughts"), with "the outside world [...] barred out". But the same paragraph ends in "soul-contact with the Parent-Soul" and influx. The world is self-built, but the builder is fed from above. (Wood, not Trine; check the original before using.)

### james-varieties.08 · *Varieties* IV–V — "Give up the feeling of responsibility [...] finding that a greater Self is there"; fn 54: "your own 'subconscious' self"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C4.19 (for), C7.1 (complicates), C7.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3424–3429, 3456–3462; fn 54, lines 16830–16833
- **Checked:** text file

> Passivity, not activity; relaxation, not intentness, should be now the rule. Give up the feeling of responsibility, let go your hold, resign the care of your destiny to higher powers, be genuinely indifferent as to what becomes of it all, and you will find not only that you gain a perfect inward relief, but often also, in addition, the particular goods you sincerely thought you were renouncing. [...]
>
> The mind‐curers have given the widest scope to this sort of experience. [...] It is but giving your little private convulsive self a rest, and finding that a greater Self is there.
>
> [fn 54] The pantheistic explanation (which is that of most mind‐curers) is by the merging of the narrower private self into the wider or greater self, the spirit of the universe (which is your own “subconscious” self), the moment the isolating barriers of mistrust and anxiety are removed.

The strongest "against" in James. Mind-cure's method, as he reads it, is the opposite of self-origination: surrender of the "little private" self to a "greater Self", as in Luther's justification by faith. It also *relieves* responsibility (against the surfeit of C7.1/C7.3). But footnote 54 locates the greater Self in "your own 'subconscious' self": the Over-Soul is moved inside the person, a step past Emerson.

### james-varieties.09 · *Varieties* IV–V — "your own personal thoughts are forces [...] the powers of the universe will directly respond to your individual appeals and needs"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3684–3699
- **Checked:** text file

> But here we have mind‐cure, with her diametrically opposite philosophy, setting up an exactly identical claim. Live as if I were true, she says, and every day will practically prove you right. That the controlling energies of nature are personal, that your own personal thoughts are forces, that the powers of the universe will directly respond to your individual appeals and needs, are propositions which your whole bodily and mental experience will verify. [...] Believing that a higher power will take care of us in certain ways better than we can take care of ourselves, if we only genuinely throw ourselves upon it and consent to use it, it finds the belief, not only not impugned, but corroborated by its observation.

The universe organized around "your individual appeals and needs" is the Self at the centre of its world. James calls it "primeval" and "savage" thought (3765). But the same paragraph again makes the power "higher" than the self and something one "throw[s] ourselves upon": a centre that depends.

### james-varieties.10 · *Varieties* IV–V — for Christian Science "evil is simply a _lie_"; fn 55: "disease should never be accepted"

- **Claims:** C5.7 (for), C7.1 (for), C1.3 (for)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3320–3337; fn 55, lines 16880–16884
- **Checked:** text file

> But philosophers usually profess to give a quasi‐logical explanation of the existence of evil, whereas of the general fact of evil in the world, the existence of the selfish, suffering, timorous finite consciousness, the mind‐curers, so far as I am acquainted with them, profess to give no speculative explanation. [...] It is Avidhya, ignorance! something merely to be outgrown and left behind, transcended and forgotten. Christian Science so‐called, the sect of Mrs. Eddy, is the most radical branch of mind‐cure in its dealings with evil. For it evil is simply a _lie_, and any one who mentions it is a liar. The optimistic ideal of duty forbids us to pay it the compliment even of explicit attention. Of course, as our next lectures will show us, this is a bad speculative omission [...]
>
> [fn 55] In mind‐cure circles the fundamental article of faith is that disease should never be accepted. It is wholly of the pit. God wants us to be absolutely healthy, and we should not tolerate ourselves on any lower terms.

Repression as doctrine, with a "duty" attached: "any one who mentions it is a liar"; "we should not tolerate ourselves on any lower terms". The ban on attention to evil is also a demand on the self: being ill becomes a failure to meet terms (C7.1).

### james-varieties.11 · *Varieties* IV–V — "the world can be handled according to many systems of ideas"; "neither is exhaustive"

- **Claims:** C5.2 (for), C5.3 (against, for mind-cure as James saw it), C5.1 (for)
- **Location:** Lectures IV–V; lines 3778–3790; fn 65, lines 16912–16914
- **Checked:** text file

> The obvious outcome of our total experience is that the world can be handled according to many systems of ideas, and is so handled by different men, and will each time give some characteristic kind of profit, for which he cares, to the handler, while at the same time some other kind of profit has to be omitted or postponed. Science gives to all of us telegraphy, electric lighting, and diagnosis, and succeeds in preventing and curing a certain amount of disease. Religion in the shape of mind‐cure gives to some of us serenity, moral poise, and happiness, and prevents certain forms of disease as well as science does, or even better in a certain class of persons. Evidently, then, the science and the religion are both of them genuine keys for unlocking the world’s treasure‐house to him who can use either of them practically. Just as evidently neither is exhaustive or exclusive of the other’s simultaneous use.
>
> [fn 65] What is certain now is the fact of lines of disparate conception, each corresponding to some part of the world’s truth, each verified in some degree, each leaving out some part of real experience.

James's verdict is SPEC §5's *healthy* Self, stated of mind-cure: one screen among many, each "leaving out some part of real experience". On his evidence, mind-cure in 1902 had not become the only admissible lens. Where C5.3 needs the moment a screen forgets it is one, James is the witness who insists it is one.

### james-varieties.12 · *Varieties* VI–VII — the verdict: "inadequate as a philosophical doctrine"; "failing [...] to accord to sorrow, pain, and death any positive and active attention"

- **Claims:** C5.7 (for), C1.3 (for), C5.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Lectures VI–VII ("The Sick Soul"); lines 5044–5054, 5086–5089; cf. 4325–4335
- **Checked:** text file

> The method of averting one’s attention from evil, and living simply in the light of good is splendid as long as it will work. It will work with many persons; it will work far more generally than most of us are ready to suppose; and within the sphere of its successful operation there is nothing to be said against it as a religious solution. But it breaks down impotently as soon as melancholy comes; and even though one be quite free from melancholy one’s self, there is no doubt that healthy‐mindedness is inadequate as a philosophical doctrine, because the evil facts which it refuses positively to account for are a genuine portion of reality; and they may after all be the best key to life’s significance, and possibly the only openers of our eyes to the deepest levels of truth. [...]
>
> [...] systematic healthy‐mindedness, failing as it does to accord to sorrow, pain, and death any positive and active attention whatever, is formally less complete than systems that try at least to include these elements in their scope.

The philosopher's verdict on the popular turn, and C5.7 almost word for word: the method drowns out "a genuine portion of reality", sorrow, pain and death. James is fair to it ("splendid as long as it will work"), so the verdict is not anti-individualist (SPEC §4 requirement). Cf. 4325–4335: "the very consecration of forgetfulness and superficiality".

### james-varieties.13 · *Varieties* VI–VII — evil as "a waste element [...] the very memory of it, if possible, wiped out"; and "an element of truth"

- **Claims:** C5.7 (for), C5.7 (complicates: James half-endorses the move)
- **Location:** Lectures VI–VII; lines 4111–4123, 4133–4134
- **Checked:** text file

> Now the gospel of healthy‐mindedness, as we have described it, casts its vote distinctly for this pluralistic view. [...] Evil, it says, is emphatically irrational, and _not_ to be pinned in, or preserved, or consecrated in any final system of truth. It is a pure abomination to the Lord, an alien unreality, a waste element, to be sloughed off and negated, and the very memory of it, if possible, wiped out and forgotten. The ideal, so far from being co‐extensive with the whole actual, is a mere _extract_ from the actual, marked by its deliverance from all contact with this diseased, inferior, and excrementitious stuff.
>
> [...] I believe that we shall have to admit it ourselves in the end as containing an element of truth.

"The very memory of it [...] wiped out" is repression in the strict sense. But James defends the *metaphysics* here (evil need not be part of the whole) while rejecting the *practice* in .12. The essay should not cite James as simply hostile.

### trine-infinite.01 · Epigraph and Ch. VIII — "Within yourself lies the cause of whatever enters into your life"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C5.3 (for), C5.6 (for)
- **Location:** title-page epigraph, `texts/trine-in-tune-with-the-infinite.txt` lines 54–58; Ch. VIII "Coming into Fullness of Power", lines 3049–3055
- **Checked:** text file

> _Within yourself lies the cause of whatever enters into your life. To come into the full realization of your own awakened interior powers, is to be able to condition your life in exact accord with what you would have it._
>
> [Ch. VIII] Within each one lies the cause of whatever comes to him. Each has it in his own hands to determine what comes. Everything in the visible, material world has its origin in the unseen, the spiritual, the thought world. This is the world of cause, the former is the world of effect. [...] What one lives in his invisible, thought world, he is continually actualizing in his visible, material world.

As close to SPEC §5's "all experience flows *from* Self" as any text in the project: the self is the cause, the world the effect. The epigraph names no God; it is the book's first sentence, and the one a buyer would read.

### trine-infinite.02 · Preface and Prelude — "Each is building his own world"; but "Within and above and below the human will incessantly works the Divine will"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C5.14 (complicates), C5.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Preface, lines 92–100, 116–123; Prelude, lines 165–171
- **Checked:** text file

> Each is building his own world. We both build from within and we attract from without. Thought is the force with which we build, for thoughts are forces. Like builds like and like attracts like. [...] The realm of the unseen is the realm of cause. The realm of the seen is the realm of effect.
>
> [...] There is a divine sequence running throughout the universe. Within and above and below the human will incessantly works the Divine will. To come into harmony with it and thereby with all the higher laws and forces, to come then into league and to work in conjunction with them, in order that they can work in league and in conjunction with us, is to come into the chain of this wonderful sequence. This is the secret of all success.
>
> [Prelude] Each is building his world from within, and the result of the building is determined by the point of view of each. The optimist, by his superior wisdom and insight, is making his own heaven, and in the degree that he makes his own heaven is he helping to make one for all the world beside.

The self-building world is stated and framed in the same preface: building is "in league" with a Divine will that works "within and above and below" the human. The Prelude also ties each self's heaven to "all the world beside": relation is kept.

### trine-infinite.03 · Ch. II "The Supreme Fact of the Universe" — the Over Soul named; man differs from God "in degree"; "the only limitations man has are the limitations he sets to himself"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C5.14 (against as written), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. II, lines 218–233, 282–287 (James quotes 218–233 at `james-varieties.txt` 3120–3134)
- **Checked:** text file

> This Spirit of Infinite Life and Power that is behind all is what I call God. I care not what term you may use, be it Kindly Light, Providence, the Over Soul, Omnipotence, or whatever term may be most convenient. [...]
>
> God, then, is this Infinite Spirit which fills all the universe with Himself alone, so that all is from Him and in Him, and there is nothing that is outside. [...] We are partakers of the life of God; and though we differ from Him in that we are individualized spirits, while He is the Infinite Spirit including us as well as all else beside, _yet in essence the life of God and the life of man are identically the same, and so are one_. They differ not in essence, in quality; they differ in degree.
>
> [...] And if the God-powers are without limit, does it not then follow that the only limitations man has are the limitations he sets to himself, by virtue of not knowing himself?

Direct evidence for the pressure point: Trine names "the Over Soul" and makes God the source of all; the individual is a drop of the same ocean (247–249). This is Emerson popularized, the frame kept. What is new is the last sentence: since the source is unlimited, every limit is self-set. That is the hinge to blame (.09, .11).

### trine-infinite.04 · Ch. III "The Supreme Fact of Human Life" — "a channel through which the infinite powers [...] can manifest"; but "the power within us to open or to close"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. III, lines 339–347, 444–450
- **Checked:** text file

> Mankind has not yet realized that the real self is one with the life of God. Through its ignorance it has never yet opened itself to the divine inflow, and so has never made itself a channel through which the infinite powers and forces can manifest. When we know ourselves merely as men, we live accordingly, and have merely the powers of men. When we come into the realization of the fact that we are God-men, then again we live accordingly, and have the powers of God-men. [...]
>
> There is this difference between the lotus pond and your life and mine. It has no power in itself of opening the gate to the inflow of the water from the reservoir which is its source. In regard to this it is helpless and dependent upon an outside agency. You and I have the power, the power within us, to open or to close ourselves to this divine inflow exactly as we choose. This we have through the power of mind, through the operation of thought.

"Channel" is Trine's own word (also 780). But he marks the difference from the pond himself: the pond is "dependent upon an outside agency", and we are not. The self does not make the water, but it alone works the gate. This is the half-step between C4.19 and C5.14.

### trine-infinite.05 · Ch. III — "creative power, not merely in a figurative sense, but creative power in reality"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C5.14 (for, with complicates: power is derived), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. III, lines 463–488
- **Checked:** text file

> In our very laboratory experiments we are demonstrating the great fact that thoughts are forces. They have form, and quality, and substance, and power, and we are beginning to find that there is what we may term a _science of thought_. We are beginning also to find that through the instrumentality of our thought forces we have creative power, not merely in a figurative sense, but creative power in reality. [...]
>
> The very universe in which we live is the result of the thought energies of God, the Infinite Spirit that is back of all. And if it is true, as we have found, that we in our true selves are in essence the same, and in this sense are one with the life of this Infinite Spirit, do we not then see that in the degree that we come into a vital realization of this stupendous fact, _we, through the operation of our interior, spiritual, thought forces, have in like sense creative power_?
>
> Everything exists in the unseen before it is manifested or realized in the seen, and in this sense it is true that the unseen things are the real, while the things that are seen are the unreal.

The claim C5.14 is looking for: thought is creative "in reality", the seen world is "unreal", effect of the unseen. But the argument runs *through* God: we create "in like sense" because God's thought made the universe. The self's creative power is a share of God's, and it borrows the authority of "science".

### trine-infinite.06 · Ch. III — Emerson, "who labored so faithfully and so fearlessly to bring about these very conditions"

- **Claims:** C4.19 (for: lineage claimed by the movement itself), C6.9 (complicates), C6.1 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. III, lines 636–645
- **Checked:** text file

> And in speaking of the great spiritual awakening that is so rapidly coming all over the world, the beginnings of which we are so clearly seeing during the closing years of this, and whose ever increasing proportions we are to witness during the early years of the coming century, I said, "How beautiful if Emerson, the illumined one so far in advance of his time, who labored so faithfully and so fearlessly to bring about these very conditions, how beautiful if he were with us today to witness it all! how he would rejoice!"

Trine claims Emerson as the prophet of New Thought, matching James's "Emersonianism" (james-varieties.03). Against C6.9 as written: here the *Over-Soul* half of Emerson is what survives in popular reception, not dropped.

### trine-infinite.07 · Ch. III and VI — "Thou shalt decree a thing"; "One need remain in no hell longer than he himself chooses to"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for, with complicates), C4.19 (for), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. III, lines 754–762; Ch. VI "Wisdom and Interior Illumination", lines 2750–2754
- **Checked:** text file

> The human will has its limitations. So far and no farther, says the law. The divine will has no limitations. It is supreme. All things are open and subject to you, says the law, and so, in the degree that the human will is transmuted into the divine, in the degree that it comes into harmony with and so, acts in conjunction with the divine, does it become supreme. Then it is that "Thou shalt decree a thing and it shall be established unto thee." The great secret of life and of power, then, is to make and to keep one's conscious connection with this Infinite Source.
>
> [Ch. VI] One need remain in no hell longer than he himself chooses to; and the moment he chooses not to remain longer, not all the powers in the universe can prevent his leaving it. One can rise to any heaven he himself chooses; and when he chooses so to rise, all the higher powers of the universe combine to help him heavenward.

The self "decrees" and "chooses" its heaven; "not all the powers in the universe" can stop it. That is answerable to nothing outside in practice. But the decree works only once the will is "transmuted into the divine", and the higher powers "help". The will is supreme on condition of not being merely one's own. (James quotes the Ch. VI sentence at `james-varieties.txt` 3149–3152.)

### trine-infinite.08 · Ch. IV "Fullness of Life—Bodily Health and Vigor" — "the builder and so the master of the body"; "he so orders them that they come into harmony with him"

- **Claims:** C5.6 (for), C5.8 (for), C5.7 (for: the body)
- **Location:** Ch. IV, lines 1102–1117
- **Checked:** text file

> The moment a person realizes his oneness with the Infinite Spirit he recognizes himself as a spiritual being, and no longer as a mere physical, material being. He then no longer makes the mistake of regarding himself as body, subject to ills and diseases, but he realizes the fact that he is spirit, spirit now as much as he ever will or can be, and that he is the builder and so the master of the body, the house in which he lives; and the moment he thus recognizes his power as master he ceases in any way to allow it the mastery over him. He no longer fears the elements or any of the forces that he now in his ignorance allows to take hold of and affect the body. The moment he realizes his own supremacy, instead of fearing them as he did when he was out of harmony with them, he learns to love them. He thus comes into harmony with them; or rather, he so orders them that they come into harmony with him.

C5.8 popularized: the self is spirit, prior to and "master" of the body and the elements. The self-correction "or rather, he so orders them that they come into harmony with him" turns harmony with the world into the world's harmony with the self: SPEC §5's metaphysical shift inside a single sentence.

### trine-infinite.09 · Ch. IV — "look within ourselves for the cause of whatever comes to us"; the draft as "scapegoat"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C7.4 (for), C5.7 (for: environment), C5.14 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. IV, lines 1075–1080, 1259–1266, 1290–1293
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] that all disease and its consequent suffering is the direct or the indirect result of the violation of law, either consciously or unconsciously, either intentionally or unintentionally.
>
> [...] No disease can enter into or take hold of our bodies unless it find therein something corresponding to itself which makes it possible. And in the same way, no evil or undesirable condition of any kind can come into our lives unless there is already in them that which invites it and so makes it possible for it to come. The sooner we begin to look within ourselves for the cause of whatever comes to us, the better it will be [...]
>
> Poor draft! How many thousands, nay millions of times it is made the scapegoat by those who are too ignorant or too unfair to look their own weaknesses square in the face, and who instead of becoming imperial masters, remain cringing slaves.

The cost of self-origination: environment is demoted to "occasion" and every illness has its cause in the sufferer. Blame of others for one's condition is called "unfair"; blame of oneself is required. This is C7.4 ("Failure is attributed mainly to the Self") and C7.1's guilt without limits, in 1897, from inside a religion of cheer.

### trine-infinite.10 · Ch. IV and VII — "expel all thoughts of death"; "there is no such thing as death"

- **Claims:** C5.7 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. IV, lines 1757–1760 (Trine quoting an unnamed physician); Ch. VII "The Realization of Perfect Peace", lines 2942–2946
- **Checked:** text file

> I would teach children early to build a strong barrier between themselves and disease, by healthy habits of thought, high thinking, and purity of life. I would teach them to expel all thoughts of death, all images of disease, all discordant emotions, like hatred, malice, revenge, envy, and sensuality, as they would banish a temptation to do evil.
>
> [Ch. VII] The loss of friends by the transition we call death will not cause sorrow to the soul that has come into this higher realization, for he knows that there is no such thing as death, for each one is not only a partaker, but an eternal partaker, of this Infinite Life.

Mortality repressed, in two forms: as a hygiene of attention (thoughts of death "expelled" like temptations) and as doctrine (death unreal, grief unnecessary). James's "inadequate" verdict (james-varieties.12) is aimed at exactly this.

### trine-infinite.11 · Ch. IX "Plenty of All Things—The Law of Prosperity" — "a man carries his success or his failure with him, and [...] it does not depend upon outside conditions"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C7.4 (for), C6.5 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. IX, lines 3701–3709, 3762–3769, 3875–3879
- **Checked:** text file

> He who lives in the realization of his oneness with this Infinite Power becomes a magnet to attract to himself a continual supply of whatsoever things he desires.
>
> If one hold himself in the thought of poverty, he will be poor, and the chances are that he will remain in poverty. If he hold himself, whatever present conditions may be, continually in the thought of prosperity, he sets into operation forces that will sooner or later bring him into prosperous conditions. [...]
>
> You in this way put your advertisement into a psychical, a spiritual newspaper [...]
>
> Faith, absolute dogmatic faith, is the only law of true success. When we recognize the fact that a man carries his success or his failure with him, and that it does not depend upon outside conditions, we will come into the possession of powers that will quickly change outside conditions into agencies that make for success.

The Infinite as a supply mechanism for "whatsoever things he desires", in the language of the market (magnet, advertisement, success). "Does not depend upon outside conditions" is C7.4 stated as law. "Absolute dogmatic faith" is Trine's phrase, not ours. A bridge to Bernays (C8.4): the self advertises itself to the universe.

### trine-infinite.12 · Ch. IX — "We are stewards merely, and as stewards we shall be held accountable"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C5.9 (complicates), C4.19 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. IX, lines 3942–3952, 4050–4055
- **Checked:** text file

> How foolish, then, for us to think that any material possessions _are ours_. How absurd, for example, for one to fence off a number of acres of God's earth and say they are _his_. Nothing is ours that we cannot retain. The things that come into our hands come not for the purpose of being possessed, as we say, much less for the purpose of being hoarded. They come into our hands to be used, to be wisely used. We are stewards merely, and as stewards we shall be held accountable for the way we use whatever is entrusted to us. That great law of compensation that runs through all life is wonderfully exact in its workings [...]
>
> Then not by hoarding but by wisely using and ridding ourselves of things as they come, an ever renewing supply will be ours, [...] In this way we not only come into possession of the richest treasures of the Infinite Good ourselves, but we also become open channels through which they can flow to others.

The answerability frame survives inside the prosperity chapter: stewardship, accountability, the "law of compensation" (an Emerson title), wealth as a channel "to others". The self that attracts its own supply is still not "answerable to nothing", so C5.14 cannot cite Trine for that phrase.

### trine-infinite.13 · Ch. V "The Secret, Power, and Effects of Love" — "we are all one with each other"; "selfishness is at the root of all error"

- **Claims:** C5.6 (against), C5.14 (against), C4.15 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. V, lines 1830–1837, 1859–1868
- **Checked:** text file

> This is the Spirit of Infinite Love. The moment we recognize ourselves as one with it we become so filled with love that we see only the good in all. And when we realize that we are all one with this Infinite Spirit, then we realize that in a sense we are all one with each other. When we come into a recognition of this fact, we can then do no harm to any one, to any thing. We find that we are all members of the one great body, and that no portion of the body can be harmed without all the other portions suffering thereby.
>
> [...] And then when we fully realize the fact that selfishness is at the root of all error, sin, and crime, and that ignorance is the basis of all selfishness, with what charity we come to look upon the acts of all. It is the ignorant man who seeks his own ends at the expense of the greater whole. [...] He is a seer, and recognizes the fact that he, a single member of the one great body, is benefited in just the degree that the entire body is benefited, and so he seeks nothing for himself that he would not equally seek for all mankind.

No singularity here: the self is "a single member of the one great body", and selfishness is the root error. Difference stays relational (SPEC §2). Mind-cure's self is causally powerful but not unique; it is the *same* divine life in everyone, as in Emerson and Whitman.
