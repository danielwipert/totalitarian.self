# Turner, *The Frontier in American History* (1920)

- **Edition used:** original English, New York, 1920 (essays dated 1893–1918; each essay's date is from its first footnote)
- **Text file:** `texts/turner-frontier.txt` (clean; Project Gutenberg #22994)
- **Citation form:** chapter and essay date, plus this file's line. Page numbers follow the footnote markers (e.g. [30:1] is p. 30).
- **Claims to test:** C6.3, C6.2, C6.1, C6.7, C6.8

**Read:** ch. I, "The Significance of the Frontier in American History" (1893), in full. Sampled: ch. IX "Contributions of the West to American Democracy" (1903), ch. X "Pioneer Ideals and the State University" (1910), ch. XI "The West and American Ideals" (1914), ch. XII "Social Forces in American History" (1910), ch. XIII "Middle Western Pioneer Democracy" (1918), and one passage of ch. V.

## Summary of the reading (2026-10-23)

Turner's frontier produces **individualism and democracy**, not "self-reliance" as such. The word self-reliance occurs once in the book for the frontier (.08, 1903); the 1893 essay's own terms are individualism, "antipathy to control" and democracy (.04, .06). He names the costs plainly: selfishness, lax civic spirit, the spoils system, wild-cat finance, waste (.05, .11).

But Turner's frontier is not the lone self-sufficient man of C6.3. In his own account it (a) made colonists *adopt* Indian ways or perish (.01), (b) bred intercolonial cooperation (.02), (c) called out the federal government's biggest powers through land, tariff and internal improvements (.03), (d) rested on the "family" (.04) and on dense voluntary association, the log rolling and the claim club (.16), and (e) on the later frontier needed capital and the state outright (.09, .12). And in 1910 he says the pioneer *brought* the ideal of individual competition with him from the East (.11): an ideology carried in, not just a survival requirement found there.

On C6.2 Turner is a strong witness for the break with Europe ("a gate of escape from the bondage of the past", .07), with his own caveat that "There is not _tabula rasa_".

On C6.7 and C6.8 Turner is the most useful text so far. After 1890 he sees frontier individualism split two ways: the farmers drop it for regulation and Populism (.12, .14), while the captains of industry carry "squatter doctrines" to "its logical result" in monopoly (.13, .15). The ideal survives its conditions (.14). Turner himself does not want it unchecked: he wants "civic power" to "dominate and utilize individual achievement" (.10).

## Passages

### turner.01 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — "The wilderness masters the colonist"

- **Claims:** C6.2 (for), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893); `texts/turner-frontier.txt` lines 272–294
- **Checked:** text file

> The frontier is the line of most rapid and effective Americanization. The wilderness masters the colonist. It finds him a European in dress, industries, tools, modes of travel, and thought. [...] In short, at the frontier the environment is at first too strong for the man. He must accept the conditions which it furnishes, or perish, and so he fits himself into the Indian clearings and follows the Indian trails. Little by little he transforms the wilderness, but the outcome is not the old Europe [...] The fact is, that here is a new product that is American. [...] Thus the advance of the frontier has meant a steady movement away from the influence of Europe, a steady growth of independence on American lines.

The nearest Turner comes to C6.3's survival language ("or perish"). But what survival demands here is not self-reliance: it is adapting to the land and borrowing from the Indians. Strong for C6.2: the frontier strips off the European inheritance.

### turner.02 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — the Indian frontier as "a cord of union"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893); lines 547–566
- **Checked:** text file

> The effect of the Indian frontier as a consolidating agent in our history is important. From the close of the seventeenth century various intercolonial congresses have been called to treat with Indians and establish common measures of defense. Particularism was strongest in colonies with no Indian frontier. This frontier stretched along the western border like a cord of union. The Indian was a common danger, demanding united action. [...] It is evident that the unifying tendencies of the Revolutionary period were facilitated by the previous cooperation in the regulation of the frontier.

In Turner's own account, the frontier's danger produces joint action and union, not lone self-sufficiency. Survival on the frontier was collective, organized through the colonies.

### turner.03 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — the frontier builds the national government

- **Claims:** C6.3 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893); lines 804–806, 822–824, 834–844
- **Checked:** text file

> The legislation which most developed the powers of the national government, and played the largest part in its activity, was conditioned on the frontier. [...] The pioneer needed the goods of the coast, and so the grand series of internal improvement and railroad legislation began, with potent nationalizing effects. [...] The public domain has been a force of profound importance in the nationalization and development of the government. [...] Administratively the frontier called out some of the highest and most vitalizing activities of the general government. [...] As frontier States accrued to the Union the national power grew.

Here is the pressure point in Turner's own words: the pioneer depended on the coast, on internal improvements and on federal land policy, and the frontier enlarged the state. The frontier thinned old European structures (C6.2) but built a new national one.

### turner.04 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — "productive of individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (for, as individualism), C6.2 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893), p. 30; lines 973–986
- **Checked:** text file

> But the most important effect of the frontier has been in the promotion of democracy here and in Europe. As has been indicated, the frontier is productive of individualism. Complex society is precipitated by the wilderness into a kind of primitive organization based on the family. The tendency is anti-social. It produces antipathy to control, and particularly to any direct control. The tax-gatherer is viewed as a representative of oppression. [...] The frontier individualism has from the beginning promoted democracy.

The core 1893 statement, verified: "antipathy to control" is exact. Note the order: democracy is the "most important effect", individualism its means. And the unit left standing is the family, not the lone self.

### turner.05 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — the costs: "strong in selfishness and individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (complicates), C6.1 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893), p. 32; lines 1023–1034
- **Checked:** text file

> So long as free land exists, the opportunity for a competency exists, and economic power secures political power. But the democracy born of free land, strong in selfishness and individualism, intolerant of administrative experience and education, and pressing individual liberty beyond its proper bounds, has its dangers as well as its benefits. Individualism in America has allowed a laxity in regard to governmental affairs which has rendered possible the spoils system and all the manifest evils that follow from the lack of a highly developed civic spirit. In this connection may be noted also the influence of frontier conditions in permitting lax business honor, inflated paper currency and wild-cat banking.

Turner names the costs himself, and in moral terms. Liberty pressed "beyond its proper bounds" is close to the essay's own picture of a Self without its checks (C6.1); but for Turner the cost is a weak civic spirit, not a weak community.

### turner.06 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — the list of frontier traits

- **Claims:** C6.3 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893), p. 37; lines 1159–1172
- **Checked:** text file

> From the conditions of frontier life came intellectual traits of profound importance. [...] The result is that to the frontier the American intellect owes its striking characteristics. That coarseness and strength combined with acuteness and inquisitiveness; that practical, inventive turn of mind, quick to find expedients; that masterful grasp of material things, lacking in the artistic but powerful to effect great ends; that restless, nervous energy;[37:1] that dominant individualism, working for good and for evil, and withal that buoyancy and exuberance which comes with freedom--these are traits of the frontier, or traits called out elsewhere because of the existence of the frontier.

The famous list, verified word for word. "Self-reliance" is not on it; resourcefulness ("quick to find expedients") and "dominant individualism, working for good and for evil" are. Turner presents these as traits of mind, not as a survival rule.

### turner.07 · "Significance of the Frontier" (1893) — "a gate of escape from the bondage of the past"

- **Claims:** C6.2 (for), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I (1893), lines 1180–1190
- **Checked:** text file

> But never again will such gifts of free land offer themselves. For a moment, at the frontier, the bonds of custom are broken and unrestraint is triumphant. There is not _tabula rasa_. The stubborn American environment is there with its imperious summons to accept its conditions; the inherited ways of doing things are also there; and yet, in spite of environment, and in spite of custom, each frontier did indeed furnish a new field of opportunity, a gate of escape from the bondage of the past; and freshness, and confidence, and scorn of older society, impatience of its restraints and its ideas, and indifference to its lessons, have accompanied the frontier.

Verified. Strong for C6.2, with Turner's own limit: "the inherited ways of doing things are also there", and the break lasts "For a moment". The environment's "summons" is again adaptation, not self-sufficiency.

### turner.08 · "Contributions of the West to American Democracy" (1903) — "grim energy and self-reliance"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (for), C6.2 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IX (1903); lines 8245–8261
- **Checked:** text file

> He had the essential traits of the Kentucky and Tennessee frontier. It was a frontier free from the influence of European ideas and institutions. The men of the "Western World" turned their backs upon the Atlantic Ocean, and with a grim energy and self-reliance began to build up a society free from the dominance of ancient forms.
>
> The Westerner defended himself and resented governmental restrictions. The duel and the blood-feud found congenial soil in Kentucky and Tennessee. [...] In a word, the unchecked development of the individual was the significant product of this frontier democracy. It sought rather to express itself by choosing a man of the people, than by the formation of elaborate governmental institutions.

The only place the book ties "self-reliance" to the frontier, and the strongest passage for C6.3 and for the "unchecked" Self. But it describes one frontier (Jackson's Kentucky and Tennessee), and Turner sets Lincoln's Northwest against it in the next pages.

### turner.09 · "Contributions of the West to American Democracy" (1903) — "social rather than individual"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (against, for the later frontier), C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. IX (1903); lines 8371–8396
- **Checked:** text file

> Individualism began to give way to cooperation and to governmental activity. Even in the earlier days of the democratic conquest of the wilderness, demands had been made upon the government for support in internal improvements, but this new West showed a growing tendency to call to its assistance the powerful arm of national authority. In the period since the Civil War, the vast public domain has been donated to the individual farmer, to States for education, to railroads for the construction of transportation lines. [...] But when the arid lands and the mineral resources of the Far West were reached, no conquest was possible by the old individual pioneer methods. Here expensive irrigation works must be constructed, cooperative activity was demanded in utilization of the water supply, capital beyond the reach of the small farmer was required. In a word, the physiographic province itself decreed that the destiny of this new frontier should be social rather than individual.

The reverse of C6.3 in Turner's own terms: the environment that "decreed" survival on the last frontier demanded cooperation, capital and the state. Even the earlier frontier asked the government for help.

### turner.10 · "Contributions of the West to American Democracy" (1903) — "civic power shall dominate and utilize individual achievement"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. IX (1903); lines 8650–8660, 8707–8712
- **Checked:** text file

> The problem of the United States is not to create democracy, but to conserve democratic institutions and ideals. In the later period of its development, Western democracy has been gaining experience in the problem of social control. [...] Its political tendencies, whether we consider Democracy, Populism, or Republicanism, are distinctly in the direction of greater social control and the conservation of the old democratic ideals. [...] Great and powerful as are the new sons of her loins, the Republic is greater than they. The paths of the pioneer have widened into broad highways. The forest clearing has expanded into affluent commonwealths. Let us see to it that the ideals of the pioneer in his log cabin shall enlarge into the spiritual life of a democracy where civic power shall dominate and utilize individual achievement for the common good.

Turner, the theorist of frontier individualism, ends by asking that it be checked. Against C6.7 as written: success did not remove every incentive to check the concept; its own prophet called for the check.

### turner.11 · "Pioneer Ideals and the State University" (1910) — "he brought the conception with him"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (against, on "not an ideology"), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. X (1910); lines 8809–8826, 8863–8868
- **Checked:** text file

> Besides the ideals of conquest and of discovery, the pioneer had the ideal of personal development, free from social and governmental constraint. He came from a civilization based on individual competition, and he brought the conception with him to the wilderness where a wealth of resources, and innumerable opportunities gave it a new scope. [...] The squatter enforced his claim to lands even against the government's title by the use of extra-legal combinations and force. He appealed to lynch law with little hesitation. He was impatient of any governmental restriction upon his individual right to deal with the wilderness. [...] Thus many of the pioneers, following the ideal of the right of the individual to rise, subordinated the rights of the nation and posterity to the desire that the country should be "developed" and that the individual should advance with as little interference as possible. Squatter doctrines and individualism have left deep traces upon American conceptions.

The sharpest single text against C6.3's wording: for Turner individualism is an *ideal* the pioneer carried in from the East, which free land gave "a new scope". It was an ideology, not only a survival need. Even the squatter acts through "combinations". The costs are named again: nation and posterity.

### turner.12 · "Pioneer Ideals and the State University" (1910) — "from the ideal of individualism to the ideal of social control"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against), C6.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. X (1910); lines 8970–8985, 9032–9034
- **Checked:** text file

> He began to lose his primitive attitude of individualism, government began to look less like a necessary evil and more like an instrument for the perpetuation of his democratic ideals. In brief, the defenses of the pioneer democrat began to shift, from free land to legislation, from the ideal of individualism to the ideal of social control through regulation by law. [...] He was coming to regard legislation as an instrument of social construction. The individualism of the Kentucky pioneer of 1796 was giving way to the Populism of the Kansas pioneer of 1896. [...] The pioneer of the arid regions must be both a capitalist and the protege of the government.

When the frontier closed, the pioneer himself, in Turner's account, dropped individualism for regulation. Against C6.7 for the farmers; the individualist line runs on elsewhere (.13, .15).

### turner.13 · "Pioneer Ideals and the State University" (1910) — the self-made man worked out "to its logical result"

- **Claims:** C6.8 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. X (1910); lines 9045–9053, 9060–9065
- **Checked:** text file

> It is profoundly significant that at the very time when American democracy is becoming conscious that its pioneer basis of free land and sparse population is giving way, it is also brought face to face with the startling outcome of its old ideals of individualism and exploitation under competition uncontrolled by government. Pioneer society itself was not sufficiently sophisticated to work out to its logical result the conception of the self-made man. But the captains of industry by applying squatter doctrines to the evolution of American industrial society, have made the process so clear that he who runs may read. [...] it seemed not impossible that the outcome of free competition under individualism was to be monopoly of the most important natural resources and processes by a limited group of men

The closest Turner comes to C6.8's mutation: a frontier ideal outlives its setting and, carried to its end, yields its opposite (monopoly). But the carrier is industrial capital, and the mutation is economic, not a dogma about the Self.

### turner.14 · "The West and American Ideals" (1914) — "sacrifice his ideal of individualism"; "Both must be preserved"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.8 (complicates), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XI (1914); lines 9828–9842, 9866–9890
- **Checked:** text file

> It was a new gospel, for the Western radical became convinced that he must sacrifice his ideal of individualism and free competition in order to maintain his ideal of democracy. [...] In a word, capital, labor, and the Western pioneer, all deserted the ideal of competitive individualism in order to organize their interests in more effective combinations. [...] But there was also the ideal of individualism. This democratic society was not a disciplined army, where all must keep step and where the collective interests destroyed individual will and work. Rather it was a mobile mass of freely circulating atoms, each seeking its own place and finding play for its own powers and for its own original initiative. We cannot lay too much stress upon this point, for it was at the very heart of the whole American movement. [...] Legislation is taking the place of the free lands as the means of preserving the ideal of democracy. But at the same time it is endangering the other pioneer ideal of creative and competitive individualism. Both were essential and constituted what was best in America's contribution to history and to progress. Both must be preserved

Both halves at once. In practice everyone "deserted" competitive individualism; as an ideal Turner calls it "at the very heart" of America and wants it kept. Supports C6.7's idea that the concept was validated and kept as an ideal after its conditions passed; but Turner pairs it with democracy, and he means it bounded by it.

### turner.15 · "Social Forces in American History" (1910) — "The self-made man has become, in popular speech, the coal baron"

- **Claims:** C6.8 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XII (1910); lines 10235–10243, 10245–10249
- **Checked:** text file

> In a word, the old pioneer individualism is disappearing, while the forces of social combination are manifesting themselves as never before. The self-made man has become, in popular speech, the coal baron, the steel king, the oil king, the cattle king, the railroad magnate, the master of high finance, the monarch of trusts. [...] At the same time the masters of industry, who control interests which represent billions of dollars, do not admit that they have broken with pioneer ideals. They regard themselves as pioneers under changed conditions

The pioneer word survives as the self-description of corporate power. That fits C6.8 (the idea hardens and changes its carrier) and gives an early case for §8's disguise mechanism; but Turner reports it as a claim, and treats it with doubt.

### turner.16 · "Middle Western Pioneer Democracy" (1918) — "the capacity for extra-legal, voluntary association"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIII (1918), p. 343; lines 10994–11021
- **Checked:** text file

> From the first, it became evident that these men had means of supplementing their individual activity by informal combinations. One of the things that impressed all early travelers in the United States was the capacity for extra-legal, voluntary association.[343:1] [...] This power of the newly arrived pioneers to join together for a common end without the intervention of governmental institutions was one of their marked characteristics. The log rolling, the house-raising, the husking bee, the apple paring, and the squatters' associations whereby they protected themselves against the speculators in securing title to their clearings on the public domain, the camp meeting, the mining camp, the vigilantes, the cattle-raisers' associations, the "gentlemen's agreements," are a few of the indications of this attitude. [...] These associations were in America not due to immemorial custom of tribe or village community. They were extemporized by voluntary action.

Turner's own late picture of frontier survival is cooperative: barn-raising, claim clubs, camp meetings. What is absent is the *inherited* community (C6.2 holds there), not community itself. Compare ch. V (line 5611–5613): "voluntary cooperation was more natural to the Southern Uplanders than action through the machinery of government". The footnote points to Tocqueville (C6.10).
