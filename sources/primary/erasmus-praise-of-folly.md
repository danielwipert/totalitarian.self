# Erasmus, *The Praise of Folly* (1511)

- **Edition used:** Wilson (1668)
- **Text file:** `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` (clean; Project Gutenberg #9371). The spelling in this file is modernised, not the 1668 spelling.
- **Citation form:** the *Moria* has no standard chapter numbers. Below, passages are named by topic and file line. For final citation, add the page in the Amsterdam edition (ASD IV-3, ed. C. H. Miller, 1979) and in a modern translation (Radice, Penguin; or Miller, Yale), and check Wilson's wording against the Latin.
- **Claims to test:** C4.21, C4.27; also bears on C4.15, C4.24, C4.31

**Reading rule.** The whole speech is spoken by Folly, praising herself, and it is ironic in layers. Erasmus says in the dedication that he has "praised folly, but not altogether foolishly" (.01). So a passage where Folly praises self-love is not Erasmus approving self-love. Each verdict below is read with the speaker in mind.

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

**Self-love is both mocked and made necessary, and the book will not say which.** Folly names Self-love (*Philautia*) her sister (.02) and makes it the "salt of life," without which no one can act with grace or bear another person (.03). She also makes vainglory the root that "laid the foundation of cities" (.06) and folly the "glue" of society (.05). That is the *form* of Mandeville's argument, two centuries early (C4.31). But it comes from Folly's mouth, and the same pages mock self-love as the vice of nobles, nations and scholars (.09). The book leaves it open whether self-love is a vice or something life cannot do without.

**There is no praise of human dignity here, and the self-exalting man is the main target.** Folly mocks man as the one creature that will not stay inside "those bounds that nature set them" (.08), and says "to err, mistake, and know nothing truly" is "to be a man." That is the opposite of Pico's self-shaping man (C4.24). It also counts against C4.21 if the Renaissance self is read as self-exaltation. The leading humanist of the North laughs at it.

**The final frame is Christian, and its highest good is losing the self.** The ending turns from irony to Paul's "fools for Christ's sake." The goal of the Christian life is that "the whole man is to be out of himself," "stripped of himself" (.13). Christ tells his followers "not [to] trust to their own wisdom but wholly depend on him" (.12). So the half of C4.27 that says "bounded" is supported. The half on "dignity" is not tested here, because the book does not praise dignity at all.

**Difference is folly.** Monks are mocked because they do not try to be like Christ "but how they may differ among themselves" (.11). Self-love's best gift is that it makes everyone content with his own lot, so that Nature "has made all equal" (.04). This supports C4.15: in 1511 difference is not a value.

**Free will.** It is barely touched. One aside mocks the schoolmen for distinguishing "charity infused" from "charity wrought in us by our own endeavors" (.10). That is mockery of the debate, not a position in it. Erasmus's position is in *De libero arbitrio* (1524), which is not in `texts/`.

## Passages

### erasmus-folly.01 · Dedication to More — "I have praised folly, but not altogether foolishly"

- **Claims:** C4.27 (complicates)
- **Location:** prefatory letter to Thomas More; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 116–120, 129–131
- **Checked:** text file

> For my own part, let other men judge of what I have written; though yet, unless an overweening opinion of myself may have made me blind in my own cause, I have praised folly, but not altogether foolishly. [...] whereas he that so taxes the lives of men, without naming anyone in particular, whither, I pray, may he be said to bite, or rather to teach and admonish? Or otherwise, I beseech you, under how many notions do I tax myself?

The author's key to the book: a jest with "serious matter," aimed at "the vices of all," himself included. Every passage below has to be read through this. Folly's praise is not Erasmus's view.

### erasmus-folly.02 · Folly's companions — *Philautia*, Self-love

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Folly's birth and household; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 304–307, 315–317
- **Checked:** text file

> this here, which you observe with that proud cast of her eye, is _Philautia_, Self-love; she with the smiling countenance, that is ever and anon clapping her hands, is _Kolakia_, Flattery [...] These, I say, are my household servants, and by their faithful counsels I have subjected all things to my dominion and erected an empire over emperors themselves.

Self-love is listed first among Folly's servants, beside Flattery, Laziness, Pleasure, Madness and Dead Sleep. The company she keeps places her as a folly, but one that rules "over emperors themselves."

### erasmus-folly.03 · Self-love the "salt of life"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (for), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** praise of Self-love; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 692–699, 706–723
- **Checked:** text file

> Will he, I pray, love anyone that hates himself? Or ever agree with another who is not at peace with himself? Or beget pleasure in another that is troublesome to himself? I think no one will say it that is not more foolish than Folly. And yet, if you should exclude me, there's no man but would be so far from enduring another that he would stink in his own nostrils, be nauseated with his own actions, and himself become odious to himself [...] unless this my friend and companion, Self-love, be present with it? Nor does she without cause supply me the place of a sister, since her whole endeavors are to act my part everywhere. For what is more foolish than for a man to study nothing else than how to please himself? To make himself the object of his own admiration? [...] Take away this salt of life, and the orator may even sit still with his action, the musician with all his division will be able to please no man, the player be hissed off the stage, the poet and all his Muses ridiculous, the painter with his art contemptible, and the physician with all his slip-slops go a-begging. [...] So necessary a thing it is that everyone flatter himself and commend himself to himself before he can be commended by others.

Both verdicts sit in one paragraph: self-love is "foolish" (to make oneself "the object of his own admiration"), and also "so necessary" that without it no art or friendship works. The engine-of-life reading (C4.31) is here in so many words. But note the reason given: self-love makes a man bearable to *others*. It serves society, not the self.

### erasmus-folly.04 · "willing to be what he is": self-love makes all equal

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for), C4.16 (complicates)
- **Location:** praise of Self-love; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 724–737
- **Checked:** text file

> Lastly, since it is the chief point of happiness "that a man is willing to be what he is," you have further abridged in this my Self-love, that no man is ashamed of his own face, no man of his own wit, no man of his own parentage, no man of his own house, no man of his manner of living, nor any man of his own country; so that a Highlander has no desire to change with an Italian, a Thracian with an Athenian, nor a Scythian for the Fortunate Islands. O the singular care of Nature, that in so great a variety of things has made all equal! Where she has been sometimes sparing of her gifts she has recompensed it with the more of self-love; though here, I must confess, I speak foolishly, it being the greatest of all other her gifts: to say nothing that no great action was ever attempted without my motion, or art brought to perfection without my help.

Self-love works here as contentment with one's own lot, and it *levels* difference: "in so great a variety of things has made all equal." People are content with their own face, wit and country, not proud of being unlike others. Note also "no great action was ever attempted" and no "art brought to perfection" without it. That is Cellini's world (see `cellini-autobiography.md`), described by a satirist.

### erasmus-folly.05 · Folly the glue of society

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C4.29 (for)
- **Location:** Folly and friendship, marriage, society; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 662–663, 682–689
- **Checked:** text file

> yet as ridiculous as they are, they make society pleasant, and, as it were, glue it together. [...] In fine, I am so necessary to the making of all society and manner of life both delightful and lasting, that neither would the people long endure their governors, nor the servant his master, nor the master his footman, nor the scholar his tutor, nor one friend another, nor the wife her husband, nor the usurer the borrower, nor a soldier his commander, nor one companion another, unless all of them had their interchangeable failings, one while flattering, other while prudently conniving, and generally sweetening one another with some small relish of folly.

Society holds together by shared weakness and mutual flattery, not by reason or virtue. It is a social self, made in the eyes of others (C4.29). It is not the separated individual of C4.31: society here is a web of "interchangeable failings," not a contract between prior individuals.

### erasmus-folly.06 · Vainglory "laid the foundation of cities"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (for, as precursor), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Folly as the source of great deeds; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 843–866
- **Checked:** text file

> what made the Decii devote themselves to the infernal gods, or Q. Curtius to leap into the gulf, but an empty vainglory, a most bewitching siren? [...] And yet from this root sprang all the great acts of the heroes which the pens of so many eloquent men have extolled to the skies. In a word, this folly is that that laid the foundation of cities; and by it, empire, authority, religion, policy, and public actions are preserved; neither is there anything in human life that is not a kind of pastime of folly. But to speak of arts, what set men's wits on work to invent and transmit to posterity so many famous, as they conceive, pieces of learning but the thirst of glory?

The closest passage to Mandeville: a vice ("empty vainglory") is the root of cities, empire and even "religion." It is Folly's paradox, made for laughs, not a theory of society. Mandeville takes the same paradox and argues it in earnest. Check `mandeville-fable.md` for any debt to Erasmus.

### erasmus-folly.07 · Life a comedy; "not to be wise beyond his condition"

- **Claims:** C4.25 (for), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** the world as a stage; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 920–925, 942–946
- **Checked:** text file

> And what is all this life but a kind of comedy, wherein men walk up and down in one another's disguises and act their respective parts, till the property-man brings them back to the attiring house. And yet he often orders a different dress, and makes him that came but just now off in the robes of a king put on the rags of a beggar. Thus are all things represented by counterfeit, and yet without this there was no living. [...] Whereas on the contrary 'tis the part of a truly prudent man not to be wise beyond his condition, but either to take no notice of what the world does, or run with it for company. But this is foolish, you'll say; nor shall I deny it, provided always you be so civil on the other side as to confess that this is to act a part in that world.

The self is a role, and the role is handed out (the "property-man" changes the dress). To strip the mask would "spoil all." Prudence means fitting one's "condition." There is no inner true self here waiting to be expressed. Supports the performance reading of C4.25, but as a part given, not a self fashioned.

### erasmus-folly.08 · "only man endeavors to exceed them": against the self-exalting man

- **Claims:** C4.24 (against), C4.27 (for), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** Folly against the philosophers; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 1053–1058, 1140–1145, 1155–1161
- **Checked:** text file

> But methinks I hear the philosophers opposing it and saying 'tis a miserable thing for a man to be foolish, to err, mistake, and know nothing truly. Nay rather, this is to be a man. And why they should call it miserable, I see no reason; forasmuch as we are so born, so bred, so instructed, nay such is the common condition of us all. [...] In like manner I can never sufficiently praise that Pythagoras in a dunghill cock, who being but one had been yet everything, a philosopher, a man, a woman, a king, a private man, a fish, a horse, a frog, and, I believe too, a sponge; and at last concluded that no creature was more miserable than man, for that all other creatures are content with those bounds that nature set them, only man endeavors to exceed them. [...] As therefore among men they are least happy that study wisdom, as being in this twice fools, that when they are born men, they should yet so far forget their condition as to affect the life of gods; and after the example of the giants, with their philosophical gimcracks make a war upon nature: so they on the other side seem as little miserable as is possible who come nearest to beasts and never attempt anything beyond man.

Pico's *Oration* (1486) praises man as the creature with no fixed place who can become anything. Here the creature who "had been yet everything" (Pythagoras) concludes that man is the most miserable, because "only man endeavors to exceed" his bounds. Folly is the speaker, so this is not Erasmus's plain view. But the humanist satirist makes self-exaltation the *butt*, not the ideal. This is the strongest "against" in either file for C4.21's picture of the Renaissance.

### erasmus-folly.09 · The self-love of scholars, artists and nations

- **Claims:** C4.21 (against), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** self-love by kinds of men; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 1482–1489, 1495–1498
- **Checked:** text file

> Or to what purpose is it I should mind you of our professors of arts? Forasmuch as this self-love is so natural to them all that they had rather part with their father's land than their foolish opinions; but chiefly players, fiddlers, orators, and poets, of which the more ignorant each of them is, the more insolently he pleases himself, that is to say vaunts and spreads out his plumes. [...] And now I consider it, Nature has planted, not only in particular men but even in every nation, and scarce any city is there without it, a kind of common self-love.

Here self-love is plain satire. Artists and performers are its chief cases. The Italians are mocked a few lines on as "the only masters of good letters." It is also "common": a trait of a whole nation or city, not of one exceptional man. The self-pleasing artist Cellini later describes himself as is, for Erasmus, a type to laugh at.

### erasmus-folly.10 · The theologians: grace "infused" or "wrought in us by our own endeavors"

- **Claims:** C4.27 (complicates)
- **Location:** Folly on the scholastic divines; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 1967–1973
- **Checked:** text file

> In like manner, the apostles press to us grace; but which of them distinguishes between free grace and grace that makes a man acceptable? They exhort us to good works, and yet determine not what is the work working, and what a resting in the work done. They incite us to charity, and yet make no difference between charity infused and charity wrought in us by our own endeavors.

The only place in the book that touches free will: whether good comes from grace or "our own endeavors." Erasmus does not take a side. He mocks the question as a schoolman's subtlety the apostles never needed. This is not enough to test the "free will" part of C4.27; *De libero arbitrio* is needed.

### erasmus-folly.11 · The monks: "how they may differ among themselves"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for), C4.27 (for)
- **Location:** Folly on the religious orders; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 2106–2109, 2116–2120, 2133–2137
- **Checked:** text file

> In a word, 'tis their only care that none of them come near one another in their manner of living, nor do they endeavor how they may be like Christ, but how they may differ among themselves. [...] as if it were not worth the while to be called Christians. And of these, a great part build so much on their ceremonies and petty traditions of men that they think one heaven is too poor a reward for so great merit, little dreaming that the time will come when Christ, not regarding any of these trifles, will call them to account for His precept of charity. [...] "Whence this new kind of Jews? I acknowledge one commandment, which is truly mine, of which alone I hear nothing. I promised, 'tis true, my Father's heritage, and that without parables, not to cowls, odd prayers, and fastings, but to the duties of faith and charity. Nor can I acknowledge them that least acknowledge their faults.

Seeking to "differ" is exactly the fault. The standard is likeness to Christ, and the common name "Christians." Each soul answers to Christ at judgment for one shared commandment, charity. That is answerability without difference, from a Catholic humanist six years before Luther's theses (C4.1, C4.13).

### erasmus-folly.12 · Christian folly: "wholly depend on him"

- **Claims:** C4.27 (for), C4.24 (against)
- **Location:** the Christian folly; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 2857–2869
- **Checked:** text file

> And Christ himself, that he might the better relieve this folly, being the wisdom of the Father, yet in some manner became a fool when taking upon him the nature of man, he was found in shape as a man; as in like manner he was made sin that he might heal sinners. Nor did he work this cure any other way than by the foolishness of the cross and a company of fat apostles, not much better, to whom also he carefully recommended folly but gave them a caution against wisdom and drew them together by the example of little children, lilies, mustard-seed, and sparrows, things senseless and inconsiderable, living only by the dictates of nature and without either craft or care. Besides, when he forbade them to be troubled about what they should say before governors and straightly charged them not to inquire after times and seasons, to wit, that they might not trust to their own wisdom but wholly depend on him.

Here the irony turns serious. The true folly is the cross, and its point is dependence: "not trust to their own wisdom but wholly depend on him." The book's frame is fully Christian, and against self-reliance.

### erasmus-folly.13 · The end: "the whole man is to be out of himself"

- **Claims:** C4.27 (for), C4.1 (for), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** the closing account of the beatific "madness"; `texts/erasmus-praise-of-folly.txt` lines 3041–3050
- **Checked:** text file

> And therefore, what is that life hereafter, after which these holy minds so pantingly breathe, like to be? To wit, the spirit shall swallow up the body, as conqueror and more durable; and this it shall do with the greater ease because heretofore, in its lifetime, it had cleansed and thinned it into such another nothing as itself. And then the spirit again shall be wonderfully swallowed up by the highest mind, as being more powerful than infinite parts; so that the whole man is to be out of himself nor to be otherwise happy in any respect, but that being stripped of himself, he shall participate of somewhat ineffable from that chiefest good that draws all things into itself.

The book's last word on happiness is the self's undoing: "out of himself," "stripped of himself," taken up into "the highest mind." This is the reverse of selfhood as a value. At its peak this Renaissance humanism makes the self something to leave behind, not to shape.
