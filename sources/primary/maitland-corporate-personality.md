# Maitland on corporate personality, *Collected Papers* vol. III (1911)

- **Edition used:** original English (OCR), ed. H. A. L. Fisher, Cambridge 1911. Page numbers are the printed ones.
- **Text file:** `texts/maitland-collected-papers-vol3.txt`
- **Claims to test:** C9.8, C4.10 (also bears on C5.3)
- **Essays read:** "The Corporation Sole" (1900, pp. 210–243, lines 8519–9895), "The Crown as Corporation" (1901, pp. 244–270, lines 9881–10950), "Moral Personality and Legal Personality" (Sidgwick Lecture 1903, pp. 304–320, lines 12173–12800), "Trust and Corporation" (1904, pp. 321–404, sampled: pp. 356–357, 367–371, 382–385, 400–403).
- **Reading note:** Maitland is a legal historian. Much of what he says reports doctrine (the canonist fiction theory, Coke, Gierke's real theory). Where he argues, he hedges: he never states Gierke's view as his own, and leaves the reality of the group's will to "the moral philosopher". The passages below say which is which.
- **Page headers:** where a quotation runs across a page break, the running head is left out and the new page is marked [p. NNN].

## Passages

### maitland.01 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," pp. 306–307 — the corporation as "another individual"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C4.10 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 306–307; `texts/maitland-collected-papers-vol3.txt` lines 12266–12310
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "knows/" corrected to "knows")

> Besides men or "natural persons," law knows persons of another kind. In particular it knows the corporation, and for a multitude of purposes it treats [p. 307] the corporation very much as it treats the man. Like the man, the corporation is (forgive this compound adjective) a right-and-duty-bearing unit. [...] In either case there stands opposite to you another right-and-duty-bearing unit—might I not say another individual?—a single "not-yourself" that can pay damages or exact them.

Reported orthodoxy. The law does model the corporation on the individual, but the model is a unit that bears rights and duties towards others, not a Self that is a source of value. The legal person here is forensic in Hobbes's and Locke's sense (a unit to which acts are attributed) with no consciousness in it at all.

### maitland.02 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," pp. 309–311 — fiction, "the gift of the prince," and the absolute individual

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against), C5.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 309–311; lines 12389–12461
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "I f" corrected to "If", "jthe" to "the")

> The group can be a person: co-ordinated, equiparated, with the man, with the natural person. With the "natural" person—for the personality of [p. 310] the universitas, of the corporation, is not natural—it is fictitious. [...] If the personality of the corporation is a legal fiction, it is the gift of the prince. [...] No longer can we see the body politic as communitas communitatum, a system of groups, each of which in its turn is a system of groups. All that stands between the State and the individual has but a derivative and precarious existence. [...] We may remember also how corporations appear to our absolutist Hobbes as troublesome entozoa. [...] [p. 311] That was one of the mottoes of modern absolutism: the absolute State faced the absolute individual.

Reported doctrine (Innocent IV, the canonists), then Maitland's own historical judgement. Under the fiction theory the corporation is the opposite of "prior to its environment": it exists only by the State's grant. The theory that admits only the individual and the State as real persons is canonist and absolutist, centuries older than the Self of §5, so the screen in which only the individual counts as a person (C5.3) has a rival, state-centred origin.

### maitland.03 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," pp. 312–313 — dividends tolerated, religious groups feared

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 312–313; lines 12497–12519
- **Checked:** text file

> for a long time past French law has afforded comfortable quarters for various kinds of groups, provided (but notice this) that the group's one and only object was the making of pecuniary gain. Recent writers have noticed it as a paradox that the State saw no harm in the selfish people who wanted dividends, while it had an intense dread of the comparatively unselfish people who would combine with some religious, charitable, literary, scientific, artistic purpose in view. [...] but personality—"civil personality," as they say in France—was only to be acquired with difficulty as the gift of the central government.

Reported, from French writers. The money-making company was the group that individualist law found harmless, because it could be read as a mere sum of selfish individuals. That fits C9.8's link between corporation and Self, but in reverse: the business group was tolerated because it was *not* treated as a person with ends of its own.

### maitland.04 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," pp. 314–315 — group-personality is "no purely legal phenomenon"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C5.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 314–315; lines 12558–12614
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; stray "y/" before "may" dropped)

> (2) Group-personality is no purely legal phenomenon. The law-giver may say that it does not exist, where, as a matter of moral sentiment, it does exist. When that happens, he incurs the penalty ordained for those who ignorantly or wilfully say the thing that is not. [...] (3) For the morality of common sense the group is person, is right-and-duty-bearing [p. 315] unit. Let the moral philosopher explain this, let him explain it as illusion, let him explain it away; but he ought not to leave it unexplained [...] there are many to tell us that the line of advance is no longer from status to contract, but through contract to something that contract cannot explain, and for which our best, if an inadequate, name is the personality of the organised group.

Maitland's own argument, but stated as what experience "has been making for" and carefully left open ("explain it as illusion"). The group's personality lives in the moral sentiment of those who deal with it: it is recognised from outside, by neighbours and creditors, not self-posited. An individual-only theory of persons "say[s] the thing that is not" here, which is a C5.3-type failure, though Maitland pins it on lawyers' dogma, not on the Self.

### maitland.05 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," p. 316 — "a real will of its own"? n + 1 persons

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 316; lines 12645–12661
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "n + i" corrected to "n + 1")

> Much disinclined though he may be to allow the group a real will of its own, just as really real as the will of a man, still he has to admit that if n men unite themselves in an organised body, jurisprudence, unless it wishes to pulverise the group, must see n + 1 persons. And that for the mere lawyer should I think be enough. [...] Still, we do not like to be told that we are dealing in fiction, even if it be added that we needs must feign, and the thought will occur to us that a fiction that we needs must feign is somehow or another very like the simple truth.

The key answer to the brief's question. Maitland does not claim the group has a will "as really real as the will of a man"; he says the lawyer needs only the n + 1 persons and can rest with a "phenomenal reality." His lean is towards realism, but it stays a lean. The corporation is one more person among persons, not a person prior to them.

### maitland.06 · "Moral Personality and Legal Personality," pp. 318–319 — Nusquamia's debt and the "ultimate and unanalysable moral unit"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (complicates), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 318–319; lines 12723–12765
- **Checked:** text file

> Who is it that really owes you money? Nusquamia. Granted, but can you convert the proposition that Nusquamia owes you money into a series of propositions imposing duties on certain human beings that are now in existence? The task will not be easy. [...] Nor, I think, shall we get much good out of the word "collectively," which is the smudgiest word in the English language, for the largest "collection" of zeros is only zero. I do not wish to say that I have suggested an impossible [p. 319] task, and that the right-and-duty-bearing group must be for the philosopher an ultimate and unanalysable moral unit: as ultimate and unanalysable, I mean, as is the man. [...] those who are to tell us of the very nature of things and the very nature of persons will not be discharging their duties to the full unless they come to close terms with that triumphant fiction, if fiction it be

Maitland's own question, and he explicitly declines to answer it ("I do not wish to say"). The test case is moral answerability: a debt owed by a group that cannot be shared out among its members. So the forensic person (C4.10) reaches beyond the individual; answerability, not selfhood, is what makes the group a person here.

### maitland.07 · "The Corporation Sole," pp. 211–212 — Gierke's real theory, and the question of a group will

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 211–212; lines 8572–8615
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "real'" corrected to "real", "Vrif" to "if")

> there are nowadays many who think that the personality of the corporation aggregate is in no sense and no sort artificial or fictitious, but is every whit as real and natural as is the personality of a man. [Note 2:] Dr Otto Gierke, of Berlin, has been its principal upholder. [...] Could we make that statement, then we might discuss the question whether the organized group of men has not a will of its own—a real, not a fictitious, will of its own—which is really distinct from the several wills of its members. As it is, however, the corporation sole stops, or seems to stop, the way. It prejudices us in favour of the Fiction Theory. We suppose that we personify offices.

Reported: the real theory belongs to Gierke and his school, and Maitland only says "we might discuss" a real group will. Note what the real theory claims: a will distinct from the members' wills, which is the nearest Maitland comes to C9.8's "singular Self". But it is the will of an "organized group of men", and Maitland's next move (maitland.08) makes plurality the mark of a true corporation.

### maitland.08 · "The Corporation Sole," pp. 225–226, 237 — the parson "bore the 'person'" of the church; persons of God and of "the policy of man"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 225–226, 237; lines 9135–9137, 9171–9174, 9644–9647
- **Checked:** text file

> Mystical theories break down: persons who can never be in the wrong are useless in a court of law. [...] The thought that the "parson" of a church was or bore the "person" of the church was probably less distant from them than it is from us, for the two words long remained one word for the eye and for the ear. [...] [p. 237] "persons natural created of God, as J. S., J. N., &c, and persons incorporate and politic created by the policy of man"

Reported (Coke's classification, quoted by Maitland). Two points for C4.10 and hobbes-leviathan.09: the legal person is one who can be "in the wrong" (answerable), and "person" is something an office-holder *bears* for another, as in Hobbes's representer. Coke's line between persons "created of God" and persons made "by the policy of man" is the orthodox frame in which only the individual is a natural person.

### maitland.09 · "The Corporation Sole," pp. 238, 241–243 — the singular corporation is "natural man or juristic abortion"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** pp. 238, 241–243; lines 9660–9664, 9786–9795, 9837–9838, 9864–9866
- **Checked:** text file

> after all, Coke's corporation sole is a man: a man who fills an office and can hold land "to himself and his successors," but a mortal man. [...] [p. 241] When we turn from this mere ghost of a fiction to a true corporation, a corporation aggregate, surely the main phenomenon that requires explanation, that sets us talking of personality and, it may be, of fictitious personality, is this, that we can conceive and do conceive that legal transactions, or acts in the law, can take place and do often take place between the corporation of the one part and some or all of the corporators of the other part. [...] [p. 242] English law has liked its persons to be real. [...] [p. 243] the ecclesiastical corporation sole is no "juristic person"; he or it is either natural man or juristic abortion.

Maitland's own argument, and the strongest result against C9.8 as worded. For him what makes a corporation a person is internal plurality: it can deal with its own members as another party. A corporation that is "singular" (one office, one man) is not a person at all. "The organization as a singular Self" is, in Maitland's terms, the abortion.

### maitland.10 · "The Crown as Corporation," p. 248 — the body absorbed by its head (Henry VIII and Hobbes)

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 248; lines 10063–10070
- **Checked:** text file

> The medieval dualism of Church and State is at length transcended by the majestic lord who broke the bonds of Rome. The frontispiece of the Leviathan is already before our eyes. But, as for Hobbes, so also for King Henry, the personality of the corporate body is concentrated in and absorbed by the personality of its monarchical head.

The closest Maitland comes to C9.8's picture: a body whose personality is swallowed by one person at its head, and he names Hobbes. But it is the State, not a corporation, and Maitland treats it as the error that led English law astray. If C9.8 wants a source here, the "singular Self" is the Hobbesian sovereign, not the trading company.

### maitland.11 · "The Crown as Corporation," pp. 259, 268 — head of "a corporation aggregate of many"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** pp. 259, 268; lines 10483–10492, 10850–10854
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; hyphenation "Common- wealth" joined)

> The way out of this mess, for mess it is, lies in a perception of the fact, for fact it is, that our sovereign lord is not a "corporation sole," but is the head of a complex and highly organized "corporation aggregate of many"—of very many. I see no great harm in calling this corporation a Crown. But a better word has lately returned to the statute book. That word is Commonwealth. [...] [p. 268] All this [...] seems to me to be the outcome of an awkward endeavour to ignore the personality of the greatest body corporate and politic that has ever existed.

Maitland arguing in his own voice ("for fact it is"). His positive model of a corporate person is a commonwealth: head and members together, the head not absorbing the body. That is a group person bounded by its members, the opposite of a singular Self.

### maitland.12 · "Trust and Corporation," pp. 367–369 — protection "against the attacks of inadequate and individualistic theories"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (complicates), C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** pp. 367–369; lines 14614–14652, 14695–14699
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "Genosseuschaft" corrected to "Genossenschaft"; check_quotes scores 0.83 on that short sentence because of this one fix, read by eye)

> It has served to protect the unincorporated Genossenschaft against the attacks of inadequate and individualistic theories. [...] [p. 368] Our Anstalt, or our Genossenschaft, or whatever it may be, has to live in a wicked world: a world full of thieves and rogues and other bad people. And apart from wickedness, there will be unfounded claims to be resisted: claims made by neighbours, claims made by the State. This sensitive being must have a hard, exterior shell. [...] [p. 369] the thought of a "jurisdiction" inherent in the Genossenschaft is strong in us, and I believe that it is at its strongest where there is no formal corporation.

Maitland's own judgement. The "individualistic" theory of persons is the enemy of group life (the C5.3-type point), but the group he defends is vulnerable, surrounded by neighbours and the State, not prior to its environment. Compare "Moral Personality," p. 317 (line 12675): the trust shielded bodies "from the assaults of individualistic theory."

### maitland.13 · "Trust and Corporation," pp. 382–383 — the club a "living being," the joint-stock company "a sort of machine"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** pp. 382–383; lines 15226–15233
- **Checked:** text file

> The natural inclination of the members of an English [p. 383] club would, so I think, be to treat the case exactly as if it were a case of corporate liability. It has often struck me that morally there is most personality where legally there is none. A man thinks of his club as a living being, honourable as well as honest, while the joint-stock company is only a sort of machine into which he puts money and out of which he draws dividends.

Maitland's own observation. For him the business corporation is the *least* person-like group: its members see it as a dividend machine, not a self. If C9.8 is about the commercial corporation, Maitland's account of personality points the other way: personality is strongest in the unincorporated moral community.

### maitland.14 · "Trust and Corporation," pp. 402–403 — wielders of power as trustees, "nur in fremdem Interesse"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** pp. 402–403; lines 15980–16000, 16014–16020
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "Rechts- trager" joined, umlaut lost in OCR and left as is; "it fs imposing" corrected to "it is imposing"; check_quotes scores 0.88, read by eye)

> The trustee, "der zwar Rechtstrager aber nur in fremdem Interesse ist" is well known to all of us, and he becomes a centre from which analogies radiate. [...] their governmental powers should be regarded as being held in trust. Those powers are, we say, "intrusted to them," or they are "intrusted with" those powers. [...] Not content, however, with permeating this region, the Trust presses forward until it is imposing itself upon all wielders of political power, upon all the organs of the body politic.

Reported English usage, with Maitland's comment. The quoted German says the trustee bears rights, but only in another's interest. The dominant English picture of corporate and political bodies is fiduciary: they hold powers for others. That is the reverse of a body "from which all value is imagined to flow."

## Findings

- **Is the corporation a Self?** Not in Maitland's account. He reports two theories: the fiction theory (the corporation is the State's creature, "derivative and precarious") and Gierke's real theory (a real group-person with a will distinct from its members'). He leans towards the second without asserting it (maitland.05, .06, .07). On either theory the corporation is bounded: by the State's grant, or by its members, its neighbours and the moral sentiment that recognises it.
- **"Singular" is the weak word in C9.8.** For Maitland the mark of a corporate person is internal plurality (maitland.09); a singular corporation is "natural man or juristic abortion." His model is the commonwealth, head and members together (maitland.11). The one picture of a body swallowed by a single person is the Hobbesian sovereign (maitland.10), which he rejects.
- **The business company is the least person-like group** (maitland.03, .13). Maitland's account gives no support to reading the commercial corporation in particular as a Self.
- **C5.3-type point, partly confirmed:** the theory that admits only individuals (and the State) as persons misdescribes groups (maitland.02, .04, .12). But Maitland traces it to canonist dogma and state absolutism ("the absolute State faced the absolute individual"), not to a Self-centred culture.
- **C4.10:** Maitland's legal person is forensic, a "right-and-duty-bearing unit," with nothing about consciousness; it extends to groups and to persons borne by office-holders (maitland.01, .06, .08). This leaves the reading of Locke intact, but shows the legal tradition's forensic person was never only individual.
