## Summary of the reading (2026-10-27)

**C5.14 stage 3 (is the self answerable to nothing?). Verdict: for on answerability, more plainly than Byrne; for on absorption, but in a third form: the frame is absorbed by *indifference*, not by declared identity.** (1) *Answerability.* No outside authority survives. "I now take orders only from my own Knowing" (doyle.10); "no one else in the world knows what I should do. The experts don't know, the ministers [...] Not even the folks who love me the most" (doyle.12); "without asking permission or offering explanation" (doyle.12); right and wrong are "not real", just "culturally constructed, artificial, ever-changing cages created to maintain institutions" (doyle.07); her memos "are neither right nor wrong; they are just mine" (doyle.20). (2) *What the final authority is.* The Knowing. Its relation to God and the self is stated three ways, and the text does not choose. (a) *God as indweller and agent*: "God lives in this deepness inside me. When I recognize God's presence and guidance, God celebrates" (doyle.09); the Knowing is "a loving, playful guide" and "We are learning to trust each other" (doyle.10). This keeps a second party. (b) *God as inner experience*: "God is not a being outside of me: God is the fire, the nudge, the warm liquid gold swelling and pressing inside me" (doyle.13). God is identified not with the self as such but with a feeling inside it. (c) *Self, and it does not matter*: to Abby's "Aren't you just talking to yourself down there?" she answers "Maybe", and if so "That is certainly enough of a miracle for me"; "It doesn't matter what we call our Knowing" (doyle.11). So the decisive move is not Byrne's "You are God in a physical body" but a shrug: God or self, the function is the same, and the function is guidance that takes orders from no one outside. (3) *Identity as goal, not fact.* The nearest she comes to Byrne is "so that one day I can say: I and the Mother are one" (doyle.20), the Gospel saying that Trine also puts at the end of the road ("I and the Father are one", Trine, `texts/trine-in-tune-with-the-infinite.txt` line 4493). For Doyle it is still a future, reached by burning "all that separates me from the Knowing"; the epilogue's "I am" (doyle.25) is the nearest she comes to claiming it now, and even that is framed under the title "Human". **Better wording for this strand:** *the frame is internalized and its otherness made optional*. Whether there is a God other than the self is left open and declared not to matter; the self is answerable only to its own depth.

**Comparison with Byrne (2006) and Brown (2012). Which strand?** Neither, quite: a third. *With Byrne:* the same answerability (none outside the self), the same rival-frames-absorbed move (shalom, nirvana, heaven and salaam all name "the unseen order inside us", doyle.14; compare byrne.03), the same name-to-function shift ("Call it whatever you want", byrne.15; Doyle's friend calls it "Sebastian", doyle.11). *Unlike Byrne:* no law that obeys, no Genie, no wealth; limits on the self are kept (pain cannot be escaped, doyle.05; the Knowing overrules "surface" desires, doyle.21); social causes are named (violence, the "1 percent", "consumer culture", doyle.05, .14). *With Brown:* social causes, accepted pain, and one key moment of wisdom given by another person (the recovery meeting, doyle.04). *Unlike Brown:* others are not the condition of courage; their advice is noise (doyle.07, .12), and the vow is to the self, "We'll forsake all others to remain whole" (doyle.19). Brown's self is bound to others for support and declares its own worth; Doyle's self declares its own worth ("I was born complete", doyle.19) **and** its own law, and treats others as people to be modelled for and freed, not consulted. Call it the **inner-oracle strand**: limited by nature, open to social causes, answerable to nothing but its own Knowing.

**C1.3 (the Self suppresses truths) and C6.13 (checks).** **For, as a text, on the checks; against on social facts.** Every shared check is named as a cage: feelings, body, beliefs, whom to love, whom to fear (doyle.02); "others' expectations, cultural mandates, and institutional allegiances" (doyle.03); religion, family structure, marriage till death, "selflessness" (doyle.17–.19); and the test is simple: if "the truth inside us can burn a belief, a family structure, a business, a religion, an industry—it should have become ashes yesterday" (doyle.17). That is C6.13's checks seen from the far end: not redirected but burned. But C1.3's sharper claim, that the frame cannot see social facts, is **against** for this text: violence, school shootings, hoarding wealth, migrant mothers (doyle.14), consumer culture (doyle.05) and the American experience "of people who don't look like me" (doyle.20) are all seen. What the excerpt does not weigh is the cost of burning to the others in the structure (the ex-husband and children appear only as "three children who adore their father", line 347); that silence may be a C1.3 case, but it cannot be tested on this excerpt.

**Natural or made?** **Both, unresolved.** The "wild" is given: the cheetah born wild in captivity (doyle.01), "the girl I was before the world told me who to be" (doyle.03), "the blueprint she'd been born with [...] her original plan" (line 441), "I was born complete" (doyle.19). It is also made: love "custom made for me, by me", "a new identity by design instead of default" (doyle.02), "What if I did choose my sexuality" (doyle.23), memos "written in sand" (doyle.20), "perpetual becoming" (line 496). The self is found and then designed, and the found self authorizes the design.

**C8.2, C8.6 (conformity felt as the self).** **Complicates.** The word "self-expression" does not occur in the excerpt. The idiom is wild/caged, "true and beautiful", "Here I Am", "full of themselves". Doyle sees conformity in others' idiom and in desire: consumer culture's "countertops, her thighs, these jeans" (doyle.05), social-media pictures of families at the shore driving a "surface desire" (doyle.21), and "Sometimes we say things that we believe are loving but actually reveal our conditioning" (doyle.23). So, like Brown, she uses the authenticity idiom against conformity. But she cannot see it in her own idiom: the inner voice is treated as uncaused ("came from within", doyle.03), and the list of what "women tell me they want" (lines 515–547) is strikingly uniform even as she marvels at "how wildly different each of their stories is" (doyle.15). That is C8.2's point exactly, but the text only shows it; a carrier cannot prove it.

**C7.4 (blame).** **Complicates; against the claim as written.** The cause of the lost self is the culture: "the world sat me down" (doyle.02), being "tamed by shame" (doyle.03), "dogma that men laid down ages ago to keep their power" (doyle.13). Social evil is named (doyle.14). Yet the cure and the responsibility are personal: suffering "is what happens when we avoid pain", from "Having such little faith in myself" (doyle.06); "I forgave myself for believing that lie" (doyle.18). Cause social, cure personal, as in Brown (brown.12, .15). Not the self as sole cause (Byrne, Smiles).

**C9.1 (one final source, no rival frame).** **For.** One source: the Knowing, on every decision "a monumental or tiny decision" (doyle.10). Rivals are absorbed, not refuted: "every great spiritual teacher tells us the same story" (line 231), and Buddha, Moses, Westley and Jesus are lined up as one lesson (lines 233–239); the religions' names for peace are one "unseen order inside us" (doyle.14); "A God by any other name is an equal miracle" (doyle.11). Scripture is reused and cut to fit: "Be still and know" with the object dropped (doyle.08). Complication: the Knowing is "beyond and beneath language" (doyle.12) and "never reveals a five-year plan" (doyle.10), so the one source does not issue a doctrine; it issues nudges, one at a time. And "Girl Gods" treats God as beyond all categories (doyle.24).

**The §4 pattern.** **For, vividly.** Luther's no-mediator faith is kept, its object turned inward: "I stopped believing in middlemen or hierarchy between me and God", faith "a private surrender to the inner Knowing" (doyle.20). The burning bush that is not consumed becomes "I am fireproof" (doyle.05); the crucifixion and resurrection become "I resurrect myself every day" (doyle.06); Eve becomes "our model" (doyle.22); the Psalm's stillness becomes a method for consulting the self (doyle.08); the divine "I am" becomes her answer (doyle.25). In each case the structure is kept and the giver absorbed or made optional.

**Against or limiting evidence found (moderate, stronger than Byrne, weaker than Brown).** (1) The recovery meeting: the first truth comes from another person, in a church basement, among "the first people I trusted with all of me" (doyle.04). (2) Pain as a limit: "I'd never be free from pain" (doyle.05); Danielle's "heartbreaking" story as the truest (doyle.16). (3) The Knowing as a check on desire (doyle.21). (4) Others' good as a stated end: "Selfless women make for an efficient society but not a beautiful, true, or just one" (doyle.18); a family where each is "both held and free" (doyle.19); "I want joy and safety for my children and for everyone else's children" (line 529). (5) God as other: "God celebrates", "learning to trust each other" (doyle.09, .10), and God beyond gender (doyle.24). (6) Uncertainty kept: Abby's question stands unanswered ("Maybe", doyle.11), and faith moves "from certain and defensive to curious, wide-eyed, and awed" (doyle.20). None of these is a judge outside the self; (1) and (5) come closest.

- **Location:** "Feel"; lines 223, 229
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> What I thought would kill me, didn't. [...] I learned that I'd never be free from pain but I could be free from the fear of pain, and that was enough. I finally stopped avoiding fires long enough to let myself burn, and what I learned was that I am like that burning bush: The fire of pain won't consume me. I can burn and burn and live. I can live on fire. I am fireproof.
>
> [...]
>
> Consumer culture promises us that we can buy our way out of pain—that the reason we're sad and angry is not that being human hurts; it's because we don't have those countertops, her thighs, these jeans. ### doyle.07 · "Know" — "Why do I trust everyone else on Earth more than I trust myself?"; right and wrong "not real"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C1.3 (for, as a text), C6.13 (for, as a text: religion, morality, family as cages), C9.1 (for)
- **Location:** "Know"; lines 266, 268, 270
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> I stared at that question and thought: Well. I have hit some sort of new rock bottom. I've just asked the internet to make the most important and personal decision of my life. Why do I trust everyone else on Earth more than I trust myself? WHERE THE HELL IS MY SELF? When did I lose touch with her?
>
> I clicked on article after article anyway. Distressingly, everyone thought I should do something different. The religious experts insisted that a good Christian would stay. Feminists argued that a strong woman would leave. Parenting articles preached that a good mother thinks only of what is best for her children. [...] When a woman finally learns that pleasing the world is impossible, she becomes free to learn how to please herself.
>
> I looked at all of those contradictory opinions and thought: If there is, in fact, an objective right or wrong way to handle this, why do all of these people have such different ideas about what a person should do? I had an epiphany: It must be that should and shouldn't, right and wrong, good and bad—they're not wild. They're not real. They're just culturally constructed, artificial, ever-changing cages created to maintain institutions. It struck me that in every family, culture, or religion, ideas of right and wrong are the hot cattle prods, the barking sheepdogs that keep the masses in the herd. They are the bars that keep us caged.

The key move of stage 3 stated as an argument: disagreement among authorities proves there is no right or wrong, so morality is "culturally constructed" and only the self is left. Church, feminism and parenting advice are demoted together; the children's good appears only as one more opinion ("Parenting articles preached"). This is C5.14's "answerable to nothing" in so many words.

### doyle.09 · "Know" — "It was a Knowing"; "God lives in this deepness inside me"

- **Location:** "Know"; lines 286, 288, 290, 292
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Eventually I sank deep enough to find a new level inside me that I'd never known existed. This place is underneath; low, deep, quiet, still. There are no voices there, not even my own. All I can hear down there is my breath. [...]
>

>

The nudge guides me toward the next precise thing, and then, when I silently acknowledge the nudge—it fills me. The Knowing feels like warm liquid gold filling my veins and solidifying just enough to make me feel steady, certain.

The exact wording matters. God "lives in" the deep, which is "not even my own" voice: an indweller, not yet the self. God acts ("celebrates", "flooding me") and guides. This is closer to Trine's inner divine channel (trine-infinite.04) than to Byrne's "You are God in a physical body" (byrne.17). The test of truth, however, is a feeling: "steady, certain".

### doyle.11 · "Know" — "Aren't you just talking to yourself down there?" "Maybe."; "It doesn't matter what we call our Knowing"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for: the frame made optional; the decisive passage for stage 3), C9.1 (for: rivals absorbed by name)
- **Location:** "Know"; lines 304, 306
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> When I talk like this, my wife raises her eyebrow and asks, "Aren't you just talking to yourself down there?" Maybe. If what I've found in the deep is just my self—if what I've learned is not how to commune with God but how to commune with myself—if who I have learned to trust is not God but myself—and if, for the rest of my life, no matter how lost I get, I know exactly where and how to find myself again—well, then. That is certainly enough of a miracle for me.
>
> Why do we worry about what to call the Knowing, instead of sharing with each other how to call the Knowing? I know many people who have found this level inside them and live solely by it. Some call the Knowing God or wisdom or intuition or source or deepest self. I have a friend with some serious God issues, and she calls it Sebastian. A God by any other name is an equal miracle and relief. It doesn't matter what we call our Knowing. What matters—if we want to live our singular shooting star of a life—is that we call it.

The answer to the brief's question. God is not asserted to be the self (Byrne) nor kept as other: the difference is declared not to matter. "Not God but myself" is "enough of a miracle". Compare Byrne's "Call it whatever you want" (byrne.15): the name goes, the function stays. Against: the doubt is put in the mouth of the person she loves most, and left standing.
