## Summary of the reading (2026-10-26)

**C5.14 stage 3 (is the self answerable to nothing?). Verdict: against, with one complication.** Stage 3 does **not** reach this mainstream 2012 bestseller in the form found in Byrne. (1) *No cosmic frame, and none absorbed.* There is no Universe, law or God to identify with: "God" occurs once, as an exclamation (line 469). Faith and spirituality are named (guidepost "Trusting Faith", line 112; "spiritual lives", line 327; "Having faith", line 401) but only as things vulnerability opens onto. (2) *The frame that remains is human nature and the data.* "We are hardwired to connect with others" (brown.03), love and belonging are "irreducible needs" (brown.06), vulnerability is "not optional" (brown.01) and "there is no 'get out of vulnerability free' card" (brown.20). These are limits on the self, stated as findings, and the self cannot opt out of them. (3) *The self is bound to others, and says so against the culture.* Myth #4, "We Can Go It Alone", is rejected outright: "We simply can't learn to be more vulnerable and courageous on our own" (brown.23, .24). Vulnerability is "based on mutuality and requires boundaries and trust" (brown.21); Gottman's trust is built by not "choosing to think only about what I wanted" (brown.22). **Complication:** worth itself has no giver. The Wholehearted "simply believe they are worthy" (brown.06); the morning thought is "I am enough" (brown.05); worthiness is "cultivated" by "choices and daily practices". No one grants or judges it: it is "a waste of time to evaluate my worthiness by weighing the reaction of the people in the stands" (brown.24). Critics are dismissed and loved ones are kept, but loved ones are support, not judges. So the self is **bound horizontally** (to others, by need and mutuality) and **limited by nature**, but **answers to no one for its worth**. The vertical frame is neither absorbed nor dropped by name; it is replaced by "the data" (brown.07) and human needs.

**Comparison with Byrne (2006).** Bound, not unbound. (1) *The other person.* Byrne: "It is not people who are giving you the things you desire" (byrne.16), harassers "weren't there" (byrne.08). Brown: support, a therapist, a spouse and friends are named as the condition of change (brown.23, .24), and disengagement is "betrayal" (brown.22). (2) *Limits.* Byrne: "Are there any limits to this? Absolutely not" (byrne.21). Brown: "Perfect and bulletproof [...] don't exist in the human experience" (brown.02); "regardless of our willingness to do vulnerability, it does us" (brown.20). (3) *Abundance.* Brown rejects the prosperity frame in so many words: "abundance and scarcity are two sides of the same coin" (brown.17). (4) *Causes.* Byrne makes thought the cause of debt and harassment; Brown names 9/11, wars, recession and unemployment as causes of a national mood (brown.15). (5) *What they share:* worth is self-declared and unconditioned ("I am enough"; Byrne's "love yourself", byrne.22); a prayer without a named addressee (brown.19; compare byrne.23's gratitude); and one master concept that explains everything (vulnerability; the law).

**C7.2 (isolation).** **For, as a diagnosis Brown herself makes.** "Going it alone is a value we hold in high esteem in our culture"; "walking alone can feel miserable and depressing" (brown.23); people "opt for disconnection as a form of protection" (brown.20); disengagement is the core problem in work, parenting and schools (brown.09, .16) and the commonest betrayal (brown.22). But she treats isolation as a cultural *value* to be resisted, not as a product of self-reliance in surfeit; the causal claim of C7.2 is not tested here.

**C7.3 (exhaustion).** **For, as description.** A guidepost names "Exhaustion as a Status Symbol and Productivity as Self-Worth" (brown.04); scarcity culture asks whether "self-worth [is] tied to achievement, productivity, or compliance" (brown.16); Brown's own control and performance were "exhausting" (brown.25). The cause given is fear of vulnerability, not individual responsibility in surfeit.

**C7.4 (blame).** **Complicates.** Brown locates causes in the *culture*: narcissism is "environmental" (brown.12), scarcity thrives in "shame-prone cultures" and trauma (brown.15), "The larger culture is always applying pressure" (brown.17). She refuses to put "the blame on those people" (brown.11). But she also refuses "blaming the system" and holds people "accountable for their behaviors" (brown.12), and the remedy is personal: change comes when "a critical mass of people start making different choices" (brown.15). Cause social, responsibility personal. This is not the self as sole cause (Byrne, Smiles); it is closer to the thesis's own position than to its target.

**C8.2, C8.6 (conformity felt as the self; "self-expression").** **Complicates.** The word "self-expression" does **not** occur in the excerpt. The vocabulary is "showing up", "being seen", "authenticity", "the real me", "I'd have to be me" (brown.02, .09, .18). This is the self as its own expression, and its enemy is conformity: copying a "template" (brown.18), "what People Think" (brown.04), "one narrow standard" (brown.16), "media-driven visions of perfection" (line 273). So Brown, like the 1899–1922 rebels (C8.6), uses the Self's vocabulary *against* conformity, and sees the conformity C8.2 says the Self cannot see: worth measured by "likes" (brown.13). She also separates vulnerability from self-display: not "celebrity-style social media information dumps" (brown.21). C8.2's claim that the idiom can no longer see conformity is against this text.

**Narcissism (C10.1).** **Complicates.** Brown cites Twenge and Campbell, *The Narcissism Epidemic* (brown.10), and answers "no" to an epidemic of the disorder (brown.14). She redescribes narcissism as "the shame-based fear of being ordinary" (brown.13) and places its cause in "our culture of scarcity" (brown.14). So a popular 2012 book already supplies a cultural cause and a mechanism for the symptom Lasch describes. C10.1 must say how the thesis's cause (the Self as only frame) differs from Brown's (shame, comparison, disengagement), and whether Brown's cure (worthiness, "I am enough") is itself a case of the thesis.

**C9.1 (the Self's form: one final source, no rival frame).** **Complicates.** One master concept: vulnerability is "the core, the heart, the center, of meaningful human experiences" (brown.07) and "the core of all emotions"; even spiritual depth goes through it: "vulnerability is the path" (brown.18). The final authority is "the data" (brown.07). But the lens is offered *alongside* others ("by also examining", line 229; diagnosis "might be necessary"), and Brown cites Roosevelt, Twist, Gottman, L'Engle and leadership research as allies, not rivals absorbed. One centre, not one exclusive source.

**C1.2, C1.3.** **C1.3: against** for this text: social facts are visible as causes (brown.15), and the culture is named as a pressure the self must "push back" against (brown.17). **C1.2: complicates.** Brown describes a culture of "never enough" as the "greatest cultural influence of our time" (brown.14): a totalizing culture, but of scarcity and comparison, not of the self-originating Self. Her book's huge success (sales not in the excerpt) would make it evidence of what *mainstream* self-help sells: a bounded self with self-given worth.

**C6.13.** **Complicates, faintly.** "Rugged individualism" survives as an inherited value ("in my DNA", brown.23), and the remedy is voluntary bonds (family, therapist, friends, "a faith community", line 295). But these bonds are framed as support for the self's journey and as cultures the self "reshapes" (brown.15), not as checks that bind it.

**Against or limiting evidence found (strong).** Limits stated as nature (brown.01, .02, .20); dependence on others (brown.23, .24); mutuality and earned trust (brown.21, .22); accountability (brown.12; "Being accountable", line 397); rejection of abundance (brown.17). The one line that comes close to stage 3 is "I am enough" with no giver (brown.05, .06): the worth is self-held, the self is not.

## Passages

### brown.01 · Note on the title, "What It Means to Dare Greatly" — "not optional"; "Our only choice is a question of engagement"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: a limit the self cannot opt out of), C9.1 (complicates: one master concept)
- **Location:** note on the title; `excerpts/brown-daring-greatly.txt` lines 17–19
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> The first time I read this quote, I thought, This is vulnerability. Everything I've learned from over a decade of research on vulnerability has taught me this exact lesson. [...]
>
> Vulnerability is not weakness, and the uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure we face every day are not optional. Our only choice is a question of engagement. Our willingness to own and engage with our vulnerability determines the depth of our courage and the clarity of our purpose; the level to which we protect ourselves from being vulnerable is a measure of our fear and disconnection.

The book's first claim is a limit: uncertainty and exposure "are not optional". Choice remains, but only over how to respond to a given condition. Contrast Byrne's "Whatever you choose is right" (byrne.24). Roosevelt's arena is read at once as "vulnerability": the single key that the rest of the book turns.

### brown.02 · Note on the title — "Perfect and bulletproof [...] don't exist"; "dare to show up and let ourselves be seen"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C8.2 (complicates: the self as its own expression)
- **Location:** note on the title; lines 21–23
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> When we spend our lives waiting until we're perfect or bulletproof before we walk into the arena, we ultimately sacrifice relationships and opportunities that may not be recoverable, we squander our precious time, and we turn our backs on our gifts, those unique contributions that only we can make.
>
> Perfect and bulletproof are seductive, but they don't exist in the human experience. [...] Rather than sitting on the sidelines and hurling judgment and advice, we must dare to show up and let ourselves be seen. This is vulnerability. This is daring greatly.

Two strands at once. The limit: perfection does not "exist in the human experience". The Self's vocabulary: "unique contributions that only we can make" (difference as value) and "let ourselves be seen" (the self shown, not made). "Gifts" implies a giver, who is not named.

### brown.03 · Introduction, "My Adventures in the Arena" — "Connection is why we're here. We are hardwired to connect with others"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: a frame above the self, as nature), C7.2 (for: disconnection as suffering)
- **Location:** Introduction; line 96
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> The surest thing I took away from my BSW, MSW, and Ph.D. in social work is this: Connection is why we're here. ### brown.05 · Introduction — Wholehearted living: "No matter what gets done and how much is left undone, I am enough"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates: self-declared worth), C7.3 (against: worth uncoupled from productivity)
- **Location:** Introduction; line 128
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Wholehearted living is about engaging in our lives from a place of worthiness. It means cultivating the courage, compassion, and connection to wake up in the morning and think, No matter what gets done and how much is left undone, I am enough. It's going to bed at night thinking, Yes, I am imperfect and vulnerable and sometimes afraid, but that doesn't change the truth that I am also brave and worthy of love and belonging.

The nearest thing to stage 3 in the excerpt. Worth is a first-person declaration with no giver, and it is unconditional ("No matter what gets done"). But it is worth "of love and belonging", so the self's worth points to others, and it is held alongside admitted limits ("imperfect and vulnerable"). Byrne declares the self God; Brown declares it enough.

### brown.06 · Introduction — "Love and belonging are irreducible needs"; the Wholehearted "simply believe they are worthy"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates: worth as belief; against: needs as given), C7.4 (against: struggles not read as fault)
- **Location:** Introduction; lines 132–136
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Love and belonging are irreducible needs of all men, women, and children. We're hardwired for connection—it's what gives purpose and meaning to our lives. The absence of love, belonging, and connection always leads to suffering.
>
> If you roughly divide the men and women I've interviewed into two groups—those who feel a deep sense of love and belonging, and those who struggle for it—there's only one variable that separates the groups: Those who feel lovable, who love, and who experience belonging simply believe they are worthy of love and belonging. They don't have better or easier lives, they don't have fewer struggles with addiction or depression, and they haven't survived fewer traumas or bankruptcies or divorces, but in the midst of all of these struggles, they have developed practices that enable them to hold on to the belief that they are worthy of love, belonging, and even joy.
>
> A strong belief in our worthiness doesn't just happen—it's cultivated when we understand the guideposts as choices and daily practices.

The book's central finding, and the hinge for C5.14. The *need* is given and universal ("irreducible needs of all men, women, and children"). The *worth* is a belief the person holds and cultivates by "choices and daily practices". Unlike Byrne, belief does not change circumstances ("They don't have better or easier lives"); it changes how the self holds them. A mind-cure structure (belief as the decisive variable) without mind-cure's power over the world.

### brown.07 · Introduction — "Vulnerability is the core, the heart, the center"; "faith in my research"

- **Location:** Introduction; lines 142, 154
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Only after twelve years of dropping deeper and deeper into this work did I finally understand the role it plays in our lives. Vulnerability is the core, the heart, the center, of meaningful human experiences.
>
> [...]
>
> My decision to dare greatly didn't stem from self-confidence as much as it did from faith in my research. I know I'm a good researcher, and I trusted that the conclusions I had drawn from the data were valid and reliable. Vulnerability would take me where I wanted or maybe needed to go.

What stands above the self here is not God or the Universe but "the data". Brown dares from "faith in my research", not from "self-confidence": the self leans on something outside it. ### brown.11 · Ch. 1, opening — "places the blame on those people"; "Shame is more likely to be the cause of these behaviors, not the cure"

- **Location:** Ch. 1, opening section; lines 206–212
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> If you're like me, you're probably wincing a bit and thinking, Yes. This is exactly the problem. Not with me, of course. But in general…this sounds about right!
>
> It feels good to have an explanation, especially one that conveniently makes us feel better about ourselves and places the blame on those people. In fact, whenever I hear people making the narcissism argument, it's normally served with a side of contempt, anger, and judgment. [...]

### brown.12 · Ch. 1, "Looking at Narcissism Through the Lens of Vulnerability" — "the cause is much more likely to be environmental"; "I'm not talking about 'blaming the system' here"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (complicates: cause social, accountability personal), C1.3 (against)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, "Looking at Narcissism Through the Lens of Vulnerability"; line 219
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Diagnosing and labeling people whose struggles are more environmental or learned than genetic or organic is often far more detrimental to healing and change than it is helpful. And when we have an epidemic on our hands, unless we're talking about something physically contagious, the cause is much more likely to be environmental than a hardwiring issue. Labeling the problem in a way that makes it about who people are rather than the choices they're making lets all of us off the hook: Too bad. That's who I am. I'm a huge believer in holding people accountable for their behaviors, so I'm not talking about "blaming the system" here. I'm talking about understanding the root cause so we can address the problems.

C7.4 in its most careful form. The cause is the environment; the person is still "accountable for their behaviors". Brown holds both and refuses both extremes: not the self as sole cause (Byrne, Smiles), not "blaming the system". This is evidence that mainstream self-help in 2012 can see social causes; C7.4's "rarely" must allow for it.

### brown.15 · Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity" — 9/11, wars, recession, unemployment; "until a critical mass of people start making different choices"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (complicates), C1.3 (against: social causes seen), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity"; lines 279–283
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Scarcity doesn't take hold in a culture overnight. But the feeling of scarcity does thrive in shame-prone cultures that are deeply steeped in comparison and fractured by disengagement. [...]
>
> [...] From 9/11, multiple wars, and the recession, to catastrophic natural disasters and the increase in random violence and school shootings, we've survived and are surviving events that have torn at our sense of safety with such force that we've experienced them as trauma even if we weren't directly involved. And when it comes to the staggering numbers of those now unemployed and underemployed, I think every single one of us has been directly affected or is close to someone who has been directly affected.
>
> Worrying about scarcity is our culture's version of post-traumatic stress. It happens when we've been through too much, and rather than coming together to heal (which requires vulnerability), we're angry and scared and at each other's throats. [...] Scarcity bubbles up from these conditions and perpetuates them until a critical mass of people start making different choices and reshaping the smaller cultures they belong to.

The plainest contrast with Byrne's debt and harassment passages (byrne.06, .08): unemployment and war are causes, and the reader is not told they attracted them. But the cure is still choice ("a critical mass of people start making different choices") acting on "the smaller cultures they belong to": family, work, school. Social cause, personal and local remedy.

### brown.16 · Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity" — "Is self-worth tied to achievement, productivity, or compliance?"; "Is everyone struggling to be seen and heard?"

- **Claims:** C7.3 (for), C7.2 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity"; lines 287–293
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Shame: Is fear of ridicule and belittling used to manage people and/or to keep people in line? Is self-worth tied to achievement, productivity, or compliance? Are blaming and finger-pointing norms? Are put-downs and name-calling rampant? What about favoritism? Is perfectionism an issue?
>
> Comparison: Healthy competition can be beneficial, but is there constant overt or covert comparing and ranking? Has creativity been suffocated? Are people held to one narrow standard rather than acknowledged for their unique gifts and contributions? Is there an ideal way of being or one form of talent that is used as measurement of everyone else's worth?
>
> Disengagement: Are people afraid to take risks or try new things? Is it easier to stay quiet than to share stories, experiences, and ideas? Does it feel as if no one is really paying attention or listening? Is everyone struggling to be seen and heard?
>

Brown's own audit of American culture, answered "YES, YES, and YES". Self-worth tied to productivity (C7.3), blame as a norm (C7.4), no one listening (C7.2): the thesis's later symptoms, attributed to shame, comparison and disengagement. The remedy is difference acknowledged ("unique gifts and contributions") against "one narrow standard".

### brown.17 · Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity" — "The larger culture is always applying pressure"; "abundance and scarcity are two sides of the same coin"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates: "I am enough"), C1.3 (against), comparison with Byrne (against the prosperity frame)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, "The Source of Scarcity"; lines 295–297
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> [...] to grow a relationship or raise a family or create an organizational culture or run a school or nurture a faith community, all in a way that is fundamentally opposite to the cultural norms driven by scarcity, it takes awareness, commitment, and work…every single day. The larger culture is always applying pressure, and unless we're willing to push back and fight for what we believe in, the default becomes a state of scarcity. We're called to "dare greatly" every time we make choices that challenge the social climate of scarcity.
>
> The counterapproach to living in scarcity is not about abundance. In fact, I think abundance and scarcity are two sides of the same coin. The opposite of "never enough" isn't abundance or "more than you could ever imagine." The opposite of scarcity is enough, or what I call Wholeheartedness. As I explained in the Introduction, there are many tenets of Wholeheartedness, but at its very core is vulnerability and worthiness: facing uncertainty, exposure, and emotional risks, and knowing that I am enough.

The sharpest break with Byrne: the abundance answer is rejected as the other face of scarcity. The self lives inside cultures it must "push back" against, and the work is done in shared forms (family, school, "a faith community"). Still, the cure ends in a first-person knowledge with no giver: "knowing that I am enough". "Called" is passive, but no caller is named.

### brown.18 · Ch. 2, "Myth #1: 'Vulnerability Is Weakness'" — "Vulnerability is the birthplace of love [...] If we want [...] more meaningful spiritual lives, vulnerability is the path"

- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #1; lines 325–327
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Vulnerability isn't good or bad: It's not what we call a dark emotion, nor is it always a light, positive experience. Vulnerability is the core of all emotions and feelings. To feel is to be vulnerable. To believe vulnerability is weakness is to believe that feeling is weakness. To foreclose on our emotional life out of a fear that the costs will be too high is to walk away from the very thing that gives purpose and meaning to living.
>

One source for everything good: love, hope, "accountability", "authenticity", and the spiritual life. That is C9.1's one-source form, but the source is a human condition (exposure, uncertainty), not the self's power. Spirit is reached through feeling, and no object of faith is named.

### brown.20 · Ch. 2, "Myth #2: 'I Don't Do Vulnerability'" — "no 'get out of vulnerability free' card"; "regardless of our willingness to do vulnerability, it does us"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: a limit), C7.2 (for: disconnection as protection)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #2; lines 501–503, 519
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Unfortunately, there is no "get out of vulnerability free" card. We can't opt out of the uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure that's woven through our daily experiences. Life is vulnerable.
>
> Look back at the list of examples. These are the challenges of being alive, of being in a relationship, of being connected. Even if we choose to stay out of relationships and opt for disconnection as a form of protection, we're still alive and that means vulnerability happens. [...]
>
> This questioning process helps because, as you can see from my answers, regardless of our willingness to do vulnerability, it does us. When we pretend that we can avoid vulnerability we engage in behaviors that are often inconsistent with who we want to be. Experiencing vulnerability isn't a choice—the only choice we have is how we're going to respond when we are confronted with uncertainty, risk, and emotional exposure.

The strongest limit-statement in the excerpt: "it does us". The self cannot choose its condition, only its response, which is the old Stoic form of limit, not the self as cause. Isolation ("opt for disconnection") is named as a defence that fails. Note the standard is still the self's own ("who we want to be").

### brown.21 · Ch. 2, "Myth #3: Vulnerability Is Letting It All Hang Out" — "based on mutuality and requires boundaries and trust"; "people who have earned the right to hear them"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: the self bound by mutuality), C8.2 (against: not self-display), C8.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #3; lines 529–533
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Vulnerability is based on mutuality and requires boundaries and trust. It's not oversharing, it's not purging, it's not indiscriminate disclosure, and it's not celebrity-style social media information dumps. Vulnerability is about sharing our feelings and our experiences with people who have earned the right to hear them. Being vulnerable and open is mutual and an integral part of the trust-building process.
>
> [...] That may be desperation or woundedness or even attention-seeking, but it's not vulnerability. Why? Because sharing appropriately, with boundaries, means sharing with people with whom we've developed relationships that can bear the weight of our story. [...]
>
> Vulnerability without boundaries leads to disconnection, distrust, and disengagement. [...] vulnerability is bankrupt on its own terms when people move from being vulnerable to using vulnerability to deal with unmet needs, get attention, or engage in the shock-and-awe behaviors that are so commonplace in today's culture.

Brown draws a line between her ideal and the expressive culture around her: not "celebrity-style social media information dumps", not "attention-seeking". Being seen is bounded by relationship and "mutuality". But the self decides who has "earned the right" to hear: the other is bound by the self's test, while the self is bound by the relation.

### brown.22 · Ch. 2, Myth #3 (trust) — Gottman: "rather than choosing to think only about what I wanted"; "the betrayal of disengagement"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: the other as a claim on the self), C7.2 (for: disengagement)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #3, the marble jar; lines 595, 601
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> [Gottman:] Now, at that moment, I was building trust; I was there for her. I was connecting with her rather than choosing to think only about what I wanted. These are the moments, we've discovered, that build trust.
>
> [...]
>
> [Brown:] In fact, this betrayal usually happens long before the other ones. I'm talking about the betrayal of disengagement. Of not caring. Of letting the connection go. ### brown.23 · Ch. 2, "Myth #4: We Can Go It Alone" — "Going it alone is a value we hold in high esteem"; "rugged individualism in my DNA"; "We need support"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against: self-sufficiency rejected), C7.2 (for: the cultural value; against: as Brown's ideal), C6.13 (complicates), C6.7 (for: the vocabulary survives)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #4; lines 617–621
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Going it alone is a value we hold in high esteem in our culture, ironically even when it comes to cultivating connection. I get the appeal; I have that rugged individualism in my DNA. [...] If Whitesnake isn't your cup of tea, there are bootstrapping anthems in every imaginable genre. In reality, walking alone can feel miserable and depressing, but we admire the strength it conveys, and going it alone is revered in our culture.
>
> Well, as much as I love the idea of walking alone down a lonely street of dreams, the vulnerability journey is not the kind of journey we can make alone. We need support. We need folks who will let us try on new ways of being without judging us. [...]
>

The direct test the brief asked for. Brown names self-sufficiency as the culture's value ("revered in our culture"; "rugged individualism", Hoover's phrase, now in pop songs) and rejects it. Others are needed, not obeyed; they help the self change, they do not set its course.

### brown.24 · Ch. 2, Myth #4 — "a waste of time to evaluate my worthiness by weighing the reaction of the people in the stands"; "We simply can't learn to be more vulnerable and courageous on our own"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates: bound to others for support, answerable to none for worth), C7.2 (against)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #4; lines 637–639
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Going back to Roosevelt's "Man in the Arena" speech, I also learned that the people who love me, the people I really depend on, were never the critics who were pointing at me while I stumbled. They weren't in the bleachers at all. They were with me in the arena. Fighting for me and with me.
>
> Nothing has transformed my life more than realizing that it's a waste of time to evaluate my worthiness by weighing the reaction of the people in the stands. The people who love me and will be there regardless of the outcome are within arm's reach. [...] I want to look at Steve and my kids and say, "I'm with you. In the arena. And when we fail, we'll fail together, while daring greatly." We simply can't learn to be more vulnerable and courageous on our own. Sometimes our first and greatest dare is asking for support.

The verdict passage for C5.14. The self is bound: "the people I really depend on", "we'll fail together", "can't [...] on our own". But its worth is answerable to no one: critics' judgment is "a waste of time", and the loved ones are valued because they "will be there regardless of the outcome", that is, because they do not judge. Answerability to others (Smith's impartial spectator, Locke's God) is replaced by unconditional support. Bound, but not answerable.

### brown.25 · Ch. 2, Myth #4 — "I performed until there was no energy left to feel [...] It was exhausting"

- **Claims:** C7.3 (for, as description; complicates on cause)
- **Location:** Ch. 2, Myth #4; lines 629–631
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> I did believe that I could opt out of feeling vulnerable, so when it happened—when the phone rang with unimaginable news; or when I was scared; or when I loved so fiercely that rather than feeling gratitude and joy I could only prepare for loss—I controlled things. I managed situations and micromanaged the people around me. I performed until there was no energy left to feel. I made what was uncertain certain, no matter what the cost. I stayed so busy that the truth of my hurting and my fear could never catch up. I looked brave on the outside and felt scared on the inside.
>
> Slowly I learned that this shield was too heavy to lug around, and that the only thing it really did was keep me from knowing myself and letting myself be known. The shield required that I stay small and quiet behind it so as not to draw attention to my imperfections and vulnerabilities. It was exhausting.

C7.3's exhaustion in the first person, from control and performance. Brown's cause is the wish to "opt out" of a given limit, which the thesis could read as the self trying to be its own cause. Her cure is to accept the limit and others' help, so the passage is for C7.3 as symptom and against any reading in which the cure is more self-reliance.
