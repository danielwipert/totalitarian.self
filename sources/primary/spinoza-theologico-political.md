## Passages

### spinoza-ttp.01 · Preface 46 — "free to choose for himself the foundations of his creed"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Preface, sentence 46; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-1.txt` lines 410–416
- **Checked:** text file

> Furthermore, as men's habits of mind differ, so that some more readily embrace one form of faith, some another, for what moves one to pray may move another only to scoff, I conclude, in accordance with what has gone before, that everyone should be free to choose for himself the foundations of his creed, and that faith should be judged only by its fruits; each would then obey God freely with his whole heart, while nothing would be publicly honoured save justice and charity.

Belief becomes a matter of individual choice, and on the ground of difference ("habits of mind differ"). But the aim of the choice is to "obey God freely": the creed is private, the obedience is not dropped.

### spinoza-ttp.02 · Preface 51–55 — the plan of the book, and who may read it

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Preface, sentences 51 and 54–55; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-1.txt` lines 441–446, 449–459
- **Checked:** text file

> I then prove, that the holders of sovereign power are the depositories and interpreters of religious no less than of civil ordinances, and that they alone have the right to decide what is just or unjust, pious or impious; lastly, I conclude by showing, that they best retain this right and secure safety to their state by allowing every man to think what he likes, and say what he thinks. [...] I know how deeply rooted are the prejudices embraced under the name of religion; I am aware that in the mind of the masses superstition is no less deeply rooted than fear [...] Therefore the multitude, and those of like passions with the multitude, I ask not to read my book [...]

The book's own summary puts the two halves side by side: the sovereign decides what is "pious or impious," and every man may "think what he likes." Freedom of thought is granted by and for the state, and the argument is addressed to philosophers, not to the multitude.

### spinoza-ttp.03 · VII.189–193 — "lodged with the individual"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. VII, "Of the Interpretation of Scripture," sentences 189–193; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-2.txt` lines 1507–1525
- **Checked:** text file

> With religion the case is widely different. Inasmuch as it consists not so much in outward actions as in simplicity and truth of character, it stands outside the sphere of law and public authority. [...] Therefore, as the supreme right of free thinking, even on religion, is in every man's power, and as it is inconceivable that such power could be alienated, it is also in every man's power to wield the supreme right and authority of free judgment in this behalf, and to explain and interpret religion for himself. [...] Similarly the supreme authority in explaining religion, and in passing judgment thereon, is lodged with the individual because it concerns questions of individual right.

The strongest "for" passage in the four authors before Paine: the "supreme authority" in religion is "lodged with the individual," not with any church or pontiff. This is Luther's private interpretation (C4.3) with the congregation and the preached word removed.

### spinoza-ttp.04 · XIV.45–48 — the dogmas of universal faith

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. XIV, "Definitions of Faith," sentences 45–48; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-3.txt` lines 1070–1088
- **Checked:** text file

> To the universal religion, then, belong only such dogmas as are absolutely required in order to attain obedience to God, and without which such obedience would be impossible; as for the rest, each man-seeing that he is the best judge of his own character-should adopt whatever he thinks best adapted to strengthen his love of justice. [...] that there exists a God, that is, a Supreme Being, Who loves justice and charity, and Who must be obeyed by whosoever would be saved [...] I. That God or a Supreme Being exists, sovereignly just and merciful, the Exemplar of the true life; that whosoever is ignorant of or disbelieves in His existence cannot obey Him or know Him as a Judge.

Each man is "the best judge of his own character," but only inside a minimal common creed whose first article is God "as a Judge." The private self is still answerable; what has shrunk is the doctrine, not the judge.

### spinoza-ttp.05 · XIV.75–77 — faith and philosophy "as wide apart as the poles"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. XIV, sentences 75–77; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-3.txt` lines 1178–1186
- **Checked:** text file

> Philosophy has no end in view save truth: faith, as we have abundantly proved, looks for nothing but obedience and piety. [...] Faith, therefore, allows the greatest latitude in philosophic speculation, allowing us without blame to think what we like about anything, and only condemning, as heretics and schismatics, those who teach opinions which tend to produce obstinacy, hatred, strife, and anger [...]

Here what separates from religion is *thought* (philosophy), not the Self as a whole: faith keeps the will (obedience), reason gets the mind. Even so, heresy survives, redefined as teaching that breeds "hatred, strife, and anger."

### spinoza-ttp.06 · XIX.5–9 — outward rites to the sovereign, inward worship to each

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for: inward belief is private; against: religion is not a voluntary society)
- **Location:** Ch. XIX, "the Right over Matters Spiritual lies wholly with the Sovereign," sentences 5–9; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-4.txt` lines 1759–1771
- **Checked:** text file

> I wish, however, first to point out that religion acquires its force as law solely from the decrees of the sovereign. (6) God has no special kingdom among men except in so far as He reigns through temporal rulers. [...] Moreover, the rites of religion and the outward observances of piety should be in accordance with the public peace and well-being, and should therefore be determined by the sovereign power alone. (8) I speak here only of the outward observances of piety and the external rites of religion, not of piety, itself, nor of the inward worship of God [...] (19:9) Inward worship of God and piety in itself are within the sphere of everyone's private rights, and cannot be alienated [...]

The split is exact: inward worship private, outward religion public and set by the state. Spinoza does not make the church a voluntary society; he makes it a department of the sovereign. This is the opposite of Locke's solution (locke-toleration.05).

### spinoza-ttp.07 · XIX.45–47 — "obey the sovereign power's commands in all things"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against)
- **Location:** Ch. XIX, sentences 45–47; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-4.txt` lines 1915–1927
- **Checked:** text file

> We can now clearly understand how the sovereign is the interpreter of religion, and further, that no one can obey God rightly, if the practices of his piety do not conform to the public welfare; or, consequently, if he does not implicitly obey all the commands of the sovereign. [...] Now, no private citizen can know what is good for the state, except he learn it through the sovereign power, who alone has the right to transact public business: therefore no one can rightly practise piety or obedience to God, unless he obey the sovereign power's commands in all things.

The religious Self is free in thought and bound in act, and bound now to the state rather than to the church. Religion is separated from the clergy, not from the community: piety is defined as obedience to public welfare.

### spinoza-ttp.08 · XX.5 — "brains are as diverse as palates"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. XX, "That in a Free State every man may think what he likes, and say what he thinks," sentence 5; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-4.txt` lines 2173–2179
- **Checked:** text file

> I admit that the judgment can be biassed in many ways, and to an almost incredible degree, so that while exempt from direct external control it may be so dependent on another man's words, that it may fitly be said to be ruled by him; but although this influence is carried to great lengths, it has never gone so far as to invalidate the statement, that every man's understanding is his own, and that brains are as diverse as palates.

Difference of minds is here a plain fact of nature, and the ground of toleration. Note that Spinoza admits the judgment is easily "ruled" by another's words: ownership of the understanding is a limit on what the state can do, not a claim that the self is its own source.

### spinoza-ttp.09 · XX.14, 23 — master of his thoughts, not of his actions

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. XX, sentences 14 and 23; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-4.txt` lines 2203–2207, 2238–2244
- **Checked:** text file

> Since, therefore, no one can abdicate his freedom of judgment and feeling; since every man is by indefeasible natural right the master of his own thoughts, it follows that men thinking in diverse and contradictory fashions, cannot, without disastrous results, be compelled to speak only according to the dictates of the supreme power. [...] Therefore, the individual justly cedes the right of free action, though not of free reason and judgment; no one can act against the authorities without danger to the state, though his feelings and judgment may be at variance therewith; he may even speak against them, provided that he does so from rational conviction, not from fraud, anger, or hatred, and provided that he does not attempt to introduce any change on his private authority.

The Self that is "master of his own thoughts" has given up "the right of free action." The separation is of the mind from the state's reach, while the acting person stays fully under it: a divided self, not a separated one.

### spinoza-ttp.10 · XX.81 — "every man should think what he likes and say what he thinks" (verified)

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for, with the limit stated)
- **Location:** Ch. XX, sentence 81; `texts/spinoza-theologico-political-4.txt` lines 2470–2475. The phrase also appears in the chapter title (line 2155) and Preface 51 (line 446).
- **Checked:** text file

> Wherefore, as we have shown in Chapter XVIII., the safest way for a state is to lay down the rule that religion is comprised solely in the exercise of charity and justice, and that the rights of rulers in sacred, no less than in secular matters, should merely have to do with actions, but that every man should think what he likes and say what he thinks.

The phrase is verified, but in its sentence it is the conclusion of a rule *for the state*: religion reduced to "charity and justice," rulers keeping their rights over religious actions. Quote the whole sentence, not the tail.

## Summary for C4.30

Spinoza makes religious *belief and interpretation* individual (spinoza-ttp.01, .03, .10), but not as a voluntary church: outward religion belongs to the sovereign (.06, .07). The private believer still obeys God "as a Judge" (.04). What separates is the mind from coercion, not the Self from God or the community.
