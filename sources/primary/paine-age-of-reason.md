# Paine, *The Age of Reason* (1794–96)

- **Edition used:** original English, as edited by Moncure Conway (editor's introduction and bracketed notes are Conway's, not Paine's)
- **Text file:** `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` (clean)
- **Citation form:** Part and chapter (Part I has a closing "Recapitulation").
- **Claims to test:** C4.30, C6.*

## Passages

### paine-reason.01 · Part I, ch. I — "My own mind is my own church" (verified)

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "The Author's Profession of Faith"; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 759–777
- **Checked:** text file

> I believe in one God, and no more; and I hope for happiness beyond this life. I believe the equality of man, and I believe that religious duties consist in doing justice, loving mercy, and endeavoring to make our fellow-creatures happy. [...] I do not believe in the creed professed by the Jewish church, by the Roman church, by the Greek church, by the Turkish church, by the Protestant church, nor by any church that I know of. My own mind is my own church. All national institutions of churches, whether Jewish, Christian, or Turkish, appear to me no other than human inventions set up to terrify and enslave mankind, and monopolize power and profit.

The phrase is verified. It is the furthest any of the four authors goes: no church at all, not even a voluntary one. But it follows a creed of one God, a hoped-for afterlife, and duties to "fellow-creatures." The mind replaces the church, not God.

### paine-reason.02 · Part I, ch. I — "mentally faithful to himself"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 779–784
- **Checked:** text file

> I do not mean by this declaration to condemn those who believe otherwise; they have the same right to their belief as I have to mine. But it is necessary to the happiness of man, that he be mentally faithful to himself. Infidelity does not consist in believing, or in disbelieving; it consists in professing to believe what he does not believe.

A new standard: fidelity to oneself. Infidelity is redefined from unbelief toward God to insincerity toward one's own mind. This is the nearest the four authors come to a Self that is its own religious court, and an early form of the ethic of sincerity (C5.5).

### paine-reason.03 · Part I, ch. I — "The adulterous connection of church and state"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for: institutional separation)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 797–806
- **Checked:** text file

> The adulterous connection of church and state, wherever it had taken place, whether Jewish, Christian, or Turkish, had so effectually prohibited, by pains and penalties, every discussion upon established creeds, and upon first principles of religion, that until the system of government should be changed, those subjects could not be brought fairly and openly before the world; but that whenever this should be done, a revolution in the system of religion would follow. Human inventions and priest-craft would be detected; and man would return to the pure, unmixed, and unadulterated belief of one God, and no more.

Paine's own story of the separation: political revolution frees religion, and the result is a *return* to "belief of one God," not an exit from belief.

### paine-reason.04 · Part I, ch. II — "as if the way to God was not open to every man alike"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C4.3 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. II, "Of Missions and Revelations"; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 815–819, 838–841
- **Checked:** text file

> Every national church or religion has established itself by pretending some special mission from God, communicated to certain individuals. The Jews have their Moses; the Christians their Jesus Christ, their apostles and saints; and the Turks their Mahomet; as if the way to God was not open to every man alike. [...] It is revelation to the first person only, and hearsay to every other, and, consequently, they are not obliged to believe it.

Luther's priesthood of all believers (C4.3) taken to its end: no mediator, not even Scripture. Note "every man alike": the access is equal and common, not singular.

### paine-reason.05 · Part II, ch. III — "put man in the place of God"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against, for the Self), C5.9 (for)
- **Location:** Part II, ch. III, "Conclusion"; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 6588–6604
- **Checked:** text file

> “When it is revealed to me, I will believe it to be revelation; but it is not and cannot be incumbent upon me to believe it to be revelation before; neither is it proper that I should take the word of man as the word of God, and put man in the place of God.” [...] I totally disbelieve that the Almighty ever did communicate any thing to man, by any mode of speech, in any language, or by any kind of vision, or appearance, or by any means which our senses are capable of receiving, otherwise than by the universal display of himself in the works of the creation, and by that repugnance we feel in ourselves to bad actions, and disposition to good ones.

The key to "my own mind is my own church": Paine refuses priests because they "put man in the place of God." The mind is the church because God speaks to it directly, through creation and conscience. The Self is the place of religion, not its authority.

### paine-reason.06 · Part II, ch. III — "call us to account"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against, for the Self), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** Part II, ch. III; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 6755–6776
- **Checked:** text file

> We must know also, that the power that called us into being, can if he please, and when he pleases, call us to account for the manner in which we have lived here; and therefore without seeking any other motive for the belief, it is rational to believe that he will, for we know beforehand that he can. [...] The creation is the Bible of the deist. He there reads, in the hand-writing of the Creator himself, the certainty of his existence, and the immutability of his power; and all other Bibles and Testaments are to him forgeries. The probability that we may be called to account hereafter, will, to reflecting minds, have the influence of belief [...] it is the fool only, and not the philosopher, nor even the prudent man, that will live as if there were no God.

The answerability frame, in the most radical of the four. Paine's deist Self has no church, no Scripture and no priest, and is still "called to account." Strong evidence that the separation from religion is institutional, with the answerable Self kept.

### paine-reason.07 · Part I, Recapitulation — the moral duty of man

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, "Recapitulation"; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt` lines 2995–3012
- **Checked:** text file

> Secondly, That the Creation we behold is the real and ever existing word of God, in which we cannot be deceived. [...] Thirdly, That the moral duty of man consists in imitating the moral goodness and beneficence of God manifested in the creation towards all his creatures. That seeing as we daily do the goodness of God to all men, it is an example calling upon all men to practise the same towards each other [...] I trouble not myself about the manner of future existence. I content myself with believing, even to positive conviction, that the power that gave me existence is able to continue it, in any form and manner he pleases [...]

Duty is defined by imitation of God and points outward, to "all men" and "all his creatures." The self does not give itself the law; it reads it in creation.

### paine-reason.08 · Part I, ch. XI and XIII — the true deist and "the Almighty lecturer"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C4.28 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. XI, lines 1970–1976; ch. XIII, lines 2358–2362; `texts/paine-age-of-reason.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> The Almighty lecturer, by displaying the principles of science in the structure of the universe, has invited man to study and to imitation. It is as if he had said to the inhabitants of this globe that we call ours, “I have made an earth for man to dwell upon, and I have rendered the starry heavens visible, to teach him science and the arts. He can now provide for his own comfort, AND LEARN FROM MY MUNIFICENCE TO ALL, TO BE KIND TO EACH OTHER.” [...] How different is this to the pure and simple profession of Deism! The true deist has but one Deity; and his religion consists in contemplating the power, wisdom, and benignity of the Deity in his works, and in endeavouring to imitate him in every thing moral, scientifical, and mechanical.

Reason is not the self's own; it is a lesson set by "the Almighty lecturer," and the lesson ends in a social duty, "TO BE KIND TO EACH OTHER."

## Summary for C4.30

Paine is the one author who makes the individual mind the *only* church (paine-reason.01) and makes sincerity to oneself the test of faith (.02). That is real support for the rival story's strongest form. But his reason is God's lesson (.08), his mind is a church because God speaks to it directly (.05), and he is still "called to account" (.06). Even at its most radical, Enlightenment religion separates the Self from churches, not from God or from duty to others.
