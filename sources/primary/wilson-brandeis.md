## Summary of the reading (2026-10-25)

**C9.8 (the organization as a singular Self).** Both writers have one striking image that fits the claim, and then take it apart. Wilson says the employee's "individuality is swallowed up in the individuality and purpose of a great organization" (.01), and that business organization now overtops every state (.11). But in the same chapter he insists "The modern corporation is not engaged in business as an individual" and is "an impersonal element" (.02); the trusts were made by "the deliberate planning will, of men" (.08); a board of twenty-four "administer that corporation as if it belonged to them" (.09); and the great "community of interest" is not a single body but "an amalgam" of personnel that can be broken up (.10). Brandeis is plainer still: corporations are the bankers' "subjects" (brandeis.01); a director is a "trustee" bound to the company's own good (brandeis.06). For both, the corporation is a **structure** run by a few men, not a will of its own. The danger they name is the few men's power reaching across many corporations, which is the opposite of one organization standing alone as a Self.

**"From which all value is imagined to flow."** This is the best-supported part of SPEC §9.3, but as a belief held by *men*, not by an organization. Wilson: "they really believe that the prosperity of the country depends upon them" (.07); Morgan's firm: "practically all the railroad and industrial development of this country has taken place initially through the medium of the great banking houses" (brandeis.08). Both authors then deny it: value comes "from the soil" (wilson.04) and from "hardy pioneers" (brandeis.08), and the bankers' power is made of "the people's own gold" (brandeis.02).

**"Admitting no frame but its own."** Partly for. Brandeis's "Ethics" rule means "the interests of the combined bankers are superior to the interests of the rest of the community" (brandeis.04). But both writers put it down to ordinary human limits, not to a new ideology of the Self: "The weakness of human nature prevents men from being good judges of their own deservings" (brandeis.03); "it is my own business that I have a vision of" (wilson, ch. III, noted under .07).

**Open question 4.** These texts answer it: the corporation **submerges** most selves (wilson.01, brandeis.05) and **enlarges** a few men past what "as individuals they could never have wielded" (wilson.01). Brandeis adds that the enlarged few break on "the fundamental laws of human limitations" (brandeis.12): Harriman dies "from overwork" (brandeis.10). Neither author treats the corporation as a Self; both treat it as a machine that lets a few selves outgrow their limits. That is the essay's pattern of the structure kept and the limit dropped, but located in the men, not the organization.

**C6.7 (corporate leaders kept the pioneer vocabulary).** Mostly against, in these two texts. The defenders of combination, as Wilson and Brandeis report them, speak of *inevitability*, *necessity*, *industrial progress*, *protection*, *benevolence* and *trusteeship* (wilson.07, .12, .13, .14; brandeis.13), not of self-made men or pioneer freedom. Wilson even calls the big manufacturers timid wards "tied to the apron strings of the government" (wilson.14). It is the Progressives who hold the pioneer and self-made vocabulary, and turn it *against* monopoly: "the men who are on the make" (wilson.05), Jack the giant-killer (ch. VIII), Forbes "a great adventurer" (brandeis.11), "the push of manhood" (brandeis.07). The nearest thing to C6.7 is Morgan's claim to have *initiated* American development (brandeis.08). Brandeis's "pseudo-ethics" (brandeis.04) is a clear C8.7 case: a moral word dressing domination.

**C6.13 (local, independent standing lost).** Strongly for. Wilson: the old America where men were "captains of industry, not employees", finding credit among neighbours "according to their character, not according to their connections" (.05); Brandeis: 228 locally owned and locally financed companies brought under one New York banking house (brandeis.09), communities made "subjects or satellites" (brandeis.11). Both writers frame this as loss of *economic* independence and credit; neither is about the clubs, lodges or churches of C6.13, so they support the working-class half (isolation, dependence) better than the business-class half.

## Passages

### wilson.01 · *New Freedom* ch. I — the individual "swallowed up"; the few "exalted"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for), C6.13 (for); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. I "The Old Order Changeth"; `texts/wilson-new-freedom.txt` lines 154–176
- **Checked:** text file

> There is a sense in which in our day the individual has been submerged. In most parts of our country men work, not for themselves, not as partners in the old way in which they used to work, but generally as employees,--in a higher or lower grade,--of great corporations. [...] Your individuality is swallowed up in the individuality and purpose of a great organization.
>
> It is true that, while most men are thus submerged in the corporation, a few, a very few, are exalted to a power which as individuals they could never have wielded. Through the great organizations of which they are the heads, a few are enabled to play a part unprecedented by anything in history in the control of the business operations of the country and in the determination of the happiness of great numbers of people.

The wording in the brief is verified: "swallowed up," and it is the organization that has "individuality and purpose." This is the strongest single line for C9.8. But the second paragraph gives open question 4 its answer in Wilson's terms: the corporation submerges most selves and enlarges a few.

### wilson.02 · *New Freedom* ch. I — "the wrongs of a system"; the corporation "not [...] an individual"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 239–252
- **Checked:** text file

> So what we have to discuss is, not wrongs which individuals intentionally do,--I do not believe there are a great many of those,--but the wrongs of a system. [...] The truth is, we are all caught in a great economic system which is heartless. The modern corporation is not engaged in business as an individual. When we deal with it, we deal with an impersonal element, an immaterial piece of society. A modern corporation is a means of co-operation in the conduct of an enterprise which is so big that no one man can conduct it, and which the resources of no one man are sufficient to finance.

Fifty lines after wilson.01, Wilson denies that the corporation is an individual at all: it is impersonal, "a means of co-operation," and the wrong is systemic, not willed. This cuts against "the organization as a singular Self."

### wilson.03 · *New Freedom* ch. I — "hide and seek" between personality and corporation

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 262–267
- **Checked:** text file

> Now, do the workingmen employed by that stock corporation deal with that president and those directors? Not at all. Does the public deal with that president and that board of directors? It does not. Can anybody bring them to account? It is next to impossible to do so. If you undertake it you will find it a game of hide and seek, with the objects of your search taking refuge now behind the tree of their individual personality, now behind that of their corporate irresponsibility.

The corporation's real use, for Wilson, is to let men escape answerability: neither fully a person nor fully a thing. This bears on the essay's "answerability" root: what is lost is the director's standing to account, not a new corporate Self.

### wilson.04 · *New Freedom* ch. IV — "the organization greater than the man"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IV "Life Comes from the Soil"; lines 1534–1541
- **Checked:** text file

> Anything that depresses, anything that makes the organization greater than the man, anything that blocks, discourages, dismays the humble man, is against all the principles of progress. When I see alliances formed, as they are now being formed, by successful men of business with successful organizers of politics, I know that something has been done that checks the vitality and progress of society.

Wilson names the danger in nearly the words of SPEC §9.3. But the whole chapter's answer to "from which all value is imagined to flow" is that value rises "from the soil" (line 1418), from unknown men, not from the top.

### wilson.05 · *New Freedom* ch. I — "the men who are on the make"; "dependence, not freedom"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 354–357, 380–398
- **Checked:** text file

> What this country needs above everything else is a body of laws which will look after the men who are on the make rather than the men who are already made. [...] and eager men were everywhere captains of industry, not employees; not looking to a distant city to find out what they might do, but looking about among their neighbors, finding credit according to their character, not according to their connections, finding credit in proportion to what was known to be in them and behind them, not in proportion to the securities they held that were approved where they were not known. In order to start an enterprise now, you have to be authenticated, in a perfectly impersonal way, not according to yourself, but according to what you own that somebody else approves of your owning. [...] Is that freedom? That is dependence, not freedom.

The clearest statement of C6.13's loss of local, independent standing: credit by neighbours and character gives way to distant, impersonal authentication. Note for C6.7: the vocabulary of men "on the make" and "captains of industry" here belongs to the reformer, used against the men "already made."

### wilson.06 · *New Freedom* ch. I — the corporation as a tenement house

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 433–435
- **Checked:** text file

> I liken that to our great modern industrial enterprises. A corporation is very like a large tenement house; it isn't the premises of a single commercial family; it is just as much a public affair as a tenement house is a network of public highways.

Wilson's own model is the reverse of a Self "prior to its environment": the corporation is a shared building whose corridors are public, to be lit and policed (cf. ch. IX, lines 3540–3543).

### wilson.07 · *New Freedom* ch. III — "the prosperity of the country depends upon them"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for, as the men's belief), C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. III "Freemen Need No Guardians"; lines 1263–1282
- **Checked:** text file

> Some of the men who have exercised this control are excellent fellows; they really believe that the prosperity of the country depends upon them. They really believe that if the leadership of economic development in this country dropped from their hands, the rest of us are too muddle-headed to undertake the task. They not only comprehend the power of the United States within their grasp, but they comprehend it within their imagination. [...]
>
> As a matter of fact, their thought does not cover the processes of their own undertakings. [...] Modern industry depends upon technical knowledge; and all that these gentlemen did was to manage the external features of great combinations and their financial operation, which had very little to do with the intimate skill with which the enterprises were conducted.

Support for "from which all value is imagined to flow," but the imagining is done by a few men about themselves, not by an organization. Their defence is trusteeship ("guardians," "trustees" run through the chapter), not pioneer freedom. Compare line 1189: "it is my own business that I have a vision of," the narrow frame put down to position, not to a Self-ideology.

### wilson.08 · *New Freedom* ch. VIII — trusts made by "the deliberate planning will, of men"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VIII "Monopoly, or Opportunity?"; lines 2814–2822
- **Checked:** text file

> Big business is no doubt to a large extent necessary and natural. The development of business upon a great scale, upon a great scale of co-operation, is inevitable, and, let me add, is probably desirable. But that is a very different matter from the development of trusts, because the trusts have not grown. They have been artificially created; they have been put together, not by natural processes, but by the will, the deliberate planning will, of men who were more powerful than their neighbors in the business world, and who wished to make their power secure against competition.

The will behind the trust is men's, not the organization's. Wilson also accepts big business as such ("probably desirable"), so his target is narrower than "corporatism."

### wilson.09 · *New Freedom* ch. VIII — "as if it belonged to them"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 3011–3016
- **Checked:** text file

> you know just how close the whole thing is knitted together in our industrial system, and how great the temptation is. These twenty-four gentlemen administer that corporation as if it belonged to them. The amazing thing to me is that the people of the United States have not seen that the administration of a great business like that is not a private affair; it is a public affair.

The United States Steel Corporation is described as the property-like extension of twenty-four men. The corporation is the instrument; the Self in question, if any, is theirs.

### wilson.10 · *New Freedom* ch. VIII — no one "planned the system"; "an amalgam"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 3139–3147, 3217–3223
- **Checked:** text file

> I do not suspect that any man has deliberately planned the system. I am not so uninstructed and misinformed as to suppose that there is a deliberate and malevolent combination somewhere to dominate the government of the United States. I merely say that, by certain processes, now well known, and perhaps natural in themselves, there has come about an extraordinary and very sinister concentration in the control of business in the country. [...] You know that the chemist distinguishes between a chemical combination and an amalgam. [...] whereas an amalgam has a mere physical union created by pressure from without. Now, you can destroy that mere physical contact without hurting the individual elements, and this community of interest is an amalgam; you can break it up without hurting any one of the single interests combined.

Against a "singular Self" twice over: the concentration has no single will (contrast wilson.08, where each trust does), and the whole is an amalgam, not a unity. Wilson is not consistent about will: single trusts are willed, the system is not.

### wilson.11 · *New Freedom* ch. VIII — business organization "overtop[s] all states"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for, on scale)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 3195–3203
- **Checked:** text file

> The organization of business has become more centralized, vastly more centralized, than the political organization of the country itself. Corporations have come to cover greater areas than states; have come to live under a greater variety of laws than the citizen himself, have excelled states in their budgets and loomed bigger than whole commonwealths in their influence over the lives and fortunes of entire communities of men. Centralized business has built up vast structures of organization and equipment which overtop all states and seem to have no match or competitor except the federal government itself.

Supports the "expansive" scale of SPEC §9.3, and the sense of an organization that answers to no frame its own size. Note the word Wilson chooses: "structures."

### wilson.12 · *New Freedom* ch. VIII — "the market in your hand"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 3178–3184
- **Checked:** text file

> Let me say again that I am not impugning the motives of the men in Wall Street. They may think that that is the best way to create prosperity for the country. When you have got the market in your hand, does honesty oblige you to turn the palm upside down and empty it? If you have got the market in your hand and believe that you understand the interest of the country better than anybody else, is it patriotic to let it go? I can imagine them using this argument to themselves.

The self-justification Wilson imagines is patriotism and superior understanding, not individual freedom or the self-made man. It is still a one-frame view ("better than anybody else"), but a guardian's, not a pioneer's.

### wilson.13 · *New Freedom* ch. IX — "administer our spiritual life"; "the creature of our own hands"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C6.7 (against), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IX "Benevolence, or Justice?"; lines 3378–3384, 3424–3425
- **Checked:** text file

> Those who administer our physical life, therefore, administer our spiritual life; and if we are going to carry out the fine purpose of that great chorus which supporters of the third party sang almost with religious fervor, then we have got to find out through whom these purposes of humanity are going to be realized. It is a mere enterprise, so far as that part of it is concerned, of making the monopolies philanthropic. [...] Shall we admit that the creature of our own hands is stronger than we are?

The total reach of SPEC §9.3 is here (physical life governs spiritual life), and so is a disguise word, philanthropy or "benevolence," which the chapter title sets against justice. But the monopoly is "the creature of our own hands": made, not self-originating.

### wilson.14 · *New Freedom* ch. XI — big business as timid wards

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XI "The Emancipation of Business"; lines 4430–4440; cf. ch. VII, lines 2712–2715
- **Checked:** text file

> are afraid,--afraid to venture out into the great world on their own merits and their own skill. Think of it, a nation full of genius and yet paralyzed by timidity! The timidity of the business men of America is to me nothing less than amazing. They are tied to the apron strings of the government at Washington. They go about to seek favors. They say: "For pity's sake, don't expose us to the weather of the world; put some homelike cover over us. Protect us. See to it that foreign men don't come in and match their brains with ours."

As Wilson tells it, the corporate leaders speak the language of *protection*, not self-reliance; ch. VII says the tariff has "robbed Americans of their independence, resourcefulness, and self-reliance." This is a campaign caricature, but it is the opposite of C6.7's picture of monopolists talking like pioneers.

### brandeis.01 · *Other People's Money* ch. I — corporations as the bankers' "subjects"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. I "Our Financial Oligarchy"; lines 417–426
- **Checked:** text file

> The dominant element in our financial oligarchy is the investment banker. Associated banks, trust companies and life insurance companies are his tools. Controlled railroads, public service and industrial corporations are his subjects. Though properly but middlemen, these bankers bestride as masters America's business world, so that practically no large enterprise can be undertaken successfully without their participation or approval. These bankers are, of course, able men possessed of large fortunes; but the most potent factor in their control of business is not the possession of extraordinary ability or huge wealth. The key to their power is Combination

For Brandeis the corporation is a tool or subject; the master is a man. Two qualifications: the power comes from "Combination," a structure, not from personal greatness; and at line 586 the banker "becomes usually the dominant spirit" of a board, so the board's spirit is a person's.

### brandeis.02 · *Other People's Money* ch. I — "the people's own gold"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I; lines 714–733
- **Checked:** text file

> They control the people through the people's own money. If the bankers' power were commensurate only with their wealth, they would have relatively little influence on American business. [...] The Astor wealth is static. The wealth of the Morgan associates is dynamic. The power and the growth of power of our financial oligarchs comes from wielding the savings and quick capital of others. [...] The fetters which bind the people are forged from the people's own gold.

The book's title thesis is the reverse of "from which all value is imagined to flow": the value flows *in*, from depositors and policy-holders, and the oligarchs only steer it.

### brandeis.03 · *Other People's Money* ch. I — "good judges of their own deservings"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I, section "Power and Pelf"; lines 814–826
- **Checked:** text file

> Power begets wealth; and added wealth opens ever new opportunities for the acquisition of wealth and power. [...] Occupying, as they so frequently do, the inconsistent position of being at the same time seller and buyer, the standard for so-called compensation actually applied, is not the "Rule of reason", but "All the traffic will bear." And this is true even where there is no sinister motive. The weakness of human nature prevents men from being good judges of their own deservings.

Close to "admitting no frame but its own," but Brandeis puts it down to an old, ordinary weakness, not to a new idea of the Self. His cure is structural (separate the functions), as with the separation of powers (lines 470–478).

### brandeis.04 · *Other People's Money* ch. II — "The Protection of Pseudo-Ethics"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C9.8 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II "How the Combiners Combine"; lines 1270–1276, 1290–1291
- **Checked:** text file

> Despotism, be it financial or political, is vulnerable, unless it is believed to rest upon a moral sanction. The longing for freedom is ineradicable. It will express itself in protest against servitude and inaction, unless the striving for freedom be made to seem immoral. Long ago monarchs invented, as a preservative of absolutism, the fiction of "The divine right of kings." Bankers, imitating royalty, invented recently that precious rule of so-called "Ethics," [...] The "Ethical" basis of the rule must be that the interests of the combined bankers are superior to the interests of the rest of the community.

A clean C8.7 case: domination dressed in a moral word. It also fits "admitting no frame but its own" (the bankers' interests set above the community's). For C6.7: the disguise word Brandeis finds is "Ethics," not pioneer freedom.

### brandeis.05 · *Other People's Money* ch. II — "indeed of manhood itself"; "the hero, the martyr or the slave"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 1363–1372, 1393–1399
- **Checked:** text file

> _Third:_ But far more serious even than the suppression of competition is the suppression of industrial liberty, indeed of manhood itself, which this overweening financial power entails. [...] Its blighting and benumbing effect extends as well to the small and seemingly independent business man, to the vast army of professional men and others directly dependent upon "Big Business," and to many another; [...]
>
> Here and there you will find a hero,--red-blooded, and courageous,--loving manhood more than wealth, place or security,--who dared to fight for independence and won. Here and there you may find the martyr, who resisted in silence and suffered with resignation. But America, which seeks "the greatest good of the greatest number," cannot be content with conditions that fit only the hero, the martyr or the slave.

Brandeis's version of Wilson's "submerged": the concentration crushes the independent self of the small man and the professional, the business class included. Against reading the corporation as a *carrier* of selfhood for those inside it.

### brandeis.06 · *Other People's Money* ch. III — the director as trustee

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. III "Interlocking Directorates"; lines 1508–1518
- **Checked:** text file

> It will merely give full legal sanction to the fundamental law of morals and of human nature: that "No man can serve two masters." The surprising fact is that a principle of equity so firmly rooted should have been departed from at all in dealing with corporations. For no rule of law has, in other connections, been more rigorously applied, than that which prohibits a trustee from occupying inconsistent positions, from dealing with himself, or from using his fiduciary position for personal profit. And a director of a corporation is as obviously a trustee as persons holding similar positions in an unincorporated association, or in a private trust estate, who are called specifically by that name.

In Brandeis the corporation is a beneficiary with its own interests, and the danger is the director who serves himself or another company. The corporation is betrayed by men, not a Self that swallows them. (Maitland's corporate-personality theory is the place to test the opposite view.)

### brandeis.07 · *Other People's Money* ch. III — "the pull of privilege for the push of manhood"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. III; lines 1409–1417
- **Checked:** text file

> The practice of interlocking directorates is the root of many evils. It offends laws human and divine. Applied to rival corporations, it tends to the suppression of competition and to violation of the Sherman law. Applied to corporations which deal with each other, it tends to disloyalty and to violation of the fundamental law that no man can serve two masters. In either event it tends to inefficiency; for it removes incentive and destroys soundness of judgment. It is undemocratic, for it rejects the platform: "A fair field and no favors,"--substituting the pull of privilege for the push of manhood.

The self-made, fair-field vocabulary is the reformer's weapon against the combination, as in Wilson.

### brandeis.08 · *Other People's Money* ch. VII — Morgan's claim to have "initiated" everything

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for, as the bankers' claim), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII "Big Men and Little Business"; lines 3120–3128
- **Checked:** text file

> J. P. Morgan & Co. declare, in their letter to the Pujo Committee, that "practically all the railroad and industrial development of this country has taken place initially through the medium of the great banking houses." That statement is entirely unfounded in fact. On the contrary nearly every such contribution to our comfort and prosperity was "initiated" _without_ their aid. The "great banking houses" came into relation with these enterprises, either after success had been attained, or upon "reorganization" after the possibility of success had been demonstrated, but the funds of the hardy pioneers, who had risked their all, were exhausted.

The nearest primary statement of "from which all value is imagined to flow," and it comes from Morgan's own firm. It is also the nearest thing to C6.7: the combiner claims the originator's role. But the word "pioneers" is Brandeis's, and he gives it to the people the bankers displaced.

### brandeis.09 · *Other People's Money* ch. VII — 228 local companies under one house

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VII, section "Trusts and Financial Concentration"; lines 3493–3515
- **Checked:** text file

> Practically every trust created has destroyed the financial independence of some communities and of many properties; for it has centered the financing of a large part of whole lines of business in New York, and this usually with one of a few banking houses. [...] Before the combinations were effected, nearly every one of these companies was owned largely by those who managed it, and had been financed, to a large extent, in the place, or in the state, in which it was located. When the Steel Trust was formed all these concerns came under one management. Thereafter, the financing of each of these 228 corporations (and some which were later acquired) had to be done through or with the consent of J. P. Morgan & Co. _That was the greatest step in financial concentration ever taken._

Concrete evidence for C6.13's loss of local, independent standing: owner-managers financed in their own town become managed units financed from New York.

### brandeis.10 · *Other People's Money* ch. VIII — Harriman: the reach of one man, and its limit

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. VIII "A Curse of Bigness"; lines 3827–3832, 3857–3863
- **Checked:** text file

> "Mr. Harriman may journey by steamship from New York to New Orleans, thence by rail to San Francisco, across the Pacific Ocean to China, and, returning by another route to the United States, may go to Ogden by any one of three rail lines, and thence to Kansas City or Omaha, without leaving the deck or platform of a carrier which he controls, and without duplicating any part of his journey. [...]
>
> On September 9, 1909, less than twelve years after Mr. Harriman first became a director in the Union Pacific, he died from overwork at the age of 61. But it was not death only that had set a limit to his achievements. The multiplicity of his interests prevented him from performing for his other railroads the great services that had won him a world-wide reputation as manager and rehabilitator of the Union Pacific and the Southern Pacific.

The quoted Interstate Commerce Commission line is the best image in either book of a self extended to continental, even global, scale. But it is one man's self, and it meets the body's limit. Open question 4 in miniature: the structure lets a person grow past his limits, and then he breaks.

### brandeis.11 · *Other People's Money* ch. VIII — Forbes the builder, "a great adventurer"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VIII, section "The Burlington"; lines 3956–3961, 3990–4004
- **Checked:** text file

> For in nearly every case the absorption into a great system of a theretofore independent railroad has involved the loss of financial independence to some community, property or men, who thereby become subjects or satellites of the Money Trust. [...] The Burlington--one of Boston's finest achievements--was the creation of John M. Forbes. He was a builder; not a combiner, or banker, or wizard of finance. He was a simple, hard-working business man. [...] He was in the best sense of the term, a great adventurer. Thus equipped, Mr. Forbes entered, in 1852, upon those railroad enterprises which later developed into the Chicago, Burlington & Quincy. Largely with his own money and that of friends who confided in him, he built these railroads

Brandeis keeps the pioneer ideal and gives it to the builder against the combiner ("Mr. Forbes was the pioneer among Boston railroad-builders," line 4031). The vocabulary was not handed to monopoly here; it was used against it.

### brandeis.12 · *Other People's Money* ch. X — "the fundamental laws of human limitations"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** ch. X "The Inefficiency of the Oligarchs"; lines 4538–4542, 4557–4563
- **Checked:** text file

> This failure of banker-management is not surprising. The surprise is that men should have supposed it would succeed. For banker-management contravenes the fundamental laws of human limitations: _First_, that no man can serve two masters; _second_, that a man cannot at the same time do many things well. [...] The real test of efficiency comes when success has to be struggled for; when natural or legal conditions limit the charges which may be made for the goods sold or service rendered. Our banker-managed railroads have recently been subjected to such a test, and they have failed to pass it. "It is only," says Goethe, "when working within limitations, that the master is disclosed."

Brandeis's diagnosis matches the essay's pattern, a self that drops its limits, and quotes Goethe for the bounded self. But the overreaching self is a handful of named men (Morgan, Baker, Rockefeller, lines 4626–4640), not the organization; and Brandeis thinks the limits reassert themselves as failure.

### brandeis.13 · *Other People's Money* ch. VIII — Morgan's defence: necessity and "industrial progress"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; lines 3695–3704
- **Checked:** text file

> J. P. Morgan & Co. (in their letter of defence to the Pujo Committee) urge the needs of Big Business as the justification for financial concentration. They declare that what they euphemistically call "coöperation" is "simply a further result of the necessity for handling great transactions"; that "the country obviously requires not only the larger individual banks, but demands also that those banks shall coöperate to perform efficiently the country's business"; and that "a step backward along this line would mean a halt in industrial progress that would affect every wage-earner from the Atlantic to the Pacific."

The one place either book quotes the combiners' own defence: it rests on necessity, efficiency and progress, and calls combination "coöperation." Not the self-made man. If C6.7 holds, it needs other witnesses (Carnegie's "Wealth," Rockefeller, trade-association speeches).
