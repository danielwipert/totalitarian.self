# Sinclair Lewis, *Babbitt* (1922)

- **Edition used:** original English. New York: Harcourt, Brace and Company, 1922 (file line 22)
- **Text file:** `texts/lewis-babbitt.txt` (Gutenberg #1156, clean)
- **Citation form:** chapter and section (e.g. ch. XIV §III), plus file line
- **Claims to test:** C6.13, C8.1–C8.7, C7.*, C9.*

## What kind of evidence this is

*Babbitt* is a satirical novel, not a document. It shows how a sharp observer saw the commercial city of 1920–22 (the story opens "in April, 1920", line 111). The narrator's comments are Lewis's satire. The characters' speeches are parody, and none of them is Lewis's own view. That includes Babbitt's address (.09), and the radical Doane (.07) too. So the novel can show what vocabulary was available to be mocked in 1922, and how an observer understood the forces at work. It cannot show how widespread anything was. For that, use *Middletown* (1929).

## Reading summary

**What replaced township and congregation (C6.13).** The novel's answer is the lunch club, the lodge, the Chamber of Commerce and the church run as a business. These are not the absence of association; they are association multiplied. Joining "was required" of a decent man, for four stated reasons: "It was the thing to do", business, honorifics, and a night away from home (.10). The church is "a true community center" whose pastor is "proud to be known as primarily a business man" (.11). Babbitt's religion is kept because it is "respectable, and beneficial to one’s business" (.11). The Boosters fine members for using anything but nicknames, and sell to each other (.01). The pattern is the one C6.13 describes: congregation and association taken over by business. But it complicates "wore away". The voluntary associations did not wane. They grew stronger and became the means of enforcing conformity, the Good Citizens' League above all (.13, .14, .17). The new force is the old one, Tocqueville's associations and the tyranny of the majority, joined together.

**The disguise word (C8.2, C8.6).** This is the most important result, and it cuts two ways.
- *For the structure.* The narrator says outright that "the large national advertisers fix [...] what he believed to be his individuality" (.03). The correspondence-school ads sell "How to create a strong personality" and "How to become a rational, powerful and original thinker" (.05). Chum Frink calls the man who writes the tobacco ads "_the_ American genius", the proof of "our American thought and originality" (.08). The Self's vocabulary has reached advertising and is aimed at the consumer, six years before Bernays.
- *Against the date and the speaker.* "Individuality" in .03 is the *narrator's* word, used against Babbitt. Babbitt never says it. His own words and those of his circle are "pep", "Regular Fellow"/"Regular Guy", "He-man", "Good Fellows", "Solid Citizen", "live wire", "hustler", "one-hundred-per-cent". These are words of manliness, energy and belonging, not of self-expression. Conformity in 1922 is not disguised at all. It is praised as conformity: "sane standardization" (.09). The League demands "a wholesome sameness of thought, dress, painting, morals, and vocabulary" in the name of "American Democracy" (.17). Goods mean *rank* (the car, .04), as in Bernays. In 1922 the language of the Self belongs to the rebels and critics: Babbitt's "I’m going to run my own life!" (.15), Doane's "time and energy to be individual in" (.07), and the closing "you knew what you wanted to do and did it" (.18). So the novel supports the claim that this vocabulary was once used to *expose* conformity. And it places the switch *after* 1922: the satirist still uses "individuality" against the conformist, and the conformist does not yet use it for himself.

**Belonging or fear (C8.4).** Both, in sequence. The first pull is belonging. The Boosters' button makes him "feel loyal and important" (.01); joining is "the thing to do" (.10). When he rebels, the enforcement is open and frightening: social boycott, "Fear sat beside him" (.13), lost business (.15), "We don’t mind a little bullying" (.14). But what brings him back is being *coaxed*. When his wife falls ill, his neighbors are kind and the League welcomes him "almost tearful with joy at being coaxed instead of bullied" (.16). Lewis makes the fear the stick, and the wish to belong the thing that actually works.

**The revolt as accessory (C8.1).** Partly. Babbitt's rebellion borrows a ready-made image ("one of these Bohemians you read about", .12). The Bunch it leads him into is "bound by the exceedingly straitened conventions [...] of their life of pleasure and freedom" from the start, and it "changed half its membership" every month (.12). That is a False Group (C8.3) in all but name. But the sequence is not C8.1's. There is no genuine first expression that later converges and decays. Babbitt's real feeling (unease, the "fairy child", grief for Paul) never finds a form of its own. It moves straight from one ready-made group to another. Lewis's account starts with the accessory; the accessory is not the end of a process.

**Carriers (C9.\*).** The boosters, the lunch clubs and the League show the business class as the carrier of enforced sameness. It is linked to the press (Colonel Snow's papers), the church, the Y.M.C.A. and the Legion (.17). This fits a reading of §9.3 as corporatism at the level of the town. It does not fit the Totalitarian *Self*. What these groups carry is the *suppression* of the self, under the name of "Good Citizenship", and not its inflation. The revivalist strand (relevant to C9.2) appears in Mike Monday's revival, used by the Chamber of Commerce to turn workmen "from wages and hours to higher things" (ch. VII §IV, lines 3541–3544). There it serves business, not reason's flight.

## Passages

### babbitt.01 · ch. I §IV; ch. XXI — the Boosters' button; nicknames by fine

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.4 (for), C8.3 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I §IV; ch. XXI; `texts/lewis-babbitt.txt` lines 387–392, 9224–9227, 9252–9256
- **Checked:** text file

> Last, he stuck in his lapel the Boosters’ Club button. With the conciseness of great art the button displayed two words: “Boosters--Pep!” It made Babbitt feel loyal and important. It associated him with Good Fellows, with men who were nice and human, and important in business circles. It was his V.C., his Legion of Honor ribbon, his Phi Beta Kappa key.

> There was a fine of ten cents for calling a Fellow Booster by anything but his nickname at a lunch [...] Though the object of the club was good-fellowship, yet they never lost sight of the importance of doing a little more business.

A badge worn in order to belong, which makes him feel "loyal and important". It stands in for military honor and scholarship. The club enforces intimacy by a fine and runs it as trade. The fellowship looks warm and is commercial underneath (C8.3).

### babbitt.02 · ch. II §I — the standard bedroom

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C7.9 (for)
- **Location:** ch. II §I; lines 522–526, 542–550
- **Checked:** text file

> The room displayed a modest and pleasant color-scheme, after one of the best standard designs of the decorator who “did the interiors” for most of the speculative-builders’ houses in Zenith.

> It was a masterpiece among bedrooms, right out of Cheerful Modern Houses for Medium Incomes. Only it had nothing to do with the Babbitts, nor with any one else. [...] It had the air of being a very good room in a very good hotel. [...]

> Every second house in Floral Heights had a bedroom precisely like this.

The home, the inmost private space, is supplied from a catalogue. It "had nothing to do with the Babbitts". This is the narrator's satire, and it is a picture of the homeless home C7.9 describes, not a finding.

### babbitt.03 · ch. VII §III; ch. V §II — advertisers "fix what he believed to be his individuality"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C8.6 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VII §III; ch. V §II; lines 3420–3428, 1858–1862
- **Checked:** text file

> Just as he was an Elk, a Booster, and a member of the Chamber of Commerce, just as the priests of the Presbyterian Church determined his every religious belief and the senators who controlled the Republican Party decided in little smoky rooms in Washington what he should think about disarmament, tariff, and Germany, so did the large national advertisers fix the surface of his life, fix what he believed to be his individuality. These standard advertised wares--toothpastes, socks, tires, cameras, instantaneous hot-water heaters--were his symbols and proofs of excellence; at first the signs, then the substitutes, for joy and passion and wisdom.

> A stranger suddenly dropped into the business-center of Zenith could not have told whether he was in a city of Oregon or Georgia, Ohio or Maine, Oklahoma or Manitoba. But to Babbitt every inch was individual and stirring.

This is the key passage: standardized goods taken as one's individuality, in 1922. It is C8.2's structure exactly. Lodge, church, party and advertiser all appear as one series of outside authorities. But "individuality" is the *narrator's* diagnosis, not Babbitt's word for himself. So it dates the critique, not the consumer idiom (C8.6).

### babbitt.04 · ch. VI §III — the car as rank

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.6 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VI §III; lines 2641–2653
- **Checked:** text file

> It was much more than a study of transportation. It was an aspiration for knightly rank. In the city of Zenith, in the barbarous twentieth century, a family’s motor indicated its social rank as precisely as the grades of the peerage determined the rank of an English family [...] and where Babbitt as a boy had aspired to the presidency, his son Ted aspired to a Packard twin-six and an established position in the motored gentry.

Goods are read as *rank*, not as self-expression. This is the vocabulary of station, as in Bernays (bernays-propaganda.07), which supports C8.6's claim that consumers still speak of position. It also shows the shift C8.7 traces: the son's ambition moves from office to purchase.

### babbitt.05 · ch. VI §III — "How to create a strong personality"

- **Claims:** C8.6 (complicates), C8.4 (for), C5.14 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VI §III; lines 2782–2786, 2911–2914
- **Checked:** text file

> How to create a strong personality.

> How to become a rational, powerful and original thinker.

> How to be a MASTER MAN!

> One benefactor implored, “Don’t be a Wallflower-- Be More Popular and Make More Money-- You Can Ukulele or Sing Yourself into Society!

Lewis parodies the mail-order ads, so these are not real ad copy, but they are true to the genre. "Personality" and "original thinker" are sold to the consumer before 1928, and C8.6 should allow for that. But personality here is a *tool* for success and popularity ("Sing Yourself into Society"), not something expressed. The motive is belonging and money.

### babbitt.06 · ch. V §III — Paul Riesling and the duty answer

- **Claims:** C7.9 (for), C6.13 (complicates), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** ch. V §III; lines 2300–2306, 2315–2319
- **Checked:** text file

> They hate the whole peppy, boosting, go-ahead game, and they’re bored by their wives and think their families are fools--at least when they come to forty or forty-five they’re bored--and they hate business

> “Well we know--not just in the Bible alone, but it stands to reason--a man who doesn’t buckle down and do his duty, even if it does bore him sometimes, is nothing but a--well, he’s simply a weakling. Mollycoddle, in fact!

Paul, the violinist and the novel's frustrated "genuine expression", names the hidden misery under the booster front: the man who belongs on the outside and is cut off from his own feelings (C7.9). Babbitt answers with duty and the Bible. In 1920 the older frames still speak, though thinly; they have not been replaced by the vocabulary of the Self.

### babbitt.07 · ch. VII §V — Doane: goods standardized, yes; thought, no

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII §V; lines 3599–3602, 3610–3616
- **Checked:** text file

> “Standardization is excellent, _per se_. When I buy an Ingersoll watch or a Ford, I get a better tool for less money, and I know precisely what I’m getting, and that leaves me more time and energy to be individual in.

> “No, what I fight in Zenith is standardization of thought, and, of course, the traditions of competition. The real villains of the piece are the clean, kind, industrious Family Men who use every known brand of trickery and cruelty to insure the prosperity of their cubs. The worst thing about these fellows is that they’re so good and, in their work at least, so intelligent. You can’t hate them properly, and yet their standardized minds are the enemy.

Lewis's radical refuses to blame the goods. Mass products free time "to be individual in". This is the opposite of the accessory: the standard thing as a plain tool, not as a token of the self. The critique still uses the word "individual" against conformity, as C8.2 says it once did.

### babbitt.08 · ch. VIII §III — Chum Frink: the ad writer as "the American genius"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VIII §III; lines 4317–4325
- **Checked:** text file

> You can’t go wrong on ’em; you know what sentiments any decent go-ahead fellow must have if he plays the game, and you stick right to ’em. But the poetry of industrialism, now there’s a literary line where you got to open up new territory. Do you know the fellow who’s really _the_ American genius? The fellow who you don’t know his name and I don’t either, but his work ought to be preserved so’s future generations can judge our American thought and originality to-day? Why, the fellow that writes the Prince Albert Tobacco ads!

The popular poet admits that feeling is supplied in advance ("you know what sentiments any decent go-ahead fellow must have"). He gives "genius" and "originality", the Romantic Self's words, to advertising. This is C6.13's passage of the Self's vocabulary to business, in satire.

### babbitt.09 · ch. XIV §III — "the Standardized American Citizen" (Real Estate Board address)

- **Claims:** C8.2 (against), C8.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIV §III; lines 6589–6597, 6635–6638, 6753–6762. **Verified:** the speech is the Annual Address at the Zenith Real Estate Board dinner (lines 6461–6463), not the Boosters' Club.
- **Checked:** text file

> Here’s the specifications of the Standardized American Citizen! Here’s the new generation of Americans: fellows with hair on their chests and smiles in their eyes and adding-machines in their offices. [...]

> “‘So! In my clumsy way I have tried to sketch the Real He-man, the fellow with Zip and Bang.

> The extraordinary, growing, and sane standardization of stores, offices, streets, hotels, clothes, and newspapers throughout the United States shows how strong and enduring a type is ours.

> but a God-fearing, hustling, successful, two-fisted Regular Guy, who belongs to some church with pep and piety to it, who belongs to the Boosters or the Rotarians or the Kiwanis, to the Elks or Moose or Red Men or Knights of Columbus or any one of a score of organizations of good, jolly, kidding, laughing, sweating, upstanding, lend-a-handing Royal Good Fellows

In the conformist's own mouth in 1922, conformity is not disguised as the Self. It is boasted of as "standardization". The value words are manliness, energy and Americanness ("He-man", "Regular Guy", "pep"). That fits C8.7. It is against C8.2 for this date: the disguise word here is "American", not "self-expression". The ideal man "belongs" to a church and a club.

### babbitt.10 · ch. XVI §I — the "joiner": four reasons

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.4 (for), C6.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XVI §I; lines 7269–7287
- **Checked:** text file

> His clubs and associations were food comfortable to his spirit.

> Of a decent man in Zenith it was required that he should belong to one, preferably two or three, of the innumerous “lodges” and prosperity-boosting lunch-clubs [...] There were four reasons for joining these orders: It was the thing to do. It was good for business, since lodge-brothers frequently became customers. It gave to Americans unable to become Geheimräte or Commendatori such unctuous honorifics as High Worthy Recording Scribe and Grand Hoogow to add to the commonplace distinctions of Colonel, Judge, and Professor. And it permitted the swaddled American husband to stay away from home for one evening a week. The lodge was his piazza, his pavement café.

Tocqueville's associations are still here, and "innumerous", but joining is "required", and the reasons are conformity, trade, rank and escape. Nothing in them is common self-government. They have not worn away (against a literal reading of C6.13); they have changed their purpose. "Food comfortable to his spirit": the lodge takes the congregation's place.

### babbitt.11 · ch. XVI §II–III — the church as business

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C9.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XVI §II–III; lines 7322–7326, 7335–7336, 7414–7417, 7437–7442
- **Checked:** text file

> For the Saturday edition of the _Evening Advocate_ he wrote editorials on “The Manly Man’s Religion” and “The Dollars and Sense Value of Christianity,” which were printed in bold type surrounded by a wiggly border. He often said that he was “proud to be known as primarily a business man” and that he certainly was not going to “permit the old Satan to monopolize all the pep and punch.”

> He had made his church a true community center. It contained everything but a bar.

> If you had asked Babbitt what his religion was, he would have answered in sonorous Boosters’-Club rhetoric, “My religion is to serve my fellow men, to honor my brother as myself, and to do my bit to make life happier for one and all.”

> The kernel of his practical religion was that it was respectable, and beneficial to one’s business, to be seen going to services; that the church kept the Worst Elements from being still worse; and that the pastor’s sermons, however dull they might seem at the time of taking, yet had a voodooistic power which “did a fellow good--kept him in touch with Higher Things.”

The congregation survives as an institution but speaks in the club's language ("Boosters’-Club rhetoric"). It is kept for respectability and business. This is C6.13's congregation giving way to club and market, from inside the church. The religion is neither fundamentalist nor reasoned; it is commercial.

### babbitt.12 · ch. XXIII §IV; ch. XXIX §III — the Bunch: rebellion with its own conventions

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.3 (for)
- **Location:** ch. XXIII §IV; ch. XXIX §III; lines 9869–9871, 12076–12079, 12091–12093, 12100–12102
- **Checked:** text file

> He wanted-- Oh, he wanted to be one of these Bohemians you read about. Studio parties. Wild lovely girls who were independent. Not necessarily bad. Certainly not! But not tame, like Floral Heights.

> When he went home, at two, he was fully a member of the Bunch, and all the week thereafter he was bound by the exceedingly straitened conventions, the exceedingly wearing demands, of their life of pleasure and freedom.

> Every member of the Bunch was expected to telephone to every other member at least once a week.

> “Old Georgie,” was now a pioneer of the Bunch, since each month it changed half its membership

The rebellion copies a picture from print ("Bohemians you read about"). The counter-group is as binding as the Boosters, and its membership is unstable. That is C8.3's False Group, coherent in appearance and not in substance. But there is no genuine first expression that decays (C8.1). The revolt is an accessory from the start, and Lewis shows no first stage.

### babbitt.13 · ch. XXIX §IV — the Good Citizens' League: "conform to decent standards"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates), C8.2 (against), C6.1 (for), C9.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXIX §IV; lines 12372–12380, 12438–12445
- **Checked:** text file

> Oh, the G. C. L. has to have some other ostensible purposes--frinstance here in Zenith I think it ought to support the park-extension project and the City Planning Committee--and then, too, it should have a social aspect, being made up of the best people--have dances and so on, especially as one of the best ways it can put the kibosh on cranks is to apply this social boycott business to folks big enough so you can’t reach ’em otherwise. Then if that don’t work, the G. C. L. can finally send a little delegation around to inform folks that get too flip that they got to conform to decent standards and quit shooting off their mouths so free.

> That evening Babbitt dined alone. He saw all the Clan of Good Fellows peering through the restaurant window, spying on him. Fear sat beside him

A voluntary association set up to enforce conformity, by social boycott and then delegation. Conformity is called by its name ("conform to decent standards"), not dressed as the Self. Enforcement here runs on fear, which complicates C8.4's focus on the wish to belong. Tocqueville's two forces, association and majority opinion, here work as one, which shows how checks that bind "by consent" can be used to coerce (C6.1).

### babbitt.14 · ch. XXXII §III — "We don’t mind a little bullying"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates), C9.8 (for)
- **Location:** ch. XXXII §III; lines 13292–13300, 13305–13310
- **Checked:** text file

> Something black and unfamiliar and ferocious spoke from Babbitt: “Now, you look here, Charley! I’m damned if I’m going to be bullied into joining anything, not even by you plutes!”

> “We’re not bullying anybody,” Dr. Billing began, but Colonel Snow thrust him aside with, “Certainly we are! We don’t mind a little bullying, if it’s necessary.

> You’ve stood in, you and your father-in-law, with some of the most substantial and forward-looking interests in town, like my friends of the Street Traction Company, and my papers have given you a lot of boosts. Well, you can’t expect the decent citizens to go on aiding you if you intend to side with precisely the people who are trying to undermine us.”

The newspaper owner, the traction company and the League act together and say openly that they are coercing him. The sanction is economic. This is corporatism at the level of the town (C9.8). Belonging is not the only motive here; there is also the threat to his livelihood.

> I don’t care! Good for her to get waked up a little. And I’m going to keep free. Of her and Tanis and the fellows at the club and everybody. I’m going to run my own life!”

> “But if it’s _the_ fad now, don’t you think you--”
>
> “No, I don’t! Oh, Myra, please quit nagging me about it. I’m sick of hearing about the confounded G.C.L. I almost wish I’d joined it when Verg first came around, and got it over. And maybe I’d’ve come in to-day if the committee hadn’t tried to bullyrag me, but, by God, as long as I’m a free-born independent American cit--”

In 1922 the vocabulary of self-rule ("run my own life", "free-born independent American") is the *rebel's*, used against the club. That supports C8.2's claim that it was once the language of exposure. His wife joins for fashion ("_the_ fad"). His partner joins for trade. The two motives are Thoreau's Fashion and Mandeville's interest (C8.7).

### babbitt.16 · ch. XXXIII §II — the return: "coaxed instead of bullied"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (for), C8.3 (against), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXXIII §II; lines 13871–13880, 13895–13907
- **Checked:** text file

> If once he had doubted his neighbors and the supreme charm of the Good Fellows, he was convinced now. You didn’t, he noted, “see Seneca Doane coming around with any flowers or dropping in to chat with the Missus,” but Mrs. Howard Littlefield brought to the hospital her priceless wine jelly (flavored with real wine); Orville Jones spent hours in picking out the kind of novels Mrs. Babbitt liked [...] Louetta Swanson knitted a pink bed-jacket

> Then did Babbitt, almost tearful with joy at being coaxed instead of bullied, at being permitted to stop fighting, at being able to desert without injuring his opinion of himself, cease utterly to be a domestic revolutionist. He patted Gunch’s shoulder, and next day he became a member of the Good Citizens’ League.
>
> Within two weeks no one in the League was more violent regarding the wickedness of Seneca Doane, the crimes of labor unions, the perils of immigration, and the delights of golf, morality, and bank-accounts than was George F. Babbitt.

The wish to belong, not fear, completes the return (C8.4). But the neighbors' kindness is real, and Lewis does not mock it. The Good Fellows are a real community in a crisis, which counts against calling them a False Group (C8.3). Neighborliness has not worn away (complicates C6.13). The sentence "without injuring his opinion of himself" is the core of the accessory: he gives in and his self-image stays intact.

### babbitt.17 · ch. XXXIV §I — "a wholesome sameness of thought, dress, painting, morals, and vocabulary"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C8.2 (against), C9.8 (for), C9.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXXIV §I; lines 13924–13939
- **Checked:** text file

> To the League belonged most of the prosperous citizens of Zenith. They were not all of the kind who called themselves “Regular Guys.” Besides these hearty fellows, these salesmen of prosperity, there were the aristocrats, that is, the men who were richer or had been rich for more generations: the presidents of banks and of factories, the land-owners, the corporation lawyers, the fashionable doctors [...] All of them agreed that the working-classes must be kept in their place; and all of them perceived that American Democracy did not imply any equality of wealth, but did demand a wholesome sameness of thought, dress, painting, morals, and vocabulary.
>
> In this they were like the ruling-class of any other country, particularly of Great Britain, but they differed in being more vigorous and in actually trying to produce the accepted standards which all classes, everywhere, desire, but usually despair of realizing.

The narrator's verdict: conformity is demanded in the name of "American Democracy". In 1922 that is the highest value word, as C8.7 predicts, and it is not "self-expression" (against C8.2 for this date). Lewis calls the League a ruling class "like the ruling-class of any other country". So he sees it as a version of an old pattern, not a new condition of the Self. The carrier is the whole business and professional class (C9.8). It does not look like a pair of opposites (C9.1).

### babbitt.18 · ch. XXXIV §VI — the ending: "you knew what you wanted to do and did it"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C7.9 (for), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XXXIV §VI; lines 14303–14322
- **Checked:** text file

> “Dad, I can’t stand it any more. Maybe it’s all right for some fellows. Maybe I’ll want to go back some day. But me, I want to get into mechanics. I think I’d get to be a good inventor. There’s a fellow that would give me twenty dollars a week in a factory right now.”

> practically, I’ve never done a single thing I’ve wanted to in my whole life! I don’t know ’s I’ve accomplished anything except just get along. [...] But I do get a kind of sneaking pleasure out of the fact that you knew what you wanted to do and did it. Well, those folks in there will try to bully you, and tame you down. Tell ’em to go to the devil! I’ll back you. Take your factory job, if you want to. Don’t be scared of the family. No, nor all of Zenith. Nor of yourself, the way I’ve been. Go ahead, old man! The world is yours!”

The novel ends with the language of personal wanting set against Zenith, as a good thing. In 1922 it still works as the language of exposure (C8.2). Babbitt's confession is C7.9's life cut off from its own wants. The irony is that Ted's "genuine" choice, a factory job and gadgets, is itself an ordinary one. Lewis leaves it open whether it will become another standard.
