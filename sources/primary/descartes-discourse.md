# Descartes, *Discourse on Method* (1637)

- **Edition used:** trans. John Veitch
- **Text file:** `texts/descartes-discourse.txt` (clean); also in Haldane & Ross vol. I
- **Standard reference** (for final citations): Adam & Tannery (AT) vol. VI; English: Cottingham, Stoothoff & Murdoch (CSM) vol. I
- **Claims to test:** C4.6, C4.7, C5.8

## Passages

### descartes-discourse.01 · Part IV — the cogito, and the mind needing no place

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for), C5.8 (for)
- **Location:** Part IV; `texts/descartes-discourse.txt` lines 880–900
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] this truth, I think, therefore I am (COGITO ERGO SUM), was so certain and of such evidence that no ground of doubt, however extravagant, could be alleged by the sceptics capable of shaking it [...] I thence concluded that I was a substance whose whole essence or nature consists only in thinking, and which, that it may exist, has need of no place, nor is dependent on any material thing; so that "I," that is to say, the mind by which I am what I am, is wholly distinct from the body, and is even more easily known than the latter, and is such, that although the latter were not, it would still continue to be all that it is.

This is the strongest text yet for the Self as *prior to the world* (C5.8): the "I" needs no place, depends on nothing material, and would continue to exist without the body. Descartes qualifies this a few pages later in the same work (descartes-discourse.02, .04), and again in *Meditations* VI (descartes-meditations.02).

### descartes-discourse.02 · Part V — "not sufficient that it be lodged in the human body exactly like a pilot in a ship"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C4.7 (complicates), C4.8 (for)
- **Location:** Part V, end; `texts/descartes-discourse.txt` lines 1541–1561
- **Checked:** text file

> I had after this described the reasonable soul, and shown that it could by no means be educed from the power of matter, as the other things of which I had spoken, but that it must be expressly created; and that it is not sufficient that it be lodged in the human body exactly like a pilot in a ship, unless perhaps to move its members, but that it is necessary for it to be joined and united more closely to the body, in order to have sensations and appetites similar to ours, and thus constitute a real man. [...] when we know how far they differ we much better comprehend the reasons which establish that the soul is of a nature wholly independent of the body, and that consequently it is not liable to die with the latter and, finally, because no other causes are observed capable of destroying it, we are naturally led thence to judge that it is immortal.

The pilot qualification is already in the 1637 *Discourse*, a few pages after Part IV (.01). So the *Discourse* is not the unqualified version that *Meditations* VI corrects: both works pair separation with union. Note the stated motive, immortality and the difference between men and brutes, which ties the separation to the old soul-question (C4.8).

### descartes-discourse.03 · Part IV — "I was not the only being in existence"

- **Claims:** C5.8 (against)
- **Location:** Part IV; `texts/descartes-discourse.txt` lines 936–941
- **Checked:** text file

> And to this I added that, since I knew some perfections which I did not possess, I was not the only being in existence (I will here, with your permission, freely use the terms of the schools); but, on the contrary, that there was of necessity some other more perfect Being upon whom I was dependent, and from whom I had received all that I possessed [...]

Immediately after the cogito, the "I" is shown to be dependent and received, not self-originating. Same move as descartes-meditations.06. The self here is prior to the *material* world (.01) but not to God.

### descartes-discourse.04 · Part VI — "lords and possessors of nature"; the mind "intimately dependent" on the body's organs

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C5.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI; `texts/descartes-discourse.txt` lines 1614–1628
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] in room of the speculative philosophy usually taught in the schools, to discover a practical, by means of which, knowing the force and action of fire, water, air the stars, the heavens, and all the other bodies that surround us, as distinctly as we know the various crafts of our artisans, we might also apply them in the same way to all the uses to which they are adapted, and thus render ourselves the lords and possessors of nature. And this is a result to be desired, not only in order to the invention of an infinity of arts [...] but also and especially for the preservation of health, which is without doubt, of all the blessings of this life, the first and fundamental one; for the mind is so intimately dependent upon the condition and relation of the organs of the body, that if any means can ever be found to render men wiser and more ingenious than hitherto, I believe that it is in medicine they must be sought for.

Two things in one sentence. The self stands over nature as its "lord and possessor", which fits the spirit of C5.8 (a self set against its world). But the mind is "so intimately dependent" on the body's organs that wisdom is to be sought in medicine, which sits badly with C4.6 taken alone and with .01's mind that "would still continue to be all that it is" without the body.
