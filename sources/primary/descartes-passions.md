## Passages

### descartes-passions.01 · Part I, arts. 5–6 — the soul does not animate the body

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, arts. 5–6, Haldane & Ross I p. 333 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 16552–16558, 16566–16577
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> [...] it has been thought that it was the absence of soul which caused these movements and this heat to cease; and thus, without any reason, it was thought that our natural heat and all the movements of our body depend on the soul: while in fact we ought on the contrary to believe that the soul quits us on death only because this heat ceases, and the organs which serve to move the body disintegrate. [...] let us consider that death never comes to pass by reason of the soul, but only because some one of the principal parts of the body decays; and we may judge that the body of a living man differs from that of a dead man just as does a watch or other automaton (i.e. a machine that moves of itself), when it is wound up [...]

The soul is stripped of its old role as the life of the body; life is mechanism. This sharpens the separation C4.6 describes: what is left to the soul is thought alone (art. 17), and the body is a machine that runs without it.

### descartes-passions.02 · Part I, art. 30 — "the soul is really joined to the whole body"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates), C4.7 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, art. 30, Haldane & Ross I p. 345 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 17188–17202
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> But in order to understand all these things more perfectly, we must know that the soul is really joined to the whole body, and that we cannot, properly speaking, say that it exists in any one of its parts to the exclusion of the others, because it is one and in some manner indivisible, owing to the disposition of its organs, which are so related to one another that when any one of them is removed, that renders the whole body defective ; and because it is of a nature which has no relation to extension, nor dimensions, nor other properties of the matter of which the body is composed, but only to the whole conglomerate of its organs [...] and because it does not become smaller owing to the cutting off of some portion of the body, but separates itself from it entirely when the union of its assembled organs is dissolved.

The late Descartes's union in its plainest form: the soul is joined to the *whole* body, not housed in a part. Yet the same sentence keeps the separation (no relation to extension; it "separates itself from it entirely" at death). It qualifies the separate mind without withdrawing it.

### descartes-passions.03 · Part I, arts. 31 and 34 — the gland, and two-way action

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for), C4.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, arts. 31, 34, Haldane & Ross I pp. 345, 347 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 17211–17222, 17285–17287, 17300–17312
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "in-the" corrected to "in the", "throngh" to "through")

> It is likewise necessary to know that although the soul is joined to the whole body, there is yet in that a certain part in which it exercises its functions more particularly than in all the others [...] the part of the body in which the soul exercises its functions immediately is in nowise the heart, nor the whole of the brain, but merely the most inward of all its parts, to wit, a certain very small gland [...]

> Let us then conceive here that the soul has its principal seat in the little gland which exists in the middle of the brain, from whence it radiates forth through all the remainder of the body [...] the small gland which is the main seat of the soul is so suspended between the cavities which contain the spirits that it can be moved by them in as many different ways as there are sensible diversities in the object, but that it may also be moved in diverse ways by the soul [...] Reciprocally, likewise, the machine of the body is so formed that from the simple fact that this gland is diversely moved by the soul, or by such other cause, whatever it is, it thrusts the spirits [...] into the muscles [...]

Two things acting on each other at a point: interaction presupposes two substances, so this is *for* separation (C4.6). But it is the opposite of a Mind "removed from the physical world" (C4.7's wording): Descartes's soul is placed, busy and constantly moved by the body. The received "separate Mind" keeps the dualism and drops the interaction.

### descartes-passions.04 · Part I, art. 47 — "but one soul"; all that opposes reason belongs to body

- **Claims:** C4.6 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, art. 47, Haldane & Ross I p. 353 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 17618–17627
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> For there is within us but one soul, and this soul has not in itself any diversity of parts; the same part that is subject to sense impressions is rational, and all the soul's appetites are acts of will. The error which has been committed in making it play the part of various personages, usually in opposition one to another, only proceeds from the fact that we have not properly distinguished its functions from those of the body, to which alone we must attribute every thing which can be observed in us that is opposed to our reason [...]

Descartes ends the old divided soul (rational vs. sensitive parts): the self is one, rational and willing, and inner conflict is relocated as a conflict between self and body. This is a strong text for "Self = Mind" and for the Self as a single will (spec §2).

### descartes-passions.05 · Part I, art. 39 — different passions because "all brains are not constituted in the same way"

- **Claims:** C4.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, art. 39, Haldane & Ross I p. 349 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 17420–17423
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> The same impression which a terrifying object makes on the gland, and which causes fear in certain men, may excite in others courage and confidence ; the reason of this is that all brains are not constituted in the same way [...]

Where Descartes explains why persons *differ* in feeling, he points to the body, not the soul. For the essay's "difference" (spec §2) this matters: in Descartes, temperamental difference between individuals is bodily; what is the same in all is the rational will (see .07).

### descartes-passions.06 · Part I, art. 41 — the will "can never be constrained"

- **Claims:** C5.8 (for), C4.6 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, art. 41, Haldane & Ross I p. 350 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 17454–17467
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; hyphenation joined)

> But the will is so free in its nature, that it can never be constrained ; and of the two sorts of thoughts which I have distinguished in the soul (of which the first are its actions, i.e. its desires, the others its passions [...]), the former are absolutely in its power, and can only be indirectly changed by the body [...] And the whole action of the soul consists in this, that solely because it desires something, it causes the little gland to which it is closely united to move in the way requisite to produce the effect which relates to this desire.

The will is the part of the self no body or world can force. This is the practical counterpart to the Med II priority (descartes-meditations.03): the self as an unconstrained source of action. Arts. 45–46 qualify it: passions can be changed only indirectly, and the will cannot stop a violent passion until "the commotion of the blood and spirits is appeased".

### descartes-passions.07 · Part III, arts. 152–154 — generosity: nothing "truly pertains to him but this free disposition of his will"

- **Claims:** C5.8 (complicates), C4.13 (for)
- **Location:** Part III, arts. 152–154, Haldane & Ross I pp. 401–402 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 20070–20076, 20085–20090, 20107–20122
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> I only remark in us one thing which might give us good reason to esteem ourselves, to wit, the use of our free will, and the empire which we possess over our wishes. Because it is for those actions alone which depend on this free will that we may with reason be praised or blamed ; and this in a certain measure renders us like God in making us masters of ourselves [...]

> Thus I think that true generosity which causes a man to esteem himself as highly as he legitimately can, consists alone partly in the fact that he knows that there is nothing that truly pertains to him but this free disposition of his will, and that there is no reason why he should be praised or blamed unless it is because he uses it well or ill [...]

> [...] every other man can also have them in his own case, because there is nothing in this that depends on another. That is why they never despise anyone [...] And, as they do not think of themselves as being much inferior to those who have more goods or honours, or even who have more mental gifts, more knowledge, more beauty [...] they do not at the same time esteem themselves much above those whom they surpass [...]

Descartes's fullest statement of what the self *is* for ethical purposes: only the free will "truly pertains" to a person, and praise and blame attach only to it (a forensic idea before Locke, compare C4.10). But it cuts against singularity: the will is the same capacity in "all other men", and it discounts rank, goods and honours, which fits C4.13 (worth acknowledged against role and status) better than C5.8.

### descartes-passions.08 · Part III, art. 212 — the good and evil of this life depend on the passions

- **Claims:** C4.7 (complicates), C4.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part III, art. 212 (the closing article), Haldane & Ross I p. 427 (check scan); `texts/descartes-works-haldane-ross-vol1.txt` lines 21413–21422
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> For the rest, the soul may have pleasures of its own, but as to those which are common to it and the body, they depend entirely on the passions, so that the men whom they can most move are capable of partaking most of enjoyment in this life. It is true that such men may also find most bitterness when they do not know how to employ them well, or fortune is contrary to them. But the principal use of prudence or self-control is that it teaches us to be masters of our passions, and to so control and guide them that the evils which they cause are quite bearable, and that we even derive joy from them all.

Descartes's last published words value the embodied life: those most moved by the body-caused passions can enjoy this life most. A received picture of Descartes as the philosopher of the bodiless Mind has to leave this out; good evidence that C4.7 is about selection in reception.
