# Voltaire, *Philosophical Dictionary* (1764)

- **Edition used:** English selection, New York: Carlton House (no translator named); Project Gutenberg eBook #18569
- **Text file:** `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` (clean)
- **Citation form:** entry title (and section, where the entry has them). This is an abridged selection: there is **no entry "Fanaticism"** (fanaticism is treated under Atheism, Religion and Tolerance), and "Self-love" appears as **"Self-Esteem."** "Liberty" here is about free will, not religious liberty.
- **Claims to test:** C4.30

## Passages

### voltaire-dict.01 · "Theist" — "submissive to God, there is his doctrine"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for: no church; against: Self still bounded by God)
- **Location:** entry "Theist"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 9570–9599
- **Checked:** text file

> The theist is a man firmly persuaded of the existence of a Supreme Being as good as He is powerful, who has formed all beings with extension, vegetating, sentient and reflecting; who perpetuates their species, who punishes crimes without cruelty, and rewards virtuous actions with kindness. [...] Reconciled in this principle with the rest of the universe, he does not embrace any of the sects, all of which contradict each other; his religion is the most ancient and the most widespread [...] He has brothers from Pekin to Cayenne, and he counts all wise men as his brethren. He believes that religion does not consist either in the opinions of an unintelligible metaphysic, or in vain display, but in worship and justice. The doing of good, there is his service; being submissive to God, there is his doctrine.

The theist leaves every sect, but his God still "punishes crimes" and "rewards virtuous actions," and his doctrine is submission. He is not alone either: his religion is "the most widespread," with "brothers from Pekin to Cayenne." Separation from church, not from God or from a (universal) community.

### voltaire-dict.02 · "The Ecclesiastical Ministry" — "free and voluntary" obedience

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** entry "The Ecclesiastical Ministry"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 3086–3150
- **Checked:** text file

> The institution of religion exists only to keep mankind in order, and to make men merit the goodness of God by their virtue. [...] All other means are repugnant to the liberty of the reason, to the nature of the soul, to the inalterable rights of the conscience, to the essence of religion and of the ecclesiastical ministry, to all the rights of the sovereign. [...] The sovereign even has no right to use coercion to lead men to religion, which supposes essentially choice and liberty. My thought is subordinate to authority no more than is sickness or health. [...] Our soul acts internally; internal acts are thought, volition, inclinations, acquiescence in certain truths. All these acts are above all coercion, and are within the ecclesiastical minister's sphere only in so far as he must instruct and never command. [...] Obedience to ecclesiastical order must consequently always be free and voluntary: no other should be possible.

The closest Voltaire comes to C4.30 as worded: "the inalterable rights of the conscience," religion "supposes essentially choice and liberty," church obedience "free and voluntary." But the first sentence makes religion an instrument of order and of meriting "the goodness of God."

### voltaire-dict.03 · "The Ecclesiastical Ministry" — the clergy under the sovereign

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "The Ecclesiastical Ministry"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 3117–3118, 3161–3163
- **Checked:** text file

> The priests are persons established under the authority of the sovereign to direct these prayers and all religious worship. [...] It follows from this further that the sovereign, careful not to suffer any partition of his authority, must permit no enterprise which puts the members of society in external and civil dependence on an ecclesiastical body.

Like Spinoza (spinoza-ttp.06), Voltaire puts the clergy "under the authority of the sovereign." What is freed is the person from the *clergy*; the church is not a self-governing voluntary society as in Locke.

### voltaire-dict.04 · "Atheism" — a "rewarding and revengeful God"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** entry "Atheism," Section I; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 893–896
- **Checked:** text file

> The interesting subject for the whole universe, is to know if it be not better, for the good of all mankind, to admit a rewarding and revengeful God, who recompenses good actions hidden, and who punishes secret crimes, than to admit none at all.

Voltaire's question is not whether the self needs a judge but whether society does, and he answers yes (voltaire-dict.06). The judging God is kept because it reaches "secret crimes," the inside of the person.

### voltaire-dict.05 · "Atheism" — the populace and "a society of philosophers above the nation"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Atheism," Section I; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 926–937
- **Checked:** text file

> The question is then asked whether a nation of atheists can exist; it seems to me that one must distinguish between the nation properly so called, and a society of philosophers above the nation. It is very true that in every country the populace has need of the greatest curb, and that if Bayle had had only five or six hundred peasants to govern, he would not have failed to announce to them the existence of a God, rewarder and revenger.

Separation from religion is allowed, at most, to a few philosophers; the many need "the greatest curb." Compare Spinoza's Preface (spinoza-ttp.02) and Voltaire's own Preface ("the people do not read," line 70). The separated Self is a class privilege, not a general condition.

### voltaire-dict.06 · "Atheism" — "absolutely necessary for princes and for peoples"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against)
- **Location:** entry "Atheism," Section II; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 1198–1202, 1236–1240
- **Checked:** text file

> The sanctity of oaths is clearly necessary, and one should have more confidence in those who believe that a false oath will be punished, than in those who think they can make a false oath with impunity. It is indubitable that in a civilized town, it is infinitely more useful to have a religion, even a bad one, than to have none at all. [...] It is therefore absolutely necessary for princes and for peoples, that the idea of a Supreme Being, creator, ruler, rewarder, revenger, shall be deeply engraved in people's minds.

Same ground as Locke on atheists (locke-toleration.11): oaths need a punishing God. Voltaire would tolerate the atheist (see .07) but still wants God "deeply engraved in people's minds."

### voltaire-dict.07 · "Atheism" — fanaticism "a thousand times more deadly"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Atheism," Section II; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 1204–1215
- **Checked:** text file

> It looks, therefore, that Bayle should have examined rather which is the more dangerous, fanaticism or atheism. Fanaticism is certainly a thousand times more deadly; for atheism inspires no bloody passion, whereas fanaticism does: atheism is not opposed to crime, but fanaticism causes crimes to be committed.

The real enemy is fanaticism, i.e. organized religious zeal, not belief. This is where Voltaire goes beyond Locke: the atheist is less dangerous than the zealot. Still, "atheism is not opposed to crime."

### voltaire-dict.08 · "Religion" — "moral philosophy must be uniform"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Religion"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 8120–8139
- **Checked:** text file

> I admired still more the intelligence which directs these vast forces. I said to myself: "One must be blind not to be dazzled by this spectacle; one must be stupid not to recognize the author of it; one must be mad not to worship Him. What tribute of worship should I render Him? Should not this tribute be the same in the whole of space, since it is the same supreme power which reigns equally in all space? [...] Light is uniform for the star Sirius and for us; moral philosophy must be uniform. [...] Everywhere the heart has the same duties [...]"

The religion that replaces the churches is natural and universal, not personal: one God, one morality, "the same duties" everywhere. The individual reaches it by his own contemplation, but what he reaches is common.

### voltaire-dict.09 · "Religion" — "Love God and your fellow-creature as yourself"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for and complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Religion"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 8364–8397
- **Checked:** text file

> Only did I conjure him to tell me in what true religion consisted. [...] "_Have I not already told you? Love God and your fellow-creature as yourself._" [...] "Is it necessary for me to take sides either for the Greek Church or the Latin?" "_When I was in the world I never made any difference between the Jew and the Samaritan._" "Well, if that is so, I take you for my only master." Then he made me a sign with his head which filled me with consolation. The vision disappeared, and a clear conscience stayed with me.

The dialogue ends with no church and "a clear conscience," but the conscience is set by a master and by a double duty, to God and to one's fellow-creature. Religion is privatized and moralized, and stays relational.

### voltaire-dict.10 · "Tolerance" — "the first law of nature"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Tolerance"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 9607–9610, 9685–9690
- **Checked:** text file

> What is tolerance? it is the consequence of humanity. We are all formed of frailty and error; let us pardon reciprocally each other's folly--that is the first law of nature. [...] But it is clearer still that we ought to be tolerant of one another, because we are all weak, inconsistent, liable to fickleness and error. Shall a reed laid low in the mud by the wind say to a fellow reed fallen in the opposite direction: "Crawl as I crawl, wretch, or I shall petition that you be torn up by the roots and burned?"

Toleration rests on shared weakness, not on the dignity of individual conscience. The ground is a common human condition ("we are all formed of frailty and error"), the opposite of a Self that is its own authority.

### voltaire-dict.11 · "Soul" — "an unknown principle"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Soul," Section I; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 8602–8603, 8632–8634
- **Checked:** text file

> This is a vague, indeterminate term, which expresses an unknown principle of known effects that we feel in us. [...] for not having any first notion from which we can descend in this examination, we can only rest for ever in a labyrinth of doubt and feeble conjecture.

If the Self separates from religion here, it does so by losing its theological substance: the soul is "an unknown principle." Separation weakens the Self's metaphysical ground rather than enlarging it.

### voltaire-dict.12 · "Self-Esteem" — "natural to all men"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** entry "Self-Esteem"; `texts/voltaire-philosophical-dictionary.txt` lines 8561–8562, 8589–8592
- **Checked:** text file

> Self-esteem is not wickedness, it is a sentiment that is natural to all men; it is much nearer vanity than crime. [...] Self-esteem is the instrument of our conservation; it resembles the instrument of the perpetuity of the species: it is necessary, it is dear to us, it gives us pleasure, and it has to be hidden.

Against Nicole's Augustinian suspicion of self-love, Voltaire makes self-esteem natural and harmless: a secular revaluation of the self. But it is "natural to all men," common, and "has to be hidden": not a value to express.

## Summary for C4.30

Voltaire separates religion from the churches and the clergy (voltaire-dict.02, .03, .09) and declares the "inalterable rights of the conscience." But the God he keeps is the judge, "rewarder and revenger," which he wants "deeply engraved in people's minds" (.04, .06); the freedom from religion is for "a society of philosophers above the nation" (.05); and his toleration rests on common frailty, not on individual authority (.10). Institution separated; Self still bounded, by God for the many and by a universal morality for all.
