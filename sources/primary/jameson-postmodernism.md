## Summary of the reading (2026-10-28)

### The collision: fragmentation vs. totalization

**What Jameson says, in his own terms.** From the end of the 1950s or early 1960s (jameson.04), the dominant cultural experience of the advanced capitalist world, above all America, is not a strengthened self but a *dissolving* one. "The alienation of the subject is displaced by the latter’s fragmentation" (jameson.11). He takes up "the “death” of the subject itself—the end of the autonomous bourgeois monad or ego or individual" and inclines to the historicist version: a centered subject once existed and "has today in the world of organizational bureaucracy dissolved" (jameson.12). With "the end of the bourgeois ego, or monad" comes "the end [...] of style, in the sense of the unique and the personal" and "a liberation from every other kind of feeling as well, since there is no longer a self present to do the feeling"; what is left are "intensities" that are "free-floating and impersonal" and "a peculiar kind of euphoria" (jameson.14). The subject "has lost its capacity [...] to organize its past and future into coherent experience" (jameson.25), and cannot "cognitively [...] map its position" in the world system (jameson.27). The "existential model of authenticity and inauthenticity" is one of the depth models now repudiated, "a casualty of the poststructural or postmodern period" (jameson.10). Culturally, the advanced countries are "a field of stylistic and discursive heterogeneity without a norm"; the ruling powers "no longer need to impose their speech (or are henceforth unable to)" (jameson.16). The only thing in his account that is totalizing is capital: "the yet more global and totalizing space of the new world system" (Ch. 1 p. 50, line 400).

**The strongest against-reading, stated plainly.** For the very period in which the thesis puts the Self's calcification (after 1930, and especially Rogers 1961 to Byrne 2006), a Tier 1 critic finds the opposite: no centered self left to calcify. If Jameson is right, (1) **C1.2 fails as worded**: what dominates American culture from the 1960s is fragmentation and a culture "without a norm", not a totalizing dogma of the Self; the dominant is capital's logic, not the Self's. (2) **C5.14 stage 3 is misread**: the carriers' sovereign self ("You are God in a physical body") is not the Self finally answerable to nothing, but at most a residual or compensatory form, one of the "residual" impulses that "must make their way" in the postmodern force field (jameson.05), or the "compensatory decorative exhilaration" Jameson finds in Warhol (Ch. 1 p. 10, line 118). Their euphoria would be the symptom of a self that is gone, not proof of one that rules. (3) **The thesis's cause is the wrong kind of cause**: Jameson explicitly sets his method against "psychologizing and moralizing culture critiques of the type of Christopher Lasch’s influential The Culture of Narcissism", because "there are, one would think, far more damaging things to be said about our social system than are available through the use of psychological categories" (jameson.24). A thesis about a cultural Self that totalizes is, on his terms, exactly such a critique. (4) **Its moral stance is unavailable**: "the indignant moral denunciation of the other, becomes unavailable" because the critic is "so deeply immersed in postmodernist space" (jameson.28), and a theory of an ever more total system paralyzes its own critical force ("winner loses", jameson.08). The thesis's title word, *totalitarian*, is open to that charge. (5) **What the thesis says the Self suppresses (history, the collective, social facts) Jameson finds suppressed by commodification and the simulacrum**, working *through* the fragmentation of the subject, not through its inflation (jameson.19, .22, .30).

**Readings on which both might hold (none of them is Jameson's own conclusion).**

1. *Ideology vs. experience.* Jameson keeps the two apart himself: he speaks of "that older notion (or experience) of the so-called centered subject" (jameson.13), calls the cult of unique style an "ideology of style" (jameson.17), and says even the poststructuralist view "must in any case take into account something like a “reality of the appearance.”" (jameson.12). So the *vocabulary* of the Self could intensify while the *experience* of a centered self dissolves. But Jameson does not say this. The nearest he comes is listing "new therapies" among the discourses that have "coalesced" into postmodernism theory (Intro p. ix, line 11), which makes therapy a symptom of the postmodern, not a counter-trend. Testing it needs Tier 2 evidence (word counts, surveys) after 1960, not Jameson.
2. *Covering over.* The totalizing Self-vocabulary as what covers the fragmented subject: a mask on a face that is not there. This would fit Jameson's own model of culture as both "mimesis of reality" and attempts "to distract and divert us from that reality or to disguise its contradictions" (Ch. 1 p. 49, line 398). It is our extension; he names no self-help or therapeutic carrier as a disguise of this kind.
3. *Answerable to nothing because unable to locate anything.* Jameson's subject cannot map its position in the totality (jameson.27, .30). A self that can find nothing to answer to might speak as if answerable to nothing. Again our construction; it turns stage 3 from sovereignty into disorientation, which changes the thesis rather than confirms it.
4. *Different evidence, different objects.* Jameson's evidence is art, architecture, film, the novel and theory; the thesis's stage-2 and stage-3 evidence is therapy, self-help and law. Each may be right about its domain. This cuts both ways: Jameson has not read the carriers, but the thesis has not shown that its carriers, rather than his, are the dominant.

**Verdict for the collision.** C1.2: **against** (Tier 1 dissent on the period after 1960). C5.14 stage 3: **against** as a claim about the culture; **complicates** as a claim about the carriers (it leaves open that they exist, as residual or compensatory forms). The thesis must answer Jameson, not absorb him.

### By claim

**C1.1 (the Self as a realization, not an invention) and C10.2 (philosophy's dissolution of the self had no cultural effect). C1.1: complicates. C10.2: against.** Jameson's exact wording (jameson.12): of two formulations, "the historicist one, that a once-existing centered subject, in the period of classical capitalism and the nuclear family, has today in the world of organizational bureaucracy dissolved; and the more radical poststructuralist position, for which such a subject never existed in the first place but constituted something like an ideological mirage—I obviously incline toward the former". So the autonomous subject was **real**, not mere ideology, which favours C1.1 against a pure invention reading. But it was real as the product of a social formation (classical capitalism, the nuclear family) and passes with it, which is not what "realization" of a truth means in C1.1. The wording in the brief (the bourgeois individual subject as a thing of the past, versus never having existed) is **not in these pages**; it is from *Postmodernism and Consumer Society* (1982), from memory, and must be checked there. On C10.2: Jameson treats poststructuralist theory, with its "death" of the subject, as "itself very precisely a postmodernist phenomenon" (Ch. 1 p. 12, line 142), a symptom of the culture. Theory does not cause the cultural dissolution, but on his account the cultural self *did* dissolve alongside the philosophical one. That contradicts C10.2's premise that the cultural Self came through untouched.

**C8.1 (genuine expression at the origin, then emptied). For, from a different cause.** Jameson's sequence is §8's: the modernists' "inimitable" private styles (jameson.15), oppositional and scandalous, are canonized by "the late 1950s" (jameson.06); their offensive features become "institutionalized" and "at one with the official or public culture" (jameson.06); style becomes "codes" (jameson.16); then pastiche, "blank parody", imitation "amputated of the satiric impulse" (jameson.17); and oppositional forms are "secretly disarmed and reabsorbed" (jameson.29). That is a genuine expression at the origin, then emptied. But the emptying has two causes in Jameson, neither the Self: commodification (jameson.07) and the end of the subject able to express (jameson.13, .15).

**C8.2 (conformity felt as the self). Complicates, leaning against.** For Jameson expression itself requires the monad (jameson.09, .13); with the monad gone, "the increasing unavailability of the personal style" (jameson.15) leaves no self to feel conformity as. The star system shows it: the older stars' "off-screen personalities [...] often connoted rebellion and nonconformism"; the new ones work "in the utter absence of “personality” in the older sense" (jameson.21). Against C8.2, that is the opposite of a vocabulary of self-expression spending a reserve: the reserve is gone, and so is the expressive word. For C8.2 (and C8.3, False Groups), at most: "the badges of affirmation of ethnic, gender, race, religious, and class-factional adhesion" (jameson.16), belonging worn as a code. He does not say they are felt as the self.

**C8.4 (the motive of belonging is old). Complicates.** The motive Jameson names is not belonging but "addiction", "a whole historically original consumers’ appetite for a world transformed into sheer images of itself" (jameson.18). The engine is outside the person: capital's colonization of "Nature and the Unconscious" through "the media and the advertising industry" (jameson.26). That backs the C5.14 pressure point that advertising may *produce* the self rather than the self producing itself.

**C8.7 (every age dresses conformity in its highest value word). Complicates for the period after 1960.** Pastiche is mimicry "devoid of laughter and of any conviction that alongside the abnormal tongue you have momentarily borrowed, some healthy linguistic normality still exists" (jameson.17), and there is "heterogeneity without a norm" (jameson.16). If he is right, the late period has no single highest value word to dress conformity in. The thesis would say the word is *self*; Jameson's account gives it no such place.

**C4.5 (economics is background, not cause) and C10.1 (this theory supplies the cause Lasch lacks). C4.5: complicates. C10.1: against.** Jameson's cause is late capitalism: postmodernism is "only the reflex and the concomitant of yet another systemic modification of capitalism itself" (jameson.02), and "the base, in the third stage of capitalism, generates its superstructures with a new kind of dynamic" (jameson.03). He uses Weber exactly as the thesis does, but runs the arrow the other way for the late period: as religious values "produced “new people”" for the modern labor process, so the postmodern is "the production of postmodern people capable of functioning in a very peculiar socioeconomic world" (jameson.03). Two qualifications: the interaction is "not [p. xv]a one-way street but a continuous reciprocal interaction and feedback loop" (jameson.03), and culture has expanded until "the very structure of the psyche itself" is "cultural" (jameson.29), so for him the line between economic and cultural cause has itself collapsed. C4.5 is about the origin (1500–1700), which Jameson does not discuss; it stands there, but its extension to the late Self faces a Tier 1 rival cause. C10.1: Jameson sets himself against Lasch's "psychologizing and moralizing" method (jameson.24), so the claim that the thesis supplies "the cause" must now answer a critic who says the cause is capital and that psychological categories cannot reach it.

**C5.7 and C1.3 (the Self suppresses history and the collective). For on the effect; against on the agent.** Jameson is strong FOR the loss of history: "an age that has forgotten how to think historically" (jameson.01); "a society bereft of all historicity" (jameson.19); "the waning of our historicity, of our lived possibility of experiencing history in some active way" (jameson.22); "any organic relationship between the American history we learn from schoolbooks and the lived experience" is gone (jameson.22); image culture "effectively abolishes any practical sense of the future and of the collective project" (jameson.28); First World subjects feel that "social classes of the classical type no longer exist" (jameson.30). But the agent is the simulacrum and commodification, and the mechanism runs through the *fragmented* subject that cannot unify past and future (jameson.25). The repression is real; who represses is in dispute.

**C6.13 (industrial structure dissolved the checks; no one chose). For, as continuation, in a later period.** Cognitive mapping is a structural account of the same kind as Dewey's: "the incapacity of our minds [...] to map the great global multinational and decentered communicational network in which we find ourselves caught as individual subjects" (jameson.27), and a "mutation in the object unaccompanied as yet by any equivalent mutation in the subject" (jameson.27). No one chose it; space and system outran the subject. It fits C1.3's social facts dropping out of view, but again without the Self as cause.

**C1.2, "above all in America". For on America; what is American is not the Self.** "This whole global, yet American, postmodern culture is the internal and superstructural expression of a whole new wave of American military and economic domination" (jameson.07); the brief "American century" (1945–73) was "the hothouse, or forcing ground, of the new system", and postmodernism "the first specifically North American global style" (jameson.04); the "life-style" of the superstate (jameson.01). Jameson's America is the home of fragmentation and commodity culture.

**Dating.** Jameson: the break is "generally traced back to the end of the 1950s or the early 1960s" (jameson.04); economic preparation in the 1950s, the "psychic habitus" achieved "in the 1960s"; both levels "crystallized in the great shock of the crises of 1973" (Intro pp. xx–xxi, line 51); canonization of modernism "can be traced to the late 1950s" (jameson.06). The thesis puts stage 2 at Rogers (1961) and *Casey* (1992), stage 3 at 2006–2020. The windows overlap: Rogers's book of 1961 and Jameson's break are the same moment, read in opposite directions. Rogers's "real self" behind façades is exactly the "depth model" (inside/outside, authenticity) that Jameson says is "a casualty" of the period (jameson.09, .10).

**C9.1 (opposites share the Self's form). Complicates.** Jameson's oppositional forms, "even overtly political interventions like those of The Clash", are "reabsorbed by a system of which they themselves might well be considered a part" (jameson.29): opposites inside one system. But the shared form is capital's, not the Self's.

**The §4 pattern (structure kept, limit dropped). Complicates.** Jameson has limits falling away: "nature is gone for good" (jameson.01); "critical distance" is "abolished" (jameson.29); the "precapitalist enclaves (Nature and the Unconscious)" are colonized (jameson.29). But in his story the *self* goes with the limits. The structure is not kept; it fragments.

**Against or limiting evidence (strong).** jameson.11, .12, .14 (the subject fragments, no self to feel); .16 (no norm; masters no longer impose speech); .10 (authenticity a casualty); .24 (against psychologizing culture critique, naming Lasch); .02, .03, .07, .26 (capital as cause); .08, .28 (total-system theories and moral denunciation disarm themselves); .05 (the dominant leaves room for residual and emergent forms, which cuts against any one-logic thesis, his own included).

- **Location:** Intro, pp. ix–x; `excerpts/jameson-postmodernism.txt` line 9
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> It is safest to grasp the concept of the postmodern as an attempt to think the present historically in an age that has forgotten how to think historically in the first place. [...] Postmodernism is what you have when the modernization process is complete and nature is gone for good. It is a more fully human world than the older one, but one in which “culture” has become a veritable “second nature.” [...] So, in postmodern culture, “culture” has become a product in its own right; the market has become a substitute for itself and fully as much a commodity as any of the items it includes within itself: modernism was still minimally and tendentially the critique of the commodity and the effort to make it transcend itself. Postmodernism is the consumption of sheer commodification as a process. The “life-style” of the superstate therefore stands in relationship to Marx’s “fetishism” of commodities as the most advanced monotheisms to primitive animisms or the most rudimentary idol worship

The opening sentence of the book is C5.7's diagnosis. The cause is in the same paragraph: commodification, not the Self. The religious figure (monotheism vs. idol worship) is Jameson's, applied to commodity culture.

### jameson.02 · Intro, p. xii — postmodernism "only the reflex and the concomitant of yet another systemic modification of capitalism itself"

- **Claims:** C10.1 (against: a rival cause), C4.5 (complicates, for the late period)
- **Location:** Intro, p. xii; line 19
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Despite the delirium of some of its celebrants and apologists (whose euphoria, however, is an interesting historical symptom in its own right), a truly new culture could only emerge through the collective struggle to create a new social system. The constitutive impurity of all postmodernism theory, then (like capital itself, it must be at internal distance from itself, must include the foreign body of alien content), confirms the insight of a periodization that must be insisted on over and over again, namely, that postmodernism is not the cultural dominant of a wholly new social order [...] but only the reflex and the concomitant of yet another systemic modification of capitalism itself.

The plainest statement of his cause. Culture follows the system; change in culture needs change in the social system. On this view a cultural mutation of the Self cannot be its own cause.

### jameson.03 · Intro, pp. xiv–xv, xxi — Weber's "new people" and "postmodern people"; the base "generates its superstructures"

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates: the Weber analogy run from the economy), C10.1 (against), C5.14 (complicates: who produces the late self)
- **Location:** Intro, pp. xiv–xv, line 27; p. xxi, line 57
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> in the same way the interrelationship of culture and the economic here is not [p. xv]a one-way street but a continuous reciprocal interaction and feedback loop. But just as (for Weber) new inner-directed and more ascetic religious values gradually produced “new people” capable of thriving in the delayed gratification of the emergent “modern” labor process, so also the “postmodern” is to be seen as the production of postmodern people capable of functioning in a very peculiar socioeconomic world indeed

The thesis leans on Weber for the origin (C4.5). Jameson accepts Weber for the origin and says the late period works the other way: people made to fit the system. He also hedges ("feedback loop"). "inner-directed" is Riesman's term, unattributed here.

### jameson.04 · Intro, p. xx; Ch. 1, p. 1 — dating: the 1950s, the 1960s, and "the first specifically North American global style"

- **Location:** Intro, p. xx, line 49; Ch. 1, p. 1, line 72
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Culturally, however, the precondition is to be found (apart from a wide variety of aberrant modernist “experiments” which are then restructured in the form of predecessors) in the enormous social and psychological transformations of the 1960s, which swept so much of tradition away on the level of mentalités. Thus the economic preparation of postmodernism or late capitalism began in the 1950s [...] On the other hand, the psychic habitus of the new age demands the absolute break, strengthened by a generational rupture, achieved more properly in the 1960s [...] non-North American readers will inevitably deplore the Americanocentrism of my own particular account, which is justified only to the degree that it was the brief “American century” (1945–73) that constituted the hothouse, or forcing ground, of the new system, while the development of the cultural forms of postmodernism may be said to be the first specifically North American global style.

America is the host, as in C1.2, but of postmodernism. His break (late 1950s to 1973) is the thesis's stage 2 window (Rogers, 1961). The two accounts claim the same years for opposite movements of the self.

### jameson.05 · Ch. 1, pp. 4, 6 — "a cultural dominant"; "residual" and "emergent" forms

- **Claims:** C1.2 (complicates: a dominant is not a totality), C5.14 stage 3 (complicates: room for residual forms)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, p. 4, line 84; p. 6, line 96
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> This is, however, precisely why it seems to me essential to grasp postmodernism not as a style but rather as a cultural dominant: a conception which allows for the presence and coexistence of a range of very different, yet subordinate, features.

> I am very far from feeling that all cultural production today is “postmodern” in the broad sense I will be conferring on this term. The postmodern is, however, the force field in which very different kinds of cultural impulses—what Raymond Williams has usefully termed “residual” and “emergent” forms of cultural production—must make their way.

Jameson's own logic is a *dominant*, not a totality. That is a model the thesis lacks and may need: the Self as dominant, residual or emergent. On his map the self-sufficient Self would be residual.

### jameson.06 · Ch. 1, p. 4 — the revolt "institutionalized" and "at one with the official or public culture"

- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> Not only are Picasso and Joyce no longer ugly; they now strike us, on the whole, as rather “realistic,” and this is the result of a canonization and academic institutionalization of the modern movement generally that can be traced to the late 1950s.

### jameson.07 · Ch. 1, pp. 4–5 — "integrated into commodity production"; "this whole global, yet American, postmodern culture"

- **Claims:** C10.1 (against: rival cause), C4.5 (complicates), C1.2 (for on America)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, pp. 4–5; line 90
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> What has happened is that aesthetic production today has become integrated into commodity production generally: the frantic economic urgency of producing fresh waves of ever more novel-seeming goods (from clothing to airplanes), at ever greater rates of turnover, now assigns [p. 5]an increasingly essential structural function and position to aesthetic innovation and experimentation. [...] Yet this is the point at which I must remind the reader of the obvious; namely, that this whole global, yet American, postmodern culture is the internal and superstructural expression of a whole new wave of American military and economic domination throughout the world: in this sense, as throughout class history, the underside of culture is blood, torture, death, and terror.

Novelty is an economic necessity, which gives C8.1's emptying an engine outside the self. America's culture is the "superstructural expression" of American power: the "above all in America" of C1.2, with a material cause.

### jameson.08 · Ch. 1, pp. 5–6 — "winner loses": the total system that paralyzes its own critique

- **Claims:** C1.2 (complicates: a warning to any totalizing thesis), C1.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. 1, pp. 5–6; line 94
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> What happens is that the more powerful the vision of some increasingly total system or logic—the Foucault of the prisons book is the obvious example—the more powerless the reader comes to feel. Insofar as the theorist wins, therefore, by constructing an increasingly closed and terrifying machine, to that very degree he loses, since the critical capacity of his work is thereby paralyzed, and the impulses of negation and revolt, not to speak of those [p. 6]of social transformation, are increasingly perceived as vain and trivial in the face of the model itself.

A direct risk for a thesis titled *totalitarian*: the more totalizing the Self is made, the less room is left for anything that could answer it. Jameson's answer is the dominant (jameson.05).

### jameson.09 · Ch. 1, pp. 11–12 — "the very concept of expression presupposes [...] some separation within the subject"

- **Location:** Ch. 1, pp. 11–12; line 140
- **Checked:** excerpt (EPUB)

> It will here be read as an embodiment not merely of the expression of that kind of affect but, even more, as a virtual deconstruction of the very aesthetic of expression itself, which seems to have dominated much of what we call high modernism but to have vanished away—for both practical and theoretical reasons—in the world of the postmodern.
