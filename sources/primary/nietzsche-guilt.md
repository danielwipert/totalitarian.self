# Nietzsche on guilt, responsibility and the subject

- **Edition used:** *Genealogy of Morals* (Kennedy); *Twilight of the Idols* (Ludovici); one passage from *Beyond Good and Evil* (Zimmern)
- **Text file:** `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt`, `texts/nietzsche-twilight-antichrist.txt`, `texts/nietzsche-beyond-good-and-evil.txt` (all clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): KSA; English: Cambridge Texts editions
- **Citation form:** work + essay/section number, plus file and line. Section numbers below are the real ones in these files (GM II has 25 sections; *Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors," has 8). They match the standard numbering.
- **Translation note:** Kennedy renders *Schuld* as both "guilt" and "owe/ought" (translator's note, GM II, line 2845: "The German world '_schuld_' means both debt and guilt"). The debt/guilt pun is the backbone of GM II.
- **Claims to test:** C4.12, C7.1, C5.3, C4.10, C5.9 (also touches C5.13)

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

1. **Nietzsche never connects any of this to Locke.** "Locke" appears once in these texts, in BGE §20 (`texts/nietzsche-beyond-good-and-evil.txt` line 722), on "the origin of ideas," not on personal identity or the forensic person. He traces the free, responsible subject to **priests and theologians** (Twilight, Four Great Errors §7), to **the resentment of the weak** (GM I.13) and to **Greek philosophers** who invented free will for the gods' entertainment (GM II.7).
2. **C4.12 half-holds.** Twilight §7 says plainly that free will was invented "with the intention of tracing guilt" (.16). But GM II says guilt (*Schuld*) is *older* than the responsible subject: it begins as debt, and punishment ran for most of history "absolutely independently" of free will (.04, .05). So, for Nietzsche, the forensic framework is not what *makes* guilt. It is a late priestly addition that makes guilt *attributable to an inner agent* and therefore inexhaustible.
3. **C7.1 is supported in structure but with two problems.** (a) The genealogy of internalized, inexpiable guilt is there (GM II.16, II.21–22, III.15, III.20), and the "limit" exists in Nietzsche's terms: God the creditor who pays the debt himself (II.21), grace (II.10), the Greek gods who "took upon themselves [...] the guilt" (II.23, not quoted). Nietzsche also says the Christian conscience survives God's death (GM III.27; Twilight, Skirmishes §5). (b) But Nietzsche's own forecast in II.20 is the *opposite* of the claim: atheism should bring "a kind of second innocence." He corrects it at once in II.21, but the correction is about moralization *inside* Christianity, not about a post-Christian Self. "Survives God's death" is our extrapolation from II.21 + III.27, not something he states about guilt.
4. **The sovereign individual (GM II.2) is not the answerable Self in Locke's sense.** Its responsibility looks forward (promising), not backward (imputing past acts). It is rare and aristocratic, and its conscience is a *good* conscience, a privilege, not guilt. It is "loose from the morality of custom" and finds in itself "his _standard of value_," which fits C5.9 (cut from frame). But Nietzsche praises it.
5. **C5.3 ally confirmed:** the subject as a fiction of grammar (GM I.13; Twilight, Four Great Errors §3; "Reason" in Philosophy §5).

## Passages

### nietzsche-guilt.01 · GM I.13 — the doer behind the deed

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** First Essay §13; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1106–1108, 1122–1123, 1141–1148
- **Checked:** text file

> But there is no such _substratum_, there is no "being" behind doing, working, becoming; "the doer" is a mere appanage to the action. The action is everything. [...] Thereby do they win for themselves the right of attributing to the birds of prey the _responsibility_ for being birds of prey [...] This kind of man finds the belief in a neutral, free-choosing "subject" _necessary_ from an instinct of self-preservation, of self-assertion, in which every lie is fain to sanctify itself. The subject (or, to use popular language, the _soul_) has perhaps proved itself the best dogma in the world simply because it rendered possible to the horde of mortal, weak, and oppressed individuals of every kind, that most sublime specimen of self-deception, the interpretation of weakness as freedom, of being this, or being that, as _merit_.

The free subject behind the deed is a fiction, and it exists *in order to* assign responsibility and merit. That backs C5.3. For C4.12: the subject makes responsibility attributable, but its authors are the resentful weak, not a philosophy of the person.

### nietzsche-guilt.02 · GM II.1 — the animal that can promise; "memory of the will"

- **Claims:** C4.10 (complicates), C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §1; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1482–1484, 1493–1496
- **Checked:** text file

> an _active_ refusal to get rid of it, a continuing and a wish to continue what has once been willed, an actual _memory of the will_ [...] above all, to reckon, to have power to calculate--how thoroughly must man have first become _calculable, disciplined, necessitated_ even for himself and his own conception of himself, that, like a man entering into a promise, he could guarantee himself _as a future_.

A useful contrast with Locke. Both ground responsibility in memory, but Locke's consciousness extends *backward* to "own and impute" past actions (locke-essay.01). Nietzsche's memory is a will that binds the *future*, and it had to be bred into a forgetful animal. It is not a given of consciousness.

### nietzsche-guilt.03 · GM II.2 — the sovereign individual

- **Claims:** C4.10 (complicates), C5.9 (for), C5.13 (for)
- **Location:** Second Essay §2; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1501, 1515–1523, 1532–1534, 1545–1550
- **Checked:** text file

> This is simply the long history of the origin of _responsibility_. [...] then do we find as the ripest fruit on its tree the _sovereign individual_, that resembles only himself, that has got loose from the morality of custom, the autonomous "super-moral" individual (for "autonomous" and "moral" are mutually-exclusive terms),--in short, the man of the personal, long, and independent will, _competent to promise_ [...] The "free" man, the owner of a long unbreakable will, finds in this possession his _standard of value_ [...] The proud knowledge of the extraordinary privilege of _responsibility_, the consciousness of this rare freedom, of this power over himself and over fate, has sunk right down to his innermost depths, and has become an instinct, a dominating instinct--what name will he give to it [...]? But there is no doubt about it--the sovereign man calls it his _conscience_.

This is answerability carried past custom ("resembles only himself," his own "standard of value"), which fits C5.9. It is not the guilty forensic Self: responsibility here is a *privilege* of the strong few, and the conscience is a proud one. God is not mentioned. The frame he drops is custom, not God. Don't read it as "the answerable Self after God's death."

### nietzsche-guilt.04 · GM II.4 — punishment older than free will; "responsible" a late distinction

- **Claims:** C4.12 (complicates), C4.10 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §4; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1643–1651, 1659–1662
- **Checked:** text file

> the cardinal moral idea of "ought"[2] originates from the very material idea of "owe"? Or that punishment developed as a retaliation absolutely independently of any preliminary hypothesis of the freedom or determination of the will?--And this to such an extent, that a high degree of civilisation was always first necessary for the animal man to begin to make those much more primitive distinctions of "intentional," "negligent," "accidental," "responsible," and their contraries, and apply them in the assessing of punishment. [...] Throughout the longest period of human history punishment was _never_ based on the responsibility of the evil-doer for his action, and was consequently not based on the hypothesis that only the guilty should be punished [...]

The hardest passage for C4.12 as written. The forensic distinctions ("intentional," "responsible") are late. Guilt-as-debt and punishment existed long before them. The forensic framework *refines* how guilt is attributed; it does not originate guilt.

### nietzsche-guilt.05 · GM II.6 — the cradle of guilt is the law of contract

- **Claims:** C4.12 (complicates), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §6; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1731–1740
- **Checked:** text file

> It is then in _this_ sphere of the law of contract that we find the cradle of the whole moral world of the ideas of "guilt," "conscience," "duty," the "sacredness of duty,"--their commencement, like the commencement of all great things in the world, is thoroughly and continuously saturated with blood. [...] It was in this sphere likewise that there first became formed that sinister and perhaps now indissoluble association of the ideas of "guilt" and "suffering."

Nietzsche's root of guilt is creditor and debtor, an economic relation, not a theory of the person. That is worth noting for C4.5 (economic origin) too, but it puts the root of guilt well before any Self.

### nietzsche-guilt.06 · GM II.7 — free will invented for the gods' spectacle

- **Claims:** C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §7; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1877–1881
- **Checked:** text file

> Must not that philosophic invention, so audacious and so fatal, which was then absolutely new to Europe, the invention of "free will," of the absolute spontaneity of man in good and evil, simply have been made for the specific purpose of justifying the idea, that the interest of the gods in humanity and human virtue was _inexhaustible_?

Here the inventors of free will are the *Greek* "moral philosophers," and the purpose is theatrical (the gods as spectators), not judicial. A second, different genealogy of free will from Twilight §7 (.16). Neither one points to Locke.

### nietzsche-guilt.07 · GM II.10 — grace as the self-destruction of justice

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** Second Essay §10; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 1996–2005
- **Checked:** text file

> It is possible to conceive of a society blessed with so great a _consciousness of its own power_ as to indulge in the most aristocratic luxury of letting its wrong-doers go _scot-free_. [...] The justice which began with the maxim, "Everything can be paid off, everything must be paid off," ends with connivance at the escape of those who cannot pay to escape--it ends, like every good thing on earth, by _destroying itself_.--The self-destruction of Justice! we know the pretty name it calls itself--_Grace!_ it remains, as is obvious, the privilege of the strongest, better still, their super-law.

In Nietzsche the debt system has a built-in limit: a creditor strong enough to forgive. That matches the thesis pattern ("the limit" = a power that can cancel the debt). For him, though, the limit is a mark of strength, not a gift of God.

### nietzsche-guilt.08 · GM II.16 — bad conscience as instinct turned inward

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C5.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §16; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 2385–2388, 2407–2410, 2418–2421
- **Checked:** text file

> I regard the bad conscience as the serious illness which man was bound to contract under the stress of the most radical change which he has ever experienced--that change, when he found himself finally imprisoned within the pale of society and of peace. [...] All instincts which do not find a vent without, _turn inwards_--this is what I mean by the growing "internalisation" of man: consequently we have the first growth in man, of what subsequently was called his soul. [...] Enmity, cruelty, the delight in persecution, in surprises, change, destruction--the turning all these instincts against their own possessors: this is the origin of the "bad conscience."

This is the genealogy of guilt turned inward, and it is *not* religious at root. It is the social enclosure of the State (§17), which fits §7.1's "unrelated to original sin." But it is prehistoric and universal, not a product of the surfeit Self. The inner world itself (the "soul") is an effect of it. C7.1 must say the surfeit Self *inherits and intensifies* this, not that it creates it.

### nietzsche-guilt.09 · GM II.20 — guilt grows with God; atheism promises "second innocence"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (against)
- **Location:** Second Essay §20; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 2624–2634
- **Checked:** text file

> The appearance of the Christian god, as the record god up to this time, has for that very reason brought equally into the world the record amount of guilt consciousness. Granted that we have gradually started on the _reverse_ movement, there is no little probability in the deduction, based on the continuous decay in the belief in the Christian god, to the effect that there also already exists a considerable decay in the human consciousness of owing (ought); in fact, we cannot shut our eyes to the prospect of the complete and eventual triumph of atheism freeing mankind from all this feeling of obligation to their origin, their _causa prima_. Atheism and a kind of second innocence complement and supplement each other.

Read alone, this goes against C7.1: as long as guilt is debt to a god, it should fade as belief fades. Nietzsche frames it as a provisional "deduction" and overturns it in the next section (.10), so the tag stays "against" only for the debt line taken alone.

### nietzsche-guilt.10 · GM II.21 — moralization: guilt pushed into the bad conscience; inexpiability; God pays

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** Second Essay §21; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 2644–2650, 2656–2663, 2673–2679
- **Checked:** text file

> at the end of the last paragraph used language to the effect that this moralisation did not exist, and that consequently these ideas had necessarily come to an end, by reason of what had happened to their hypothesis, the credence in our "creditor," in God. The actual facts differ terribly from this theory. It is with the moralisation of the ideas "ought" and "duty," and with their being pushed back into the _bad_ conscience [...] it is at this juncture that the ideas "guilt" and "duty" have to turn backwards--turn backwards against _whom_? There is no doubt about it; primarily against the "ower," in whom the bad conscience now establishes itself, eats, extends, and grows like a polypus throughout its length and breadth, all with such virulence, that at last, with the impossibility of paying the debt, there becomes conceived the idea of the impossibility of paying the penalty, the thought of its inexpiability (the idea of "eternal punishment") [...] till suddenly we stand before that paradoxical and awful expedient, through which a tortured humanity has found a temporary alleviation, that stroke of genius called Christianity:--God personally immolating himself for the debt of man, God paying himself personally out of a pound of his own flesh, God as the one being who can deliver man from what man had become unable to deliver himself--the creditor playing scapegoat for his debtor, from _love_ [...]

The key passage for C7.1. (1) Once guilt is moralized into the conscience, it does *not* end when belief in the creditor-God ends. (2) Moralized guilt becomes unpayable, i.e. pervasive. (3) God is "the one being who can deliver man": the limit, in the thesis's sense. The Self that keeps the moralized guilt and loses that God fits his scheme exactly. But Nietzsche does not draw that conclusion himself; he calls Christianity only a "temporary alleviation."

### nietzsche-guilt.11 · GM II.22 — the will to find oneself guilty

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** Second Essay §22; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 2705–2713
- **Checked:** text file

> man's _will_ to find himself guilty and blameworthy to the point of inexpiability, his _will_ to think of himself as punished, without the punishment ever being able to balance the guilt, his _will_ to infect and to poison the fundamental basis of the universe with the problem of punishment and guilt, in order to cut off once and for all any escape out of this labyrinth of "fixed ideas," his will for rearing an ideal--that of the "holy God"--face to face with which he can have tangible proof of his own un-worthiness.

Guilt that no punishment can balance is the "pervasive guilt" of §7.1. Note the direction: for Nietzsche the will to guilt comes first and *uses* God ("exploited the religious hypothesis," line 2690–2691). God is the instrument, not the source. This both helps C7.1 (the will can outlive its instrument) and weakens "God as limit" (here God intensifies guilt; only in II.21 does he relieve it).

### nietzsche-guilt.12 · GM III.15 — "it is the fault of thyself alone"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** Third Essay §15; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 3932–3934, 3948–3954
- **Checked:** text file

> "It must be somebody's fault that I feel bad"--this kind of reasoning is peculiar to all invalids, and is but the more pronounced, the more ignorant they remain of the real cause of their feeling bad, the physiological cause [...] "I suffer: it must be somebody's fault"--so thinks every sick sheep. But his herdsman, the ascetic priest, says to him, "Quite so, my sheep, it must be the fault of some one; but thou thyself art that some one, it is all the fault of thyself alone--_it is the fault of thyself alone against thyself_": that is bold enough, false enough, but one thing is at least attained; thereby, as I have said, the course of resentment is--_diverted_.

Very close to SPEC §7.1: failure "attributed to the Self or to other Selves," never to non-personal causes. Here it is both: blame others (resentment), then blame the self (the priest's redirection), with the real (physiological) cause ignored. The agent is the priest, not a theory of the person.

### nietzsche-guilt.13 · GM III.20 — "the sick man has been turned into 'the sinner'"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** Third Essay §20; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 4363–4369, 4378–4385
- **Checked:** text file

> the exploitation of the feeling of "_guilt_." I have already indicated in the previous essay the origin of this feeling--as a piece of animal psychology and nothing else: we were thus confronted with the feeling of "guilt," in its crude state, as it were. It was first in the hands of the priest, real artist that he was in the feeling of guilt, that it took shape [...] he must search for it in _himself_, in his guiltiness, in a piece of the past, he must understand his very suffering as a _state of punishment_. [...] The sick man has been turned into "the sinner"--and now for a few thousand years we never get away from the sight of this new invalid, of "a sinner"--shall we ever get away from it?

Two layers: raw animal guilt (II.16), then priestly "sin," which reads all suffering as one's own fault. "Shall we ever get away from it?" leaves open whether the form outlasts the priest. This is suggestive for C7.1, not proof.

### nietzsche-guilt.14 · GM III.27 — Christian conscience survives God as "the scientific conscience"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** Third Essay §27; `texts/nietzsche-genealogy-of-morals.txt` lines 4962–4967, 4978–4983
- **Checked:** text file

> Everywhere otherwise, where the mind is at work seriously, powerfully, and without counterfeiting, it dispenses altogether now with an ideal (the popular expression for this abstinence is "Atheism")--_with the exception of the will for truth_. But this will, this _remnant_ of an ideal, is, if you will believe me, that ideal itself in its severest and cleverest formulation [...] _What_, I put the question with all strictness, has really _triumphed_ over the Christian God? The answer stands in my _Joyful Wisdom_, Aph. 357: "the Christian morality itself, the idea of truth, taken as it was with increasing seriousness, the confessor-subtlety of the Christian conscience translated and sublimated into the scientific conscience [...]"

The clearest statement of the thesis pattern in Nietzsche: the structure (the confessor's conscience) is kept and the God is dropped, and the structure is what killed the God. But what survives here is *truthfulness*, not guilt. Applying it to guilt is an extension, though a natural one.

### nietzsche-guilt.15 · *Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors" §3 — the ego as fiction

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for)
- **Location:** "The Four Great Errors" §3; `texts/nietzsche-twilight-antichrist.txt` lines 1421–1422, 1437–1440, 1448–1452
- **Checked:** text file

> Otherwise we should not be free to perform them, we should not have been responsible for them. [...] And as for the ego! It has become legendary, fictional, a play upon words: it has ceased utterly and completely from thinking, feeling, and willing! What is the result of it all? There are no such things as spiritual causes. [...] Man projected his three “inner facts of consciousness,” the will, the spirit, and the ego in which he believed most firmly, outside himself. He first deduced the concept Being out of the concept Ego, he supposed “things” to exist as he did himself, according to his notion of the ego as cause.

A strong ally for C5.3: the ego-as-cause is a fiction, and the world of "things" is projected *from* it. That is the Self taking itself for the origin of what it sees. See also "Reason" in Philosophy §5 (lines 1022–1024, 1046–1047): "the doer and his deed are seen in all circumstances [...] I fear we shall never be rid of God, so long as we still believe in grammar."

### nietzsche-guilt.16 · *Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors" §7 — the error of free will

- **Claims:** C4.12 (for, with correction), C7.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** "The Four Great Errors" §7; `texts/nietzsche-twilight-antichrist.txt` lines 1573–1595, 1600–1602
- **Checked:** text file

> _The Error of Free-Will._ At present we no longer have any mercy upon the concept “free-will”: we know only too well what it is—the most egregious theological trick that has ever existed for the purpose of making mankind “responsible” in a theological manner,—that is to say, to make mankind dependent upon theologians. [...] Wherever men try to trace responsibility home to anyone, it is the instinct of punishment and of the desire to judge which is active. Becoming is robbed of its innocence when any particular condition of things is traced to a will, to intentions and to responsible actions. The doctrine of the will was invented principally for the purpose of punishment,—that is to say, with the intention of tracing guilt. The whole of ancient psychology, or the psychology of the will, is the outcome of the fact that its originators, who were the priests at the head of ancient communities, wanted to create for themselves a right to administer punishments—or the right for God to do so. Men were thought of as “free” in order that they might be judged and punished—in order that they might be held guilty: consequently every action had to be regarded as voluntary, and the origin of every action had to be imagined as lying in consciousness [...] the theologians, who with their notion of “a moral order of things,” still continue to pollute the innocence of Becoming with punishment and guilt Christianity is the metaphysics of the hangman.

The one passage that says what C4.12 needs: free will plus "the origin of every action [...] in consciousness" exist *so that* guilt can be attributed. Note "in consciousness": that is close to Locke's ground of the person, though Locke is not named. The inventors are "priests" and "theologians," and the setting is "ancient." Nietzsche puts this framework at the root of Christianity, not in modern philosophy of the person.

### nietzsche-guilt.17 · *Twilight*, "The Four Great Errors" §8 — "no one shall any longer be made responsible"

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C5.3 (for)
- **Location:** "The Four Great Errors" §8; `texts/nietzsche-twilight-antichrist.txt` lines 1607–1613, 1623–1630
- **Checked:** text file

> What then, alone, can our teaching be?—That no one gives man his qualities, neither God, society, his parents, his ancestors, nor himself [...] No one is responsible for the fact that he exists at all, that he is constituted as he is, and that he happens to be in certain circumstances and in a particular environment. [...] The fact that no one shall any longer be made responsible, that the nature of existence may not be traced to a _causa prima_, that the world is an entity neither as a sensorium nor as a spirit—_this alone is the great deliverance_,—thus alone is the innocence of Becoming restored.... The concept “God” has been the greatest objection to existence hitherto.... We deny God, we deny responsibility in God: thus alone do we save the world.—

Nietzsche's cure is to drop responsibility *with* God, the opposite of the surfeit Self, which keeps responsibility and drops God. Note "nor himself" and "circumstances [...] environment": he puts back exactly the frames that SPEC §7.1 says the Self deflects. For him, though, God is the cause of guilt, not its limit. That cuts against the thesis's reading of God as the limit.

### nietzsche-guilt.18 · *Twilight*, "Skirmishes in a War with the Age" §5 — G. Eliot: rid of God, holding to the morality

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** "Skirmishes in a War with the Age" §5; `texts/nietzsche-twilight-antichrist.txt` lines 2121–2129, 2131–2134
- **Checked:** text file

> _G. Eliot._—They are rid of the Christian God and therefore think it all the more incumbent upon them to hold tight to Christian morality: this is an English way of reasoning [...] In England, every man who indulges in any trifling emancipation from theology, must retrieve his honour in the most terrifying manner by becoming a moral fanatic. That is how they do penance in that country.—As for us, we act differently. When we renounce the Christian faith, we abandon all right to Christian morality. [...] Christianity is a system, a complete outlook upon the world, conceived as a whole. If its leading concept, the belief in God, is wrenched from it, the whole is destroyed; nothing vital remains in our grasp.

The pattern as cultural diagnosis: the English (Anglo-American) keep the morality and drop the God, and they become *more* moralistic ("moral fanatic," "penance"). That helps C7.1 and §6 (host). Nietzsche adds that the remainder is groundless: the structure survives in practice even though, in his view, it cannot stand in logic.

### nietzsche-guilt.19 · BGE §21 — causa sui: total responsibility, absolving God, world, society

- **Claims:** C7.1 (for), C5.9 (for), C4.12 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Beyond Good and Evil* §21; `texts/nietzsche-beyond-good-and-evil.txt` lines 728–735, 762–767
- **Checked:** text file

> The desire for "freedom of will" in the superlative, metaphysical sense, such as still holds sway, unfortunately, in the minds of the half-educated, the desire to bear the entire and ultimate responsibility for one's actions oneself, and to absolve God, the world, ancestors, chance, and society therefrom, involves nothing less than to be precisely this CAUSA SUI, and, with more than Munchausen daring, to pull oneself up into existence by the hair, out of the slough of nothingness. [...] some will not give up their "responsibility," their belief in THEMSELVES, the personal right to THEIR merits, at any price (the vain races belong to this class); others on the contrary, do not wish to be answerable for anything, or blamed for anything, and owing to an inward self-contempt, seek to GET OUT OF THE BUSINESS, no matter how.

Perhaps the best single text for §7.1: total self-responsibility "absolves" God, the world, chance and society, so the Self becomes its own cause (C5.9, "its own ground"). It also ties responsibility to vanity ("belief in THEMSELVES," "THEIR merits"), which links guilt and narcissism (§7.3). It names a modern ("half-educated") attitude, not a priestly one, so here Nietzsche *does* describe the post-theological form.
