# Walter Lippmann, *A Preface to Morals* (1929)

- **Edition used:** original English, New York: The Macmillan Company, 1929 (file line 21)
- **Text file:** `texts/lippmann-preface-to-morals.txt` (Gutenberg #77019) (clean; original page numbers kept in the file as [pNNN])
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): 1929 Macmillan first edition
- **Citation form:** part, chapter and section, original page, plus file and line
- **Claims to test:** C6.13, C4.30, C5.9, C5.14, C7.*

## Reading summary

**Read:** Part I in full (chs. I–VII, the "Acids of Modernity" and "Breakdown of Authority" chapters among them), the Part II introduction and ch. IX.2, IX.7–9, X.3–4, and in Part III ch. XI.2–3, XII.2, XII.6–7, XIV.1, XIV.5 and XV in full. Not read: II.1–4, III, IV.4, V.1–2, VI.2–3e, VII.2–7, VIII, IX.1, IX.3–6, X.1–2, XI.1, XI.4, XII.1, XII.3–5, XIII, XIV.2–4, XIV.6–7.

**The dissolution.** Lippmann's subject is exactly the one C6.13 and C5.14 ask about: men who have lost "the religion of their fathers" and feel "a vacancy in their lives" (lines 224–227). The phrase "The acids of modernity" is his (lines 374–375, 687, 1650; title of ch. IV). He dates the process long: "In the last four hundred years" (line 1658), "at first imperceptibly with the rise of the towns, and then catastrophically since the mechanical revolution" (lines 668–669). What is new in 1929 is not the rebellion but "their disillusionment with their own rebellion", in the generation since "the debacle of idealism at the end of the War" (lines 616–619). So he does not date the break to the 1890s.

**Is the self answerable to nothing?** Half yes. "There is no moral authority to which he must turn now" (lines 402–403), and the conscience finds "no fixed point outside of it" (lines 1747–1748). But Lippmann does not describe a self that has become its own origin. He describes a self that has *dissolved*: the soul is "republican", each impulse has its "Bill of Rights", and there is no longer "any personality to own them" (lines 3219–3232). And it is still bound, though by forces it does not respect: "coercion in opinions, fashions and fads", "the opinions of his neighbors" (lines 403, 428). Its freedom is felt as emptiness: "no freedom in mere freedom" (lines 8894–8895). Where a self does claim authority for its own wishes, Lippmann calls it childish (lines 5247–5256).

**The substitutes.** He names cults, fads and "the best advertised notion" (line 386), patriotism (ch. V.5), Babbitt's "religion of progress" (lines 649–650), the "random collections of successful men" who rule America (line 1935), and popular psychology and psychoanalysis (lines 3231, 8354–8361). **The word "self-expression" does not occur in the book** (grep of the file). Nor do "mind-cure", "New Thought" or "Christian Science". Business is treated as a lost province of the church (ch. VI.1), and then, surprisingly, as a *school of disinterestedness*: the corporate executive becomes "a relatively disinterested person" (line 7021). That cuts against the part of C6.13 that has the Self's vocabulary pass to business.

**The new bound.** The "religion of the spirit" (ch. XV.3) and "high religion" (chs. IX.9, X.4) both rest on "disinterestedness" (verified: lines 5634, 6034–6041, 8806–8811). The bound is located *in* the personality ("an ideal of the human personality", line 8902) but is not self-given: "they do not have to invent such an ideal" (lines 8902–8903). It is given by the sages, confirmed by psychology and science, and answers to "reality itself" (line 6041) and to "necessity" (line 7064). It is the opposite of a self answerable to nothing. It is also, Lippmann admits, beyond most people (lines 5616–5617).

**C4.30.** Lippmann confirms the claim's picture of the earlier reformers: Luther's "Protestantism for good Catholics", Jefferson's "democracy for men who had an aristocratic training" (lines 563–566). And he says that by 1929 the separation has reached the Self, not just institutions: the modern man "is unable any longer to think of himself as a single personality approaching an everlasting judgment" (lines 3204–3205). He also gives a mechanism linking the two: institutional toleration itself wears down inner conviction (lines 2185–2200).

## Passages

### lippmann.01 · Part I, ch. I.1, pp. 3–4 — "a vacancy in their lives"

- **Claims:** C7.9 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "Whirl is King", pp. 3–4; `texts/lippmann-preface-to-morals.txt` lines 224–227, 237–250
- **Checked:** text file

> Among those who no longer believe in the religion of their fathers, some are proudly defiant, and many are indifferent. But there are also a few, perhaps an increasing number, who feel that there is a vacancy in their lives.

> When such men put their feelings into words they are likely to say that, having lost their faith, they have lost the certainty that their lives are significant, and that it matters what they do with their lives. [...] They are likely to point to the world about them, and to ask whether the modern man possesses any criterion by which he can measure the value of his own desires, whether there is any standard he really believes in which permits him to put a term upon that pursuit of money, of power, and of excitement which has created so much of the turmoil and the squalor and the explosiveness of modern civilization.

Lippmann's starting point: with faith gone, the self has its desires and no measure for them. That is the C5.14 situation, but felt as lack, not as sovereignty. Note his limit: "a few, perhaps an increasing number", not the whole culture.

### lippmann.02 · Part I, ch. I.2, pp. 6–7 — "The prison door is wide open"

- **Claims:** C7.9 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "False Prophecies", pp. 6–7; lines 334–347
- **Checked:** text file

> These are the prisoners who have been released. They ought to be very happy. They ought to be serene and composed. They are free to make their own lives. There are no conventions, no tabus, no gods, priests, princes, fathers, or revelations which they must accept. Yet the result is not so good as they thought it would be. The prison door is wide open. They stagger out into trackless space under a blinding sun. They find it nerve-wracking. [...] They must find their own courage for battle and their own consolation in defeat.

The fullest description in our sources so far of a self bound by nothing outside, and it is a description of distress. Being "free to make their own lives" is a burden, not a creed.

### lippmann.03 · Part I, ch. I.2, pp. 8–9 — "the acids of modernity"; "the best advertised notion"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C5.14 (against)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "False Prophecies", pp. 8–9; lines 370–396
- **Checked:** text file

> These are the gifts of a vital religion which can bring the whole of a man into adjustment with the whole of his relevant experience. Our forefathers had such a religion. They quarrelled a good deal about the details, but they had no doubt that there was an order in the universe which justified their lives because they were a part of it. The acids of modernity have dissolved that order for many of us [...]. He does not believe the words of the Gospel but he believes the best advertised notion. [...] The modern man has ceased to believe in it but he has not ceased to be credulous, and the need to believe haunts him. It is no wonder that his impulse is to turn back from his freedom, and to find someone who says he knows the truth and can tell him what to do, to find the shrine of some new god, of any cult however newfangled [...].

Verifies the phrase "acids of modernity". Advertising appears as a substitute *belief*, which supports C6.13. Against C5.14 step 2: the emancipated self does not become its own origin; its impulse is to "turn back from his freedom" to a new authority.

### lippmann.04 · Part I, ch. I.2, pp. 9–10 — "coercion in opinions, fashions and fads"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C6.13 (for), C7.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "False Prophecies", pp. 9–10; lines 398–430
- **Checked:** text file

> For the modern man who has ceased to believe, without ceasing to be credulous, hangs, as it were, between heaven and earth, and is at rest nowhere. There is no theory of the meaning and value of events which he is compelled to accept, but he is none the less compelled to accept the events. There is no moral authority to which he must turn now, but there is coercion in opinions, fashions and fads.

> But when he believes that events are determined by the votes of a majority, the orders of his bosses, the opinions of his neighbors, the laws of supply and demand, and the decisions of quite selfish men, he yields because he has to yield. He is conquered but unconvinced.

The key distinction for step 2: no *moral* authority, but plenty of *coercion*. The self is not answerable to nothing; it is answerable to forces it does not respect. For C7.9, the bond here is to opinion and fashion, and it is felt as compulsion, not as belonging.

### lippmann.05 · Part I, ch. I.4, pp. 14–15 — "Protestantism for good Catholics"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "Deep Dissolution", pp. 14–15; lines 553–571
- **Checked:** text file

> When Luther, for example, rebelled against the authority [p015] of the Church, he did not suppose the way of life for the ordinary man would be radically altered. [...] The individual for whom he claimed the right of private judgment was one whose prejudgments had been well fixed in a Catholic society. The authority of the Pope was to be destroyed and certain evils abolished, but there was to remain that feeling for objective moral certainties which Catholicism had nurtured. [...] For what he believed in was Protestantism for good Catholics. The reformers of the Eighteenth Century made a similar assumption. They really believed in democracy for men who had an aristocratic training.

An independent statement of C4.30's picture, from 1929: the Reformation and the Enlightenment removed authorities but assumed a self already formed by the old order. The institutions changed; the formed Self was taken for granted.

### lippmann.06 · Part I, ch. I.4, pp. 17, 19 — when the dissolution happened

- **Claims:** C6.13 (complicates), C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. I, "Deep Dissolution", pp. 17, 19; lines 616–622, 668–678
- **Checked:** text file

> What most distinguishes the generation who have approached maturity since the debacle of idealism at the end of the War is not their rebellion against the religion and the moral code of their parents, but their disillusionment with their own rebellion.

> For in the modern age, at first imperceptibly with the rise of the towns, and then catastrophically since the mechanical revolution, there have gone into dissolution not only the current orthodoxy, but the social order and the ways of living which supported it. [...] The earlier rebels summoned men from one allegiance to another, but the feeling for certainty in religion and for decorum in society persisted. In the modern world it is this very feeling of certainty itself which is dissolving. It is dissolving not merely for an educated minority but for everyone who comes within the orbit of modernity.

Dating: a long process (towns, then the machine) with a felt break after 1918, not in the 1890s. The C6.13 window can hold the visible symptom, but not the cause. For C4.30: what dissolves now is the inner "feeling of certainty itself", not an allegiance.

### lippmann.07 · Part I, ch. IV.1–2, pp. 56, 59 — conscience with "no fixed point outside of it"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates), C5.14 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. IV, "The Kingly Pattern" and "Landmarks", pp. 56, 59; lines 1648–1656, 1745–1752
- **Checked:** text file

> It is these supporting conceptions—the unconscious assumption that we are related to God as creatures to creator, as vassals to a king, as children to a father—that the acids of modernity have eaten away. The modern man’s daily experience of modernity makes instinctively incredible to him these unconscious ideas which are at the core of the great traditional and popular religions. He does not wantonly reject belief, as so many churchmen assert. His predicament is much more serious. With the best will in the world, he finds himself not quite believing.

> The modern man is an emigrant who lives in a revolutionary society and inherits a protestant tradition. He must be guided by his conscience. But when he searches his conscience, he finds no fixed point outside of it by which he can take his bearings. He does not really believe that there is such a point, because he himself has moved about too fast to fix any point long enough in his mind. For the sense of authority is not established by argument. It is acquired by deep familiarity and indurated association.

The single best passage for C4.30 and C5.14 together. Locke's and Paine's conscience survives (the "protestant tradition"), but the God it answered to is gone, so it answers to itself. This is step 2's structure, but it comes from experience (migration, change), not from argument, and it is felt as loss of bearings, not as self-authorship.

### lippmann.08 · Part I, ch. IV.3, IV.5, pp. 61–63, 65–66 — America: no landmarks, no rulers

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.1 (for), C6.7 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. IV, "Barren Ground" and "Rulers", pp. 61–63, 65–66; lines 1791–1793, 1807–1810, 1836–1837, 1909–1921, 1935–1947
- **Checked:** text file

> The American people, more than any other people, is composed of individuals who have lost association with their old landmarks. [...] For faith grows well only as it is passed on from parents to their children amidst surroundings that bear witness, because nothing changes radically, to a deep permanence in the order of the world. [...] The city is an acid that dissolves [p063] this piety.

> The American social system is migratory, revolutionary, and protestant. It provides no recognized leaders and no clear standards of conduct. [...] There are smart sets, first families, and successful people, to whom a good deal of deference is paid and a certain tribute of imitation. But these leaders have no real authority in morals or in matters of taste because they themselves have few standards that are not the fashions of a season.

> Our rulers to-day consist of random collections of successful men and their wives. [...] They have been educated to achieve success; few of them have been educated to exercise power.

Supports C6.1 and C6.13: America is where the inherited checks are weakest, and the city dissolves what is left. The new leaders are "successful people" whose only standards are "the fashions of a season", which fits C6.7. Lippmann names migration and the city as causes, not consent.

### lippmann.09 · Part I, ch. V.3, V.6, pp. 76, 82 — toleration wears down conviction

- **Claims:** C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. V, "The Logic of Toleration" and "The Dissolution of a Sovereignty", pp. 76, 82; lines 2185–2200, 2352–2364
- **Checked:** text file

> The human soul is not so divided in compartments that a man can be indifferent in one part of his soul and firmly believing in another. The existence of rival sects, the visible demonstration that none has a monopoly, the habit of neutrality, cannot but dispose men against an unquestioning acceptance of the authority of one sect. [...] None has that power to discipline the individual which a universal church exercises. For, as Dr. Figgis puts it, when many churches are tolerated, “excommunication has ceased to be tyrannical by becoming futile.”

> Thus there has gradually been dissolving the conception that the government of human affairs is a subordinate part of a divine government presided over by God the King. In place of one church which is sovereign over all men, there are now many rival churches, rival states, voluntary associations, and detached individuals. [...] Religion has become for most modern men one phase in a varied experience; it no longer regulates their civic duties, their economic activities, their family life, and their opinions.

C4.30 separates institutional separation from the Self's separation. Lippmann says they are linked: Locke's toleration, kept up over time, wears down the inner conviction it was meant to protect. The institutional step is the slow cause of the later inner one.

### lippmann.10 · Part I, ch. VI.3(f), pp. 108–111 — "compelled to be creative because his world is chaotic"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. VI, "Art: (f) The Burden of Originality", pp. 108–111; lines 3073–3074, 3083–3089, 3144–3156
- **Checked:** text file

> The modern artist has to be original. That is to say, he has to seize experience, pick it over, and drag from it his theme.

> But if we refrain from using words loosely, and reserve the word creation to mean the finding of the original intuition and idea, then creation is plainly not a necessary part of the artist’s equipment. Creation is an obligation which the artist has had thrust upon him as a result of the dissolution of the great accepted themes. He is compelled to be creative because his world is chaotic.

> What was happening to painting is precisely what has happened to all the other separated activities of men. Each activity has its own ideal, indeed a succession of ideals, for with the dissolution of the supreme ideal of service to God, there is no ideal which unites them all, and sets them in order. [...] There is no point of reference outside which can determine the relative value of competing ideals. [...] They have become differentiated. They are free and they are incommensurable.

The nearest thing in Lippmann to the self as origin: each must invent his own meaning. But it is an "obligation [...] thrust upon him", a result of collapse, not a claim of power. This suggests step 2 began as a burden and only later became a creed.

### lippmann.11 · Part I, ch. VII.1, pp. 113–115 — "the constitutional form in popular psychology to-day is republican"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C5.14 (complicates), C7.9 (for)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. VII, "The Soul in the Modern World", pp. 113–115; lines 3204–3207, 3216–3219, 3224–3241, 3247–3248
- **Checked:** text file

> The modern man is unable any longer to think of himself as a single personality approaching an everlasting judgment. He is one man to-day and another to-morrow, one person here and another there. He does not feel he knows himself. He is sure that no one else knows him at all.

> The problems of his character have become dissociated from any feeling that they involve his immortal destiny. They have become dissociated from the feeling that they deeply matter. From the feeling that [p114] they are deeply his own. From the feeling that there is any personality to own them.

> The thoroughly modern man has really ceased to believe that there is an immortal essence presiding like a king over his appetites. [...] It is certainly not the fashion any longer to think of the soul as a little lord ruling the turbulent rabble of his carnal passions; the constitutional form in popular psychology to-day is republican. Each impulse may invoke the Bill of Rights, and have its way if the others will let it. [...] Morality thus becomes a traffic code designed to keep as many desires as possible moving together without too many violent collisions. [...] The objective moral certitudes have dissolved, and in the liberal philosophy there is nothing to take their place.

Here the *Self*, not the institution, separates from religion: it no longer stands before "an everlasting judgment". That is the answerability root of C1.1 cut. But the result is not a sovereign Self; it is a self with no "personality to own" its impulses. Step 2 as written ("its own origin") does not fit; a self with no owner and many impulses does. Lippmann names "popular psychology" as the vehicle.

### lippmann.12 · Part II, ch. IX.2, p. 158 — "this major premise of modernity"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II, ch. IX, "Freedom and Restraint", p. 158; lines 4355–4362
- **Checked:** text file

> The modern world, as it has emancipated itself from its ancestral regime, has assumed almost as a matter of course that the human passions, if thoroughly liberated from all tyrannies and distortions, would by their fulfilment achieve happiness. All those who teach asceticism, deny this major premise of modernity, and the result is that the prevailing philosophy is at odds on the most fundamental of all issues with the wisdom of the past.

Lippmann's name for the trust in the liberated self is "this major premise of modernity". It is the premise of later self-expression, but stated as liberated *passions*, not as an expressed Self; the word "self-expression" is absent from the book.

### lippmann.13 · Part II, ch. IX.8–9, pp. 190, 193 — the self that gives "authority to his appetites" is childish

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II, ch. IX, "The Passage into Maturity" and "The Function of High Religion", pp. 190, 193; lines 5247–5256, 5337–5344
- **Checked:** text file

> The child’s philosophy rests upon the assumption that the world outside is in gear with his own appetites. For this reason an adult with a childish character will ascribe an authority to his appetites which may easily land him in fanaticism or frustration, in a crazy indulgence or a miserable starvation. And to the environment he will ascribe a willingness to conform to him, a capacity to be owned by him, which land him in all sorts of delusions of grandeur. [...] The world is full of semi-adult persons who secretly nurse the notion that they are, or that by rights they ought to be, Don Juan, Crœsus, Napoleon, or the Messiah.

> I venture, at least, to suggest that the function of high religion is to reveal to men the quality of mature experience, that high religion is a prophecy and an anticipation of what life is like when desire is in perfect harmony with reality.

Lippmann diagnoses the self that is its own authority as infantile, a failure to grow up. The 1929 humanist answer to lost religion is not self-authorship but adjustment of desire "with reality".

### lippmann.14 · Part III, ch. XI.2, pp. 220–221 — "disinterestedness in the presence of reality itself"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (for), C5.14 (against)
- **Location:** Part III, ch. XI, "Superstition and Self-Consciousness", pp. 220–221; lines 6033–6041
- **Checked:** text file

> To become detached from one’s passions and to understand them consciously [p221] is to render them disinterested. A disinterested mind is harmonious with itself and with reality.

> This is the principle by which a humanistic culture becomes bearable. If the principle of a theocratic culture is dependence, obedience, conformity in the presence of a superhuman power which administers reality, the principle of humanism is detachment, understanding, and disinterestedness in the presence of reality itself.

Verifies "disinterestedness". The new bound replaces God with "reality itself": the self is still answerable, now to the facts. A thinker who drops God here keeps a bound outside the self, which fits C5.9 (philosophy does not make the Self its own origin) and cuts against any reading of 1929 thought as step 2.

### lippmann.15 · Part I, ch. VI.1, p. 88; Part III, ch. XII.6–7, pp. 256–258 — business: lost province, then school of disinterestedness

- **Claims:** C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part I, ch. VI, "Business", p. 88; Part III, ch. XII, "The Diffusion of the Acquisitive Instinct" and "Ideals", pp. 256–258; lines 2494–2500, 7017–7028, 7062–7066
- **Checked:** text file

> The emancipation of business from religious control is perhaps even more threatening to the authority of the churches than the rivalry of sects or the rise of the civil power. Business is a daily occupation; government meets the eye of the ordinary men only now and then. That the main interest in the waking life of most people should be carried on wholly separated from the faith they profess means that the churches have lost one of the great provinces of the human soul.

> But as compared with the old-style business man, the ordinary executive in a great corporation is something quite strange. He is [p257] so little the monarch of all he surveys, his experience is so continually with stubborn and irreducible facts, he is so much compelled to adjust his own preferences to the preferences of others, that he becomes a relatively disinterested person.

> The only kind of liberty which is workable in the real world is the liberty of the disinterested man, of the man who has transformed his passions by an understanding of necessity.

For C6.13's first half: business has left religion's control. Against its last clause: Lippmann does not see business taking over the Self's vocabulary. He sees the great corporation as a new *check* that makes the executive "relatively disinterested". Read as a 1929 hope, not as evidence of what happened, but it is the opposite of the claim's picture.

### lippmann.16 · Part III, ch. XIV.5, pp. 302, 306–307 — "man is a collection of separate impulses"

- **Claims:** C7.9 (for), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part III, ch. XIV, "The New Hedonism", pp. 302, 306–307; lines 8231–8251, 8354–8367
- **Checked:** text file

> But the newest hedonism has had an even better prospect than the classical: it finds men emancipated not only of all fear of divine authority and human custom but of physical and social consequences as well. [...] Do they report that they have found happiness in their freedom? Well, hardly. Instead of the gladness which they were promised, they seem, like Hegesias, to have found the wasteland.

> All the varieties of the modern doctrine that man is a collection of separate impulses, each of which can attain its private satisfaction, are in fundamental contradiction not only with the traditional body of human wisdom but with the modern conception of the human character. Thus in one breath it is said in advanced circles that love is a series of casual episodes, and in the next it transpires that the speaker is in process of having himself elaborately psychoanalyzed in order to disengage his soul from the effects of apparently trivial episodes in his childhood.

The "wasteland" of freedom fits C7.9's no-man's-land. The "advanced circles" Lippmann describes take pleasure as the aim and therapy as the tool: a dissolved self, not a self that originates.

### lippmann.17 · Part III, ch. XV.2–3, pp. 325–327 — "no freedom in mere freedom"; an ideal they "do not have to invent"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C5.14 (against), C7.9 (for)
- **Location:** Part III, ch. XV, "The Choice of a Way" and "The Religion of the Spirit", pp. 325–327; lines 8861–8867, 8889–8906
- **Checked:** text file

> They are compelled to choose consciously, clearly, and with full realization of what the choice implies, between religion as a system of cosmic government and religion as insight into a cleansed and matured personality: between God conceived as the master of that fate, creator, providence, and king, and God conceived as the highest good at which they might aim.

> There are, however, those who do not find a principle of order in the belief that they are related to a supernatural power. They cannot be argued into the ancient belief, for it has been dissolved by the circumstances of their lives. They are deeply perplexed. They have learned that the absence of belief is vacancy; they know, from disillusionment and anxiety, that there is no freedom in mere freedom. They must find, then, some other principle which will give coherence and direction to their lives.

> Since they are unable to find a principle of order in the authority of a will outside themselves, there is no place they can find it except in an ideal of the human personality. But they do not have to invent such an ideal out of hand. The ideal way of life for men who must make their own terms with experience and find [p327] their own happiness has been stated again and again. It is that only the regenerate, the disinterested, the mature, can make use of freedom.

Lippmann's answer moves the source of order *into* the personality, so the Self has separated from "a will outside themselves" (C4.30's line crossed). But the ideal is inherited, not self-made: "they do not have to invent such an ideal". The bound is self-held but not self-given. It is the exact opposite of step 2's "answerable to nothing".
