# Luther, *Concerning Christian Liberty* (1520)

Also known as *On the Freedom of a Christian*; Latin *De libertate christiana*, German *Von der Freiheit eines Christenmenschen*.

- **Edition used:** trans. Wace & Buchheim, *First Principles of the Reformation* (1883)
- **Text file:** `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` (clean); also in `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt`
- **Standard modern edition** (for final citations): *Luther's Works* (American Edition), vol. 31
- **Claims to test:** C4.1, C4.3, C4.13

## Passages

### luther-liberty.01 · Opening propositions: free lord and dutiful servant

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** opening of the treatise; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` line 399
- **Checked:** text file

> A Christian man is the most free lord of all, and subject to none; a Christian man is the most dutiful servant of all, and subject to every one.

Inner freedom is announced together with total service to others. Whatever individuation Luther achieves comes bound to a new kind of obligation, so C4.3 must say what is freed (the conscience before God) and what is not.

### luther-liberty.02 · The twofold nature: inward and outward man

- **Claims:** C4.1 (complicates), C4.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** start of the treatment of the inner man; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 413–423
- **Checked:** text file

> Man is composed of a twofold nature, a spiritual and a bodily. As regards the spiritual nature, which they name the soul, he is called the spiritual, inward, new man; as regards the bodily nature, which they name the flesh, he is called the fleshly, outward, old man. [...] in the same man these two men are opposed to one another; the flesh lusting against the spirit, and the spirit against the flesh (Gal. v. 17).

This is the "inner Self" the to-do list was looking for, but the inward man is the *new* man, made by faith (lines 425–427: "by what means a man becomes justified, free, and a true Christian; that is, a spiritual, new, and inward man"), not a pre-existing self uncovered (C4.1). For C4.8: Luther's inner/outer split comes from Paul (2 Cor. iv. 16), which is a source the claim leaves out alongside Plato and Augustine.

### luther-liberty.03 · No outward thing touches the soul: vestments, holy places, sacred offices

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** the inner man; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 427–430, 442–448
- **Checked:** text file

> It is certain that absolutely none among outward things, under whatever name they may be reckoned, has any influence in producing Christian righteousness or liberty, nor, on the other hand, unrighteousness or slavery. [...] And so it will profit nothing that the body should be adorned with sacred vestments, or dwell in holy places, or be occupied in sacred offices, or pray, fast, and abstain from certain meats, or do whatever works can be done through the body and in the body.

The clearest "for" in the treatise. Sacred office, sacred place and sacred dress (the marks of the clerical estate) are declared irrelevant to the soul's standing. The soul's relation to God is cut loose from the institution's role-markers.

### luther-liberty.04 · The soul needs only the word, and the word comes by preaching

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** the inner man; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 465–467, 479–482, 488–490
- **Checked:** text file

> Let us therefore hold it for certain and firmly established that the soul can do without everything except the word of God, without which none at all of its wants are provided for. [...] Christ was sent for no other office than that of the word; and the order of Apostles, that of bishops, and that of the whole body of the clergy, have been called and instituted for no object but the ministry of the word. [...] To preach Christ is to feed the soul, to justify it, to set it free, and to save it, if it believes the preaching.

The soul is freed from works but not left alone with God: it depends on an outside word, and that word comes through preaching by a ministry Luther keeps ("faith cometh by hearing," line 1168). The church structure is cut back to the ministry of the word, not removed.

### luther-liberty.05 · All are priests, but not all should teach publicly

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** after the priesthood of believers; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 869–875
- **Checked:** text file

> For Holy Scripture makes no distinction between them, except that those who are now boastfully called popes, bishops, and lords, it calls ministers, servants, and stewards, who are to serve the rest in the ministry of the word, for teaching the faith of Christ and the liberty of believers. For though it is true that we are all equally priests, yet we cannot, nor, if we could, ought we all to, minister and teach publicly.

The clergy lose their separate *estate* but keep an *office*. For C4.13, note that the argument is for equality ("all equally priests"), not difference.

### luther-liberty.06 · The freed inner man must subdue the self-seeking will of the flesh

- **Claims:** C4.13 (against), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** the outward man; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 962–972
- **Checked:** text file

> For the inner man, being conformed to God and created after the image of God through faith, rejoices and delights itself in Christ, in whom such blessings have been conferred on it, and hence has only this task before it: to serve God with joy and for nought in free love. But in doing this he comes into collision with that contrary will in his own flesh, which is striving to serve the world and to seek its own gratification. This the spirit of faith cannot and will not bear, but applies itself with cheerfulness and zeal to keep it down and restrain it

SPEC §2 defines the Self as acknowledging that an individual's "feelings, will, and desires" are distinct from the collective's. Luther's freed person does the opposite with his own will and desires: he keeps them down. What Luther frees is conscience before God, not individual desire. This is the strongest "against" for C4.13 in the treatise.

### luther-liberty.07 · Conclusion: the Christian does not live in himself

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** conclusion of the main argument; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 1436–1440
- **Checked:** text file

> We conclude therefore that a Christian man does not live in himself, but in Christ and in his neighbour, or else is no Christian: in Christ by faith; in his neighbour by love. By faith he is carried upwards above himself to God, and by love he sinks back below himself to his neighbour, still always-abiding in God and His love

Luther's own summary places the person outside himself, in two relations. See also lines 1178–1180 ("he lives only for others, and not for himself"). The individuated relationship with God does not produce a self-centred self. It fits SPEC §2's *relational* difference better than an autonomous Self.

### luther-liberty.08 · The freeman keeps his station and complies with pope, bishop and magistrate

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against), C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** works toward the neighbour; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 1366–1371, 1381–1386
- **Checked:** text file

> Such, too, ought to have been the works of all colleges, monasteries, and priests; every one doing the works of his own profession and state of life, not in order to be justified by them, but in order to bring his own body into subjection, as an example to others [...] For the Christian freeman will speak thus: I will fast, I will pray, I will do this or that which is commanded me by men, not as having any need of these things for justification or salvation, but that I may thus comply with the will of the Pope, of the bishop, of such a community or such a magistrate, or of my neighbour as an example to him

Liberty changes the *meaning* of one's station ("profession and state of life") and of obedience, not whether one fills them. That runs against C4.2's "rather than fill socioeconomic roles". Outward church commands are still obeyed, only now freely, so C4.3's "freeing from church structure" holds for the conscience, not for conduct.

### luther-liberty.09 · Nature itself, confirmed by lawgivers, cannot free itself

- **Claims:** C4.1 (against), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** closing pages; `texts/luther-christian-liberty.txt` lines 1627–1638
- **Checked:** text file

> But since human nature and natural reason, as they call it, are naturally superstitious, and quick to believe that justification can be attained by any laws or works proposed to them, and since nature is also exercised and confirmed in the same view by the practice of all earthly lawgivers, she can never of her own power free herself from this bondage to works, and come to a recognition of the liberty of faith. We have therefore need to pray that God will lead us and make us taught of God, that is, ready to learn from God; and will Himself, as He has promised, write His law in our hearts; otherwise there is no hope for us.

This decides against C4.1 as applied to Luther. The bondage starts in *nature* and is only "confirmed" by social orders (lawgivers). The liberty is not something already present in the person that the orders had repressed; it has to be given from outside ("taught of God"). See also lines 1137–1144 ("Nature cannot expel it by her own power [...] And when custom steps in besides"). Social orders strengthen the repression; they do not cause it.
