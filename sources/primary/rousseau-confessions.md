## Passages

### rousseau-confessions.01 · Book I, opening — uniqueness and the last trumpet

- **Claims:** C4.16 (for), C4.17 (complicates), C4.15 (for)
- **Location:** Book I; `texts/rousseau-confessions.txt` lines 318–343
- **Checked:** text file

> I have entered upon a performance which is without example, whose accomplishment will have no imitator. I mean to present my fellow-mortals with a man in all the integrity of nature; and this man shall be myself.
>
> I know my heart, and have studied mankind; I am not made like any one I have been acquainted with, perhaps like no one in existence; if not better, I at least claim originality, and whether Nature did wisely in breaking the mould with which she formed me, can only be determined after having read this work.
>
> Whenever the last trumpet shall sound, I will present myself before the sovereign judge with this book in my hand, and loudly proclaim, thus have I acted; these were my thoughts; such was I. [...] even as thou hast read my inmost soul: Power eternal! assemble round thy throne an innumerable throng of my fellow-mortals, let them listen to my confessions, let them blush at my depravity, let them tremble at my sufferings; let each in his turn expose with equal sincerity the failings, the wanderings of his heart, and, if he dare, aver, I was better than that man.

Difference is claimed as a value ("I at least claim originality" over "better") and as the warrant for the book. But the judge here is still God, who has already "read my inmost soul"; what moves is the *record* (a self-authored book) and the *jury* (a throng of fellow-mortals), not the source of judgement into feeling. The closing dare also assumes everyone's heart has comparable "failings": unique in make, common in fault.

### rousseau-confessions.02 · Book I — the feeling heart, felt before conceived

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C5.6 (complicates), C4.16 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book I; lines 437–440, 472–478
- **Checked:** text file

> Such were the authors of my being: of all the gifts it had pleased Heaven to bestow on them, a feeling heart was the only one that descended to me; this had been the source of their felicity, it was the foundation of all my misfortunes.

> An infinity of sensations were familiar to me, without possessing any precise idea of the objects to which they related--I had conceived nothing--I had felt the whole. This confused succession of emotions did not retard the future efforts of my reason, though they added an extravagant, romantic notion of human life, which experience and reflection have never been able to eradicate.

The defining trait is inherited (from parents, by Heaven's gift), not self-originating, and it is a burden ("foundation of all my misfortunes"). Feeling comes before ideas, but as a stage of childhood that distorts his view of life, a fault he admits, not a ground he celebrates.

### rousseau-confessions.03 · Book I — the broken comb: inner certainty against appearances

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C4.17 (for), C4.16 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book I; lines 862–865, 893–901
- **Checked:** text file

> Fifty years have expired since this adventure--the fear of punishment is no more. Well, then, I aver, in the face of Heaven, I was absolutely innocent: and, so far from breaking, or even touching the comb, never came near the fire. It will be asked, how did this mischief happen? I can form no conception of it, I only know my own innocence.

> The first instance of violence and oppression is so deeply engraved on my soul, that every relative idea renews my emotion: the sentiment of indignation, which in its origin had reference only to myself, has acquired such strength, and is at present so completely detached from personal motives, that my heart is as much inflamed at the sight or relation of any act of injustice (whatever may be the object, or wheresoever it may be perpetrated) as if I was the immediate sufferer.

"I only know my own innocence" puts inner certainty over the evidence: a first case of feeling trumping fact. But the feeling then widens into a general sense of injustice "detached from personal motives". Here the inner feeling joins him to others rather than setting him apart.

### rousseau-confessions.04 · Book II — Marion and the ribbon: guilt, motive, self-expiation

- **Claims:** C7.1 (complicates), C4.17 (for), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book II; lines 3164–3174, 3237–3249, 3279–3284
- **Checked:** text file

> I carried with me the long and painful remembrance of a crime; an insupportable weight of remorse which yet hangs on my conscience, and whose bitter recollection, far from weakening, during a period of forty years, seems to gather strength as I grow old.

> The weight, therefore, has remained heavy on my conscience to this day; and I can truly own the desire of relieving myself, in some measure, from it, contributed greatly to the resolution of writing my Confessions.
>
> I have proceeded truly in that I have just made, and it will certainly be thought I have not sought to palliate the turpitude of my offence; but I should not fulfill the purpose of this undertaking, did I not, at the same time, divulge my interior disposition, and excuse myself as far as is conformable with truth.

> If it is a crime that can be expiated, as I dare believe, forty years of uprightness and honor on various difficult occasions, with the many misfortunes that have overwhelmed my latter years, may have completed it.

The clearest case of guilt in the book. The guilt is real and specific (an injured person), not free-floating, which sets it apart from the "surfeit" guilt of C7.1. What is new is the procedure: he judges by "interior disposition" (he goes on to call friendship for Marion the cause of the lie), and he declares his own expiation. Confession to the public partly stands in for confession to a priest or a friend: writing the book is itself the relief.

### rousseau-confessions.05 · Book IV, end — the reader as judge

- **Claims:** C4.17 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book IV; lines 6232–6245
- **Checked:** text file

> I would be able by some means to render my soul transparent to the eyes of the reader, and for this purpose endeavor to show it in every possible point of view, to give him every insight, and act in such a manner, that not a motion should escape him, as by this means he may form a judgment of the principles that produce them.
>
> Did I take upon myself to decide, and say to the reader, "Such is my character," he might think that if I did not endeavor to deceive him, I at least deceived myself; but in recounting simply all that has happened to me, all my actions, thoughts, and feelings, I cannot lead him into an error, unless I do it wilfully, [...] since it is his province to compare the elements, and judge of the being they compose: thus the result must be his work, and if he is then deceived the error will be his own.

Judgement is not moved inward here. It is handed *outward*, to the reader. The self supplies the evidence and the public delivers the verdict. Self-expression in this book is addressed to an audience and needs that audience to complete it, from the start rather than only in a later corruption (relevant to C8).

### rousseau-confessions.06 · Book VI — conscience against the Jansenists; prayer without walls

- **Claims:** C4.17 (for), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book VI; lines 8748–8769, 8516–8520
- **Checked:** text file

> I should like to know whether there ever passed such childish notions in the hearts of other men as sometimes do in mine. In the midst of my studies, and of a life as innocent as man could lead, notwithstanding every persuasion to the contrary, the dread of hell frequently tormented me. I asked myself, "What state am I in? Should I die at this instant, must I be damned?" According to my Jansenists the matter was indubitable, but according to my conscience it appeared quite the contrary [...] From that moment I never doubted my salvation: I know not on recollecting this trait, whether I ought to laugh or shudder at myself.

> I never love to pray in a chamber; it seems to me that the walls and all the little workmanship of man interposed between God and myself: I love to contemplate Him in his works, which elevate my soul, and raise my thoughts to Him. My prayers were pure, I can affirm it, and therefore worthy to be heard

Conscience is set against doctrine, and human mediation ("the workmanship of man") is pushed aside: answerability before God, with the inner verdict taking precedence. The bound (God, damnation, salvation) is still there. And the passage mocks itself: what settles the question is a stone thrown at a tree, not conscience.

### rousseau-confessions.07 · Book VII — "I cannot be deceived in what I have felt"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C4.17 (for), C4.16 (for)
- **Location:** Book VII; lines 9885–9898
- **Checked:** text file

> I have but one faithful guide on which I can depend: this is the chain of the sentiments by which the succession of my existence has been marked, and by these the events which have been either the cause or the effect of the manner of it. [...] I may omit facts, transpose events, and fall into some errors of dates; but I cannot be deceived in what I have felt, nor in that which from sentiment I have done; and to relate this is the chief end of my present work. The real object of my confessions is to communicate an exact knowledge of what I interiorly am and have been in every situation of my life. I have promised the history of my mind, and to write it faithfully I have no need of other memoirs: to enter into my own heart, as I have hitherto done, will alone be sufficient.

The key text for feeling coming before facts. Facts and dates can be wrong; feeling cannot. Events are ranked by feeling ("cause or the effect" of sentiment), and the heart alone is enough. The scope is limited to self-knowledge, not knowledge of the world, so it supports C5.3 only for knowledge of oneself. In context the documents are lost, so this is partly a forced choice.

### rousseau-confessions.08 · Book VIII — independence of opinion; walking alone and its price

- **Claims:** C4.16 (complicates), C7.8 (for), C4.18 (for)
- **Location:** Book VIII; lines 12906–12910, 13150–13158
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] completed the fermentation of my heart of that first leaven of heroism and virtue which my father, my country, and Plutarch had inspired in my infancy. Nothing now appeared great in my eyes but to be free and virtuous, superior to fortune and opinion, and independent of all exterior circumstances

> These, hurt at seeing me walk alone in a new path, while I seemed to take measures for my happiness, used all their endeavors to render me ridiculous, and that they might afterwards defame me, first strove to make me contemptible. It was less my literary fame than my personal reformation, of which I here state the period, that drew upon me their jealousy; they perhaps might have pardoned me for having distinguished myself in the art of writing; but they could never forgive my setting them, by my conduct, an example, which, in their eyes, seemed to reflect on themselves.

The value at stake is classical civic virtue and freedom from opinion, inherited from "my father, my country, and Plutarch". It is not difference for its own sake, and difference comes out as a by-product of virtue. But difference, once lived ("walk alone in a new path"), is read at once as provoking envy and persecution. Suspicion of others travels with it.

### rousseau-confessions.09 · Book VIII — the children: custom, then "No, I feel"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C7.1 (complicates), C4.16 (against)
- **Location:** Book VIII; lines 12463–12467, 12940–12957
- **Checked:** text file

> I caught the manners I daily had before my eyes: I formed my manner of thinking upon that I observed to be the reigning one amongst amiable, and upon the whole, very honest people. I said to myself, since it is the custom of the country, they who live here may adopt it; this is the expedient for which I sought.

> [...] can these meet in the same mind with the depravity which without scruple treads under foot the most pleasing of all our duties? No, I feel, and openly declare this to be impossible. Never in his whole life could J. J. be a man without sentiment or an unnatural father. I may have been deceived, but it is impossible I should have lost the least of my feelings. [...] Since that time the regrets of my heart have more than once told me I was deceived; but my reason was so far from giving me the same intimation, that I have frequently returned thanks to Heaven for having by this means preserved them from the fate of their father

Against singularity: by his own account, the gravest act of his life was conformity to "the custom of the country". Then feeling is used to acquit him in spite of the deed ("No, I feel"). Yet here the *heart* is the accuser and *reason* the excuser, so feeling works as conscience against him as well as in his defence.

### rousseau-confessions.10 · Book VIII — the citizen conforms in worship

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.17 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book VIII; lines 14316–14325
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] mortified at being excluded from the rights of a citizen by the possession of a religion different from that of my forefathers, I resolved openly to return to the latter. I thought the gospel being the same for every Christian, and the only difference in religious opinions the result of the explanations given by men to that which they did not understand, it was the exclusive right of the sovereign power in every country to fix the mode of worship, and these unintelligible opinions; and that consequently it was the duty of a citizen to admit the one, and conform to the other in the manner prescribed by the law.

The community limit is kept, and on principle: inner faith is one and the same for all, while outward form belongs to the city's law. Rousseau's Self still has a civic bound. It is the reverse of difference as an absolute value.

### rousseau-confessions.11 · Book IX — the imaginary world "after my own heart"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (complicates), C5.8 (complicates), C7.2 (for)
- **Location:** Book IX; lines 15657–15666, 15780–15783
- **Checked:** text file

> The impossibility of attaining real beings threw me into the regions of chimera, and seeing nothing in existence worthy of my delirium, I sought food for it in the ideal world, which my imagination quickly peopled with beings after my own heart. [...] Entirely forgetting the human species, I formed to myself societies of perfect beings, whose virtues were as celestial as their beauty, tender and faithful friends, such as I never found here below.

> [...] but my imagination, fatigued with invention, wished for some real place which might serve it as a point to rest upon, and create in me an illusion with respect to the real existence of the inhabitants I intended to place there.

Feeling makes its own world when the real one fails, and that world (the source of *Julie*) shuts out "the human species". But the imagination cannot do without the real: it needs "some real place" to rest on. And he calls the result "chimera" and "delirium", not a truer reality. The Self that generates its world is there, but it knows it is a screen.

### rousseau-confessions.12 · Book IX — Rousseau condemns "follow the inclinations of the heart"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (against), C4.17 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book IX; lines 17180–17191
- **Checked:** text file

> He who feels his heart inflamed with this celestial fire strives to diffuse it, and wishes to show what he internally is. He would wish to place his heart in his countenance, and thinks not of other paint for his cheeks.
>
> I remember the summary of his morality which Madam d'Epinay had mentioned to me and adopted. This consisted in one single article; that the sole duty of man is to follow all the inclinations of his heart. This morality, when I heard it mentioned, gave me great matter of reflection, although I at first considered it solely as a play of wit. But I soon perceived it was a principle really the rule of his conduct, and of which I afterwards had, at my own expense, but too many convincing proofs.

Strong counter-evidence. The doctrine that one's sole duty is to follow one's heart belongs to Grimm, his enemy, and Rousseau presents it as a vice. For Rousseau the heart is a witness to one's moral nature, not a licence. The first paragraph keeps the ideal of showing "what he internally is", so the line he draws runs between showing the heart and obeying it.

### rousseau-confessions.13 · Book X — against Montaigne; "the best of men"; correcting the public

- **Claims:** C4.15 (complicates), C4.16 (complicates), C7.8 (for), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book X; lines 18995–19006
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] I determined to make of these the only work of the kind, by an unexampled veracity, that, for once at least, the world might see a man such as he internally was. I had always laughed at the false ingenuousness of Montaigne, who, feigning to confess his faults, takes great care not to give himself any, except such as are amiable; whilst I, who have ever thought, and still think myself, considering everything, the best of men, felt there is no human being, however pure he may be, who does not internally conceal some odious vice. I knew I was described to the public very different from what I really was, and so opposite, that notwithstanding my faults, all of which I was determined to relate, I could not but be a gainer by showing myself in my proper colors.

Rousseau marks his own novelty against Montaigne: what is new is not taking the self as subject (Montaigne did that) but total sincerity. That bears on the C4.15 pressure point. The motive is set by the audience: he writes to correct a public image he believes false. And "a man such as he internally was" is offered as an example of every man ("no human being [...] does not internally conceal"), not as a singular case.

### rousseau-confessions.14 · Book XII — difference as the reason he is misjudged; the conspiracy

- **Claims:** C4.16 (for), C7.8 (for), C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** Book XII; lines 23767–23775, 21660–21669
- **Checked:** text file

> This is another declaration upon which I am previously certain of the incredulity of many of my readers, who obstinately continue to judge me by themselves, although they cannot but have seen, in the course of my life, a thousand internal affections which bore no resemblance to any of theirs. But what is still more extraordinary is, that they refuse me every sentiment, good or indifferent, which they have not, and are constantly ready to attribute to me such bad ones as cannot enter into the heart of man

> With this book begins the work of darkness, in which I have for the last eight years been enveloped, though it has not by any means been possible for me to penetrate the dreadful obscurity. In the abyss of evil into which I am plunged, I feel the blows reach me, without perceiving the hand by which they are directed or the means it employs. [...] the authors of my ruin have the inconceivable art of rendering the public, unknown to itself, or without its perceiving the effects of it, accomplice in their conspiracy.

In the late books the difference claim hardens: his inner affections bear "no resemblance to any of theirs", so others cannot judge him and misjudge him by default. Being unlike others makes them adversaries, and in the end the whole public is cast as an unknowing accomplice. Even here he appeals to "the heart of man" as a common standard.

### rousseau-confessions.15 · Book XII, last page — "were they a thousand times proved"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for), C4.17 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book XII; lines 24205–24222
- **Checked:** text file

> I added what follows on reading my memoirs to M. and Madam, the Countess of Egmont, the Prince Pignatelli, the Marchioness of Mesme, and the Marquis of Juigne.
>
> I have written the truth: if any person has heard of things contrary to those I have just stated, were they a thousand times proved, he has heard calumny and falsehood; and if he refuses thoroughly to examine and compare them with me whilst I am alive, he is not a friend either to justice or truth. For my part, I openly, and without the least fear declare, that whoever, even without having read my works, shall have examined with his own eyes, my disposition, character, manners, inclinations, pleasures, and habits, and pronounce me a dishonest man, is himself one who deserves a gibbet.
>
> Thus I concluded, and every person was silent; Madam d'Egmont was the only person who seemed affected; she visibly trembled, but soon recovered herself, and was silent like the rest of the company.

The book's last move sets self-testimony above any proof ("were they a thousand times proved"). The court is now an aristocratic salon, not the last trumpet of Book I, and the verdict is laid down for the audience rather than left to it (compare .05). It is performed as a reading, and it ends in silence.
