# Luther, *On the Babylonish Captivity of the Church* (1520)

Usually *The Babylonian Captivity of the Church* in modern translations.

- **Edition used:** trans. Wace & Buchheim, *First Principles of the Reformation* (1885 printing)
- **Text file:** `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` (OCR); starts at the section titled "On the Babylonish Captivity of the Church"
- **Standard modern edition** (for final citations): *Luther's Works* (American Edition), vol. 36
- **Claims to test:** C4.3

## Passages

### luther-captivity.01 · "Every man stands for himself; individual faith is required"

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for)
- **Location:** "Of the Sacrament of the Bread" (the mass as promise), Wace & Buchheim p. 174; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 11364–11380
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; margin marks ":" and "'■" removed at 11372–11373)

> Why did not Abraham believe on behalf of all the Jews? Why was every individual Jew required to exercise faith in the same promise which Abraham believed? Let us keep to this impregnable truth:—where there is a divine promise, there every man stands for himself; individual faith is required; every man shall give account for himself, and shall bear his own burdens; [...] just as a priest cannot administer a sacrament to any man on behalf of another, but administers the same sacrament to each individual separately. The priests in their work of consecration and administration act as ministers for us; not that we offer up any good work through them, or communicate actively; but by their means we receive the promise and its sign, and communicate passively.

This is the most direct textual support for C4.3 in all three treatises: no one can believe, or be saved, on another's behalf. The priest stays, but only as a minister who hands over the promise. A side note for C4.10: the individual who "shall give account for himself" is already here, some 170 years before Locke.

### luther-captivity.02 · No one may bind a Christian by one syllable without his consent

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** "Of the Sacrament of Baptism," Wace & Buchheim pp. 194–195; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 12328–12340
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; "what- 'ever" joined; "What ever" → "Whatever"; stray "/" removed)

> I say then, neither Pope, nor bishop, nor any man whatever has the right of making one syllable binding on a Christian man, unless it is done with his own consent. Whatever is done otherwise is done in a spirit of tyranny; and thus the prayers, fastings, almsgiving, and whatever else the Pope ordains and requires in the whole body of his decrees, which are as many as they are iniquitous, he has absolutely no right to require and ordain; and he sins against the liberty of the Church as often as he attempts anything of the kind.

Church law is said to bind only by the person's own consent. That is a strong individuating move, and the ecclesiastical counterpart to consent-based authority. For C4.1, the liberty is "given us by baptism" (line 12318), so it is present before the papal laws repress it.

### luther-captivity.03 · Proclaim liberty, but endure the tyranny

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Of the Sacrament of Baptism," Wace & Buchheim p. 196; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 12413–12422
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; margin marks "^ i," "/," "[," "I" removed)

> I cry aloud on behalf of liberty and conscience, and I proclaim with confidence that no kind of law can with any justice be imposed on Christians, whether by men or by angels, except so far as they themselves will; for we are free from all. If such laws are imposed on us, we ought so to endure them as still to preserve the consciousness of our liberty. We ought to know and stedfastly to protest that a wrong is being done to that liberty, though we may bear and even glory in that wrong; taking care neither to justify the tyrant nor to murmur against the tyranny.

The freedom from church structure is a freedom of *conscience* (to know and protest the wrong). It is not a licence to stop obeying. Outwardly one still bears the laws. The same pattern appears in luther-liberty.08.

### luther-captivity.04 · Infants are saved by the faith of others, and the Church's faith can change even a wicked adult

- **Claims:** C4.3 (against)
- **Location:** "Of the Sacrament of Baptism," Wace & Buchheim pp. 197–198; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 12479–12490
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; stray "'" removed at 12479)

> To this I reply, what all men say, that infants are aided by the faith of others, namely, that of those who bring them to baptism. [...] so through the prayers of the Church which brings the child in faith, to which prayers all things are possible, the infant is changed, cleansed, and renewed by faith infused into it. Nor should I doubt that even a wicked adult, if the Church were to bring him forward and pray for him, might undergo a change in any of the sacraments; just as we read in the gospel that the paralytic man was healed by the faith of others.

This sits uneasily with .01 ("can I believe for another man?"). In this passage the faith of the Church does stand in for the individual's, and not only for infants. Luther does not fully individuate salvation. The community's faith keeps a role the individual's faith cannot replace. This is the clearest "against" for the strong form of C4.3.

### luther-captivity.05 · Vows: allowed as private choice, condemned as a public estate

- **Claims:** C4.3 (for), C4.1 (for), C4.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** end of "Of the Sacrament of Baptism," Wace & Buchheim pp. 198–201; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 12539–12543, 12595–12604, 12621–12623
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; "_one" → "one"; "ancL" → "and"; dash "— -" at 12599 removed)

> We make an ample vow at baptism, a greater one than we can fulfil; and we shall have enough to do if we give all our efforts to this alone. But now we compass sea and land to make many proselytes; we fill the world with priests, monks, and nuns; and we imprison all these in perpetual vows. [...] I should certainly not forbid or object to any vow which a man may make of his own private choice. I do not wish altogether to condemn or depreciate vows; but my advice would be altogether against the public establishment or confirmation of any such mode of life. It is enough that every man should be at liberty to make private vows at his own peril; but that a public system of living under the constraint of vows should be inculcated, I consider to be a thing pernicious to the Church and to all simple souls. [...] even if every individual must be allowed to venture upon it at his own peril, if he will.

This is the clearest case of Luther preferring individual choice to an assigned estate. Monastic life is allowed as a private choice "at his own peril," but it may not be a public, lifelong status ("imprison [...] in perpetual vows"). For C4.2, the role being taken apart is a religious estate, not a socioeconomic one.

### luther-captivity.06 · Confession: the priest is not needed, but a brother is

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Concerning the Sacrament of Penance," Wace & Buchheim pp. 209–211; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 13040–13047, 13116–13122, 13134–13139
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; line 13120 is badly garbled ("|haj_manjfestly bestojve(LJ'Iie_J30wgr_^ on every^"); read as "has manifestly bestowed the power [...]"; "Hvm" → "Him" at 13122. **Must be checked against the scan.**)

> The secret confession, however, which is now practised, though it cannot be proved from Scripture, is in my opinion highly satisfactory, and useful or even necessary. I could not wish it not to exist; nay, I rejoice that it does exist in the Church of Christ, for it is the one great remedy for afflicted consciences; when, after laying open our conscience to a brother, and unveiling all the evil which lay hid there, we receive from the mouth of that brother the word of consolation [...] From all this I do not hesitate to say that whosoever voluntarily confesses his sins privately, in the presence of any brother, or, when told of his faults, asks pardon and amends his life, is absolved from his secret sins, since Christ has manifestly bestowed the power [...] on every believer in Him [...] to allow the freest permission to hear confessions of secret sins to all brethren and sisters; so that the sinner may reveal his sin to whom he will, with the object of seeking pardon and consolation, that is, the word of Christ uttered by the mouth of his neighbour.

This answers the to-do question. Yes, Luther removes the *priest* as necessary mediator ("any brother," "to whom he will"). But he does not remove mediation: absolution still comes as a word spoken by another person, and he keeps private confession as "even necessary." The relationship with God is taken out of the hierarchy and placed in the fellowship of believers, not made private.

### luther-captivity.07 · The Church is born of the word; the mind is judged by truth rather than judging it

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Of Orders," Wace & Buchheim pp. 228–229; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 13910–13917, 13932–13933
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; margin marks "I" and ";" removed at 13907–13909)

> The Church is born of the word of promise through faith, and is nourished and preserved by the same word; that is, she herself is established by the promises of God, not the promise of God by her. The word of God is incomparably above the Church, and her part is not to establish, ordain, or make anything in it, but only to be established, ordained, and made, as a creature. What man begets his own parent? Who establishes the authority by which he himself exists? [...] In fact the mind itself is taken possession of, and, having truth as its judge, is judged rather than judges.

Authority moves from the Church to the *word*, not to the individual. The individual mind is likewise "judged rather than judges." This is not yet the Self as source (cf. C5.3, C5.6). If anything it is a model of the healthy, receiving Self the essay wants. The passage is not tagged to §5 because it only offers a contrast there.

### luther-captivity.08 · "We are all equally priests", but no one may use the power without the community's consent

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Of Orders," Wace & Buchheim p. 235; `texts/luther-first-principles-wace-buchheim.txt` lines 14255–14262
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; spacing cleaned up; "com- munity" joined)

> Let every man then who has learnt that he is a Christian recognise what he is, and be certain that we are all equally priests; that is, that we have the same power in the word, and in any sacrament whatever; although it is not lawful for any one to use this power, except with the consent of the community, or at the call of a superior. For that which belongs to all in common no individual can arrogate to himself, until he be called.

The priesthood of all believers is held *in common*: the individual has the power but cannot exercise it alone. The argument is for equality ("equally priests," "the same power"), not for difference, which matters for C4.13. The same structure appears in the Address, lines 4463–4467.
