## Summary of the reading (2026-10-28)

### C5.1 (every vocabulary selects and deflects). **For**, with a narrower sense of "deflection" than the spec's.

The wording is Burke's, nearly exactly: "Even if any given terminology is a reflection of reality, by its very nature as a terminology it must be a selection of reality; and to this extent it must function also as a deflection of reality" (burke-ts.02, p. 45). It is universal and unavoidable: "We must use terministic screens, since we can't say anything without the use of terms; whatever terms we use, they necessarily constitute a corresponding kind of screen" (burke-ts.11, p. 50). Two limits on how the spec uses it. (1) Burke concedes that a terminology can be "a reflection of reality"; the screen is not a fiction laid over nothing. (2) His "deflection" is modest on purpose: he raises Pascal's "directing the intention", a device for evading a prohibition, only "in the hopes that I can thereby settle for less", and says the deflection he means "concerns simply the fact that any nomenclature necessarily directs the attention into some channels rather than others" (burke-ts.03). Deflection in Burke is direction of attention, not repression. When the essay speaks of deflection as suppression of truths (C5.7), that is a step past this sentence.

### C5.2 (the healthy Self is one screen among others available). **Complicates.**

Burke never speaks of a *healthy* screen or of a screen's health as depending on rival screens being available. What he has: (1) screens with "their proper uses", not "mutually exclusive" (burke-ts.01); (2) Bentham's remedy, reported as apt: the fictions cannot be avoided, but "we recognize their nature as fictions" and "discount accordingly" (burke-ts.07); (3) correction by competition, in the poem: "in our conflicts / Correcting one another", until "how we say things are" and "how things are" are "one" (burke-ts.19, .20). Against the claim as worded: Burke *doubts* plurality as a remedy. If we "correct the excesses of one terminology, by borrowing from several", what canon could sanction it? "Would not such an eclectic recipe itself involve a generalized philosophy of some sort?" (burke-ts.13). His answer to "an endless catalogue of terministic screens [...] none of which can be considered central" is "In one sense, yes", and then a claim of "special favors" for one central screen, Dramatism (burke-ts.14, .15). So for Burke the cure for a screen's excess is not having many screens, but one comprehensive screen of the right kind. "Perspective by incongruity" is not in this essay (the word "incongruity" does not occur); it is in *Permanence and Change*.

### Does the working definition (C5.3) hold?

**What is Burke's.**

1. Every vocabulary is a screen that selects and deflects (burke-ts.02, .11).
2. Every screen in part *produces* what it sees: "many of the "observations" are but implications of the particular terminology", and "much that we take as observations about "reality" may be but the spinning out of possibilities implicit in our particular choice of terms" (burke-ts.05). Patients "soon learn to have the kind of dreams best suited to the terms favored by their analysts" (burke-ts.04): the screen shapes the experience it then reads.
3. Every screen begins like faith: "Believe, that you may understand" has its terministic counterpart, "Pick some particular nomenclature, some one terministic screen" (burke-ts.08).
4. Forgetting the screen is the *normal* human state, not a special pathology: man "clings to a kind of naive verbal realism that refuses to let him realize the full extent of the role played by symbolicity in his notions of reality" (burke-ts.09); "To mistake this vast tangle of ideas for immediate experience" is a fallacy worse than taking a dream for experience (burke-ts.10).
5. A specialized screen goes wrong when it is "stretched to cover not just its own special field but a more comprehensive area", yielding the "over-socialized," "over-biologized," "over-psychologized" man (burke-ts.13); and "a specialized scientific nomenclature [...] necessarily involves fragmentation" of man (burke-ts.21).
6. A screen can see a rival principle only in its own terms, as a vice: Darwin's screen "so stressed the principle of continuity here that he could view the principle of discontinuity only as a case of human self-flattery" (burke-ts.12). This is the nearest thing in the essay to a screen that *admits no rival*.
7. A screen that glorifies one side of its own grammar and slights the other is condemned: to "glorify the "active" nature of "Man,"" while one must "slight any admonition as regards human susceptibility to the passivities of conditioning" is "a drastic kind of vandalism" (burke-ts.22).
8. When "an entire structure is infused by a single generating principle, this principle will be tautologically or repetitively implicit in all the parts" (burke-ts.18). This is a mechanism of totality, but Burke offers it as the *happy* route.

**What goes beyond Burke.**

- *A screen that no longer knows it is a screen*, as a special type. Burke has no such type. Not knowing is the default (point 4). It cannot be what marks the Totalitarian Self off from other screens.
- *Claims to be the origin of what it sees.* Burke has nothing like this as a property of one screen; on his account every screen is in part the origin of its observations (point 2). The spec's line between a Self that *filters* experience and a Self that *generates* it (SPEC §5, §10.2) is not Burke's, and it does not survive his essay as a formal test. To keep it, "origin" must be the *content* of this vocabulary (the Self says experience flows from the self), not a mark of how the screen works.
- *The only lens; admits no rival.* Not in Burke. The word "rival" occurs once, for university "interdepartmental rivalries" (p. 57, line 618). Burke himself argues that one screen should be central (burke-ts.15), so being privileged is not, for him, a pathology.
- *The Self as a screen.* Burke never discusses the self or the individual as a vocabulary; the word "individual" does not occur in the essay. His "personal equations" make each man "necessarily free" to be "his own tyrant" (burke-ts.14), but that is the private screen of every person, by "the nature of things", not a cultural vocabulary of the Self.
- *The feedback loop* (C5.4, below).

So the working definition is the essay's own construction, built with Burke's term. That is allowed, but the essay must not say Burke defines it.

**The pressure point: if every vocabulary is a screen, what makes this one different?** Burke does not answer it for the Self, but he gives four criteria for telling screens apart, and they are better than the definition's:

1. **Scope.** A specialized screen stretched over man in general (burke-ts.13). If the Self's vocabulary is a psychological or therapeutic specialty stretched to cover the whole person, it is Burke's "over-psychologized" man. This reading also explains C1.4 better than *the only lens*: in Burke one and the same move (a specialty stretched beyond its field) both totalizes the vocabulary and fragments the man (burke-ts.21). Our construction, not Burke's.
2. **Completeness of its grammar.** A screen of human action must keep the passive with the active, the Fall with the Creation; dropping one is "vandalism" (burke-ts.22, .24). This is the strongest criterion and it matches the §4 pattern: the Totalitarian Self would be the active half kept and the passive half (bondage, suffering, conditioning, "servitude") dropped. Our reading of Burke's case against Chein.
3. **Answerability to its objects.** The poem asks that we know "their rejoinders / To our utterances" and correct our utterances by them, "Not suppressing by dictatorial lie"; that the soil, and "nerve and muscle", get speech "To frame objections" (burke-ts.19). A screen is bad when it silences what it names. This fits C1.4's *cannot be answered to*. But it is a hymn that "states the problem in the accents of an ideal solution" (burke-ts.18), not an analytic test.
4. **Grounding.** Invented by "one person, or even one particular philosophic school" vs grounded in a "collective revelation" (burke-ts.15). This one cuts **against** the thesis: the Self's vocabulary can claim a long collective grounding (the §4 roots), and Burke's own privileged screen is the screen of *persons* who *act*, which is the Self's home ground.

**The strongest against-reading.** Burke's privileged screen, Dramatism, is a vocabulary of persons and action, and he insists the race "cannot possibly get along with itself on the basis of any other intuition" (burke-ts.16). The Self's vocabulary is a descendant of exactly this screen. If so, the Totalitarian Self is not an alien screen that pushes the others out but Burke's own central screen with its passive half cut away: Chein's "sales talk on "Man"" (burke-ts.22) is the model. The thesis then has to locate the fault inside the grammar of the person (what it drops), not in the fact that one vocabulary dominates. Burke also warns that no comprehensive statement "can exemplify any one of these emphases with complete consistency" (burke-ts.24): a screen of the whole person cannot in fact be total, which is a reason to doubt *admits no rival* as an empirical claim.

**Verdict on C5.3.** **Complicates.** The working definition (*a vocabulary that has become the only lens*) uses Burke's term but none of its distinguishing marks is Burke's; two of them (*does not know it is a screen*, *claims to be the origin*) are, on Burke's account, true of every screen. Burke supplies better marks: overextension of a specialty (burke-ts.13), a grammar that drops its passive half (burke-ts.22, .24), and silencing of rejoinders (burke-ts.19). Recommend restating C5.3 in those terms.

### C5.4 (the feedback loop). **Not in this essay.** Settles the logged pressure point for this text: the loop as worded is the spec's.

What is there:

- **"Implications" of terms**, many times: observations as "the spinning out of possibilities implicit in our particular choice of terms" (burke-ts.05); "track down the kinds of observation implicit in the terminology" (burke-ts.08); Dramatism as "a methodic tracking down of the implications" (burke-ts.17). This is how *every* vocabulary works, not a disease of one used in isolation.
- **"Perfection"**, once: "drama is the culminative form of action (this is a variant of the "perfection" principle discussed in the previous chapter)", with the chain from drama to conflict to "victimage" (burke-ts.17). This is the nearest kin to a vocabulary driven to the end of its line, but it is a chain of implication inside one key term; nothing about isolation, self-reference, or negating other vocabularies.
- **"Feedback"**, twice, both times as "current cant" for the *reflexive* (the middle voice, "words-about-words"), conceded to "the lowest kind of reduction" (burke-ts.23). It is a grammatical function, not a mechanism of calcification. But the same passage ends: "all of us, in pious terror, should be on guard regarding the role of the reflexive in our ideas of identity." That is the nearest Burke comes to the thesis, and he does not say why.
- **A loop of confirmation**, as a parenthetical commonplace: patients learn the dreams their analysts' terms favour (burke-ts.04).
- **Against:** "cycles of terms that imply one another" are named, and they produce not closure but "an ingredient of improvisational "freedom"" (burke-ts.26).

What is not there (searched the whole file): "entelechial", "terministic compulsion", "end of the line", "occupational psychosis", "trained incapacity", "isolation", "god-term". "Psychosis" occurs once, in scare quotes, for symbolicity in general: "the perspective or "psychosis" of symbolicity" (p. 61, line 777). The pieces of the spec's loop that Burke has (implications, perfection, screen-shaped experience) are real, and the spec may assemble them, but it must say the assembly is its own. *Permanence and Change* and "Definition of Man" are still to check.

### C1.4 and C10.2 (the self, the person, identity, the body). **C1.4: complicates. C10.2: for, on persons, not on the Self.**

- **Personality comes from speech.** "The human animal, as we know it, emerges into personality by first mastering whatever tribal speech happens to be its particular symbolic environment" (burke-ts.16). On this, a vocabulary that truly totalized should *form* the person, not float over a fragmented one. That cuts against C1.4's two layers, unless the vocabulary is of the fragmenting, specialized kind (burke-ts.13, .21).
- **But the layers can come apart.** The behaviorist's terms make persons into things, yet he "must treat his colleagues as persons" (burke-ts.16); Eliot, quoted by Burke, separates identity "lived out" from identity "known" (burke-ts.25). So Burke allows vocabulary and lived practice to diverge. In his cases, though, the *vocabulary* reduces and the *lived* layer keeps the person, the reverse of C1.4.
- **Fragmentation comes from specialization**, not from a totalizing vocabulary: "a specialized scientific nomenclature (no matter how comprehensive it may be) necessarily involves fragmentation" (burke-ts.21).
- **The two poles named.** "And may we have neither the mania of the One / Nor the delirium of the Many" (burke-ts.20). C1.4 claims both at once (a vocabulary that totalizes over a subject that fragments); Burke's poem treats them as the two failures to steer between. That Burke has words for both is useful; that he treats them as alternatives complicates C1.4.
- **C10.2.** Burke grants that the person may be a metaphysical illusion ("Maybe we are but things in motion") and holds that the practice of persons survives anyway: "illusion or not, the human race cannot possibly get along with itself on the basis of any other intuition" (burke-ts.16). That supports C10.2's premise that philosophical dissolution need not move the lived vocabulary of persons. It is about *persons* (actors vs things), not the Self, and it concerns the pragmatic distinction, not a growing vocabulary.
- **The body.** The brief's phrase about the human body as a point of reference is **not in this essay**; nor is any claim that the biological individual grounds identity while terms do the rest. What Burke says of the body: "we must posit a sheerly physiological set of "terms,"" the senses translating events "into terms of" sight and sound, with pleasure and pain "a rudimentary physiological analogue of the Yes-No pair" (burke-ts.25). The body has proto-terms; identity in the full sense comes with names (Eliot: "to do this we must have names"). And the reflexive "in our ideas of identity" is to be watched "in pious terror" (burke-ts.23). Check "Definition of Man" and "(Nonsymbolic) Motion/(Symbolic) Action" for the body as ground of individuality.

### C1.3, C5.7 (the screen suppresses truths). **For, in form; not about the Self.**

Burke's examples are screens that slight what they cannot fit: Darwin slighting the evidence for discontinuity (burke-ts.12); Freud's terms that "automatically deflect our attention from symbols that are not repressed but merely remote" (Harding, reported, p. 51, lines 320–321); Chein's active "Man" slighting "human bondage" (burke-ts.22). And "his powers of symbolicity give rise to kinds of symbolic action that, by the same token, make him susceptible to corresponding kinds of servitude" (burke-ts.24): the powers of the symbol-user carry their own bondage. This is the shape of C1.3. But each case is a screen deflecting a field, not a culture repressing truths, and Burke's word is "slight", not "repress".

### C5.6 (the Self prior to experience). **Complicates.**

For Burke the key term of every screen comes before what is seen through it ("the faith must "precede" the rationale", burke-ts.08), and the person comes after speech (burke-ts.16). A Self "prior to" experience would, in his terms, be a screen's key term mistaken for a fact: the logic of every screen, not a mark of a mutation.

### C5.14 (the self in God's place) and the pattern. **Complicates.**

"God" as "key term" is put in "by fiat", outright as in the Bible or by a break in the argument, or brought in "ambiguously" as in metaphysics, "coy theology" (burke-ts.06). Logology studies "words about God" as "words about words" (p. 47, lines 155–156). The Logos hymn praises a "Vast Almighty Title" whose one "generating principle" is "tautologically or repetitively implicit in all the parts" (burke-ts.18, .19). On Burke's account any vocabulary's top term holds the place God holds in theology. So a vocabulary with *self* as its key term would put the self structurally in God's place; that is predictable from logology, not a sign that this vocabulary is unique. Byrne's "You are God in a physical body" (C5.14) is in Burke's terms the key term put in by fiat. Our application.

### burke-ts.03 · p. 45 — "settle for less": deflection as the direction of attention

- **Claims:** C5.1 (for), C5.7 (complicates: deflection is not repression), §4 pattern (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 45; lines 64–70 (Pascal's "directing the intention", lines 52–63)
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> I bring up this satirically excessive account of directing the intention, in the hopes that I can thereby settle for less when discussing the ways in which "terministic screens" direct the attention. Here the kind of deflection I have in mind concerns simply the fact that any nomenclature necessarily directs the attention into some channels rather than others. In one sense, this likelihood is painfully obvious. A textbook on physics, for instance, turns the attention in a different direction from a textbook on law or psychology. But some implications of this terministic incentive are not so obvious.

Pascal's Jesuits used words to keep the act and evade the Church's prohibition, a near-perfect case of structure kept, limit dropped. Burke raises it only to set it aside: his deflection is "simply" where attention goes, not a device of evasion.

### burke-ts.04 · pp. 45–46 — colour filters; patients "learn to have the kind of dreams best suited to the terms favored by their analysts"

- **Claims:** C5.1 (for), C5.4 (complicates: a confirmation loop, as a commonplace), C5.3 (against the filter/generate line)
- **Location:** pp. 45–46; lines 71–92
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> When I speak of "terministic screens," I have particularly in mind some photographs I once saw. They were different photographs of the same objects, the difference being that they were made with different color filters. Here something so "factual" as a photograph revealed notable distinctions in texture, and even in form, depending upon which color filter was used for the documentary description of the event being recorded. Similarly, a man has a dream. He reports his dream to a Freudian analyst, or a Jungian, or an Adlerian, or to a practitioner of some other [p. 46] school. In each case, we might say, the "same" dream will be subjected to a different color filter, with corresponding differences in the nature of the event as perceived, recorded, and interpreted. (It is a commonplace that patients soon learn to have the kind of dreams best suited to the terms favored by their analysts.)

The source image of the screen. The parenthesis is the essay's only loop of the C5.4 kind: the therapeutic screen shapes the experience that then confirms it. Burke's example is therapy, the Self's own ground, but he calls it "a commonplace", true of any school.

### burke-ts.05 · p. 46 — "the spinning out of possibilities implicit in our particular choice of terms"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (against: every screen in part generates what it sees), C5.1 (for), C5.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 46; lines 99–104
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> We have now moved things one step further along. Not only does the nature of our terms affect the nature of our observations, in the sense that the terms direct the attention to one field rather than to another. Also, many of the "observations" are but implications of the particular terminology in terms of which the observations are made. In brief, much that we take as observations about "reality" may be but the spinning out of possibilities implicit in our particular choice of terms.

The brief's wording is exact. This is the key against-passage for the working definition: generating what it sees is not a mark of a totalitarian screen but a property of all screens ("many", "much", hedged by "may be").

### burke-ts.06 · p. 46 — "God" as "key term": put in "by fiat"; metaphysics as "coy theology"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C5.3 (for: a key term introduced by fiat governs its implications)
- **Location:** p. 46; lines 105–115
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Perhaps the simplest illustration of this point is to be got by contrasting secular and theological terminologies of motives. If you want to operate, like a theologian, with a terminology that includes "God" as its key term, the only sure way to do so is to put in the term, and that's that. The Bible solves the problem by putting "God" into the first sentence—and from this initial move, many implications "necessarily" follow. A naturalistic, Darwinian terminology flatly omits the term, with a corresponding set of implications—and that's that. I have called metaphysics "coy theology" because the metaphysician often introduces the term "God" not outright, as with the Bible, but by beginning with a term that ambiguously contains such implications; then he gradually makes these implications explicit. If the term is not introduced thus ambiguously, it can be introduced only by fiat, either outright at the beginning (like the Bible) or as a non sequitur (a break in the argument somewhere along the way).

A key term cannot be argued into a vocabulary; it is put in, or smuggled in. For C5.14: a self-vocabulary's top term would be put in the same way (Byrne's "You are God" is fiat outright; Emerson's Over-Soul might be the "coy" route). Our application.

### burke-ts.07 · pp. 46–47 — Bentham: "recognize their nature as fictions" and "discount accordingly"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for: the nearest thing to a screen that knows it is a screen, and Bentham's, not Burke's), C5.2 (complicates: the remedy is awareness, not plurality)
- **Location:** pp. 46–47; lines 119–121, 135–138
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> As Jeremy Bentham aptly pointed out, all terms for mental states, sociopolitical relationships, and the like are necessarily "fictions," in the sense that we must express such concepts by the use of terms borrowed from the realm of the physical. [...] Bentham does not hope that such "fictions" can be avoided. He but asks that we recognize their nature as fictions. So he worked out a technique for helping to disclose the imagery in such ideas, and to discount accordingly.

The one place the essay offers awareness of the screen as a remedy, and it is reported from Bentham with approval ("aptly"). Note it covers all terms for "mental states", so the vocabulary of the Self is made of such fictions too.

### burke-ts.08 · p. 47 — "Believe, that you may understand": "Pick some particular nomenclature, some one terministic screen"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (complicates: every screen begins in faith), C5.6 (complicates), C5.4 (complicates: tracking implications is the method, not a disease)
- **Location:** p. 47; lines 143–150, 163–167
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> by its very thoroughness theology does have a formula that we can adapt, for purely secular purposes of analysis. I have in mind the injunction, at once pious and methodological, "Believe, that you may understand (crede, ut intelligas)." In its theological application, this formula served to define the relation between faith and reason. That is, if one begins with "faith," which must be taken on authority, one can work out a rationale based on this faith. But the faith must "precede" the rationale. [...] The "logological," or "terministic" counterpart of "Believe" in the formula would be: Pick some particular nomenclature, some one terministic screen. And for "That you may understand," the counterpart would be: "That you may proceed to track down the kinds of observation implicit in the terminology you have chosen, whether your choice of terms was deliberate or spontaneous."

Every screen is taken on something like faith and precedes what it shows. So *dogma* in the sense of a vocabulary taken before evidence is not, for Burke, what sets one screen apart. The choice may be "spontaneous", i.e. unaware.

### burke-ts.09 · p. 48 — "naive verbal realism" (quoting chapter 1, "Definition of Man", p. 5)

### burke-ts.10 · p. 48 — "To mistake this vast tangle of ideas for immediate experience"

### burke-ts.11 · pp. 49–50 — "We must use terministic screens, since we can't say anything without the use of terms"

- **Claims:** C5.1 (for), C5.3 pressure point (for: the pressure is real; every vocabulary is a screen)
- **Location:** p. 49, lines 242–246; p. 50, lines 294–297
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Our point is: All three terminologies (Watson's, Bowlby's, Augustine's) directed the attention differently, and thus led to a correspondingly different quality of observations. ### burke-ts.12 · p. 50 — Darwin's screen could view discontinuity "only as a case of human self-flattery"

The best Burkean criterion for C5.3: not that a screen is the only one, but that a specialty is stretched over the whole man. The last two sentences cut against C5.2: borrowing from several screens needs a governing philosophy of its own.

### burke-ts.14 · p. 52 — "each man is "necessarily free" to be his own tyrant"; symbols as "alienation" from the start

- **Claims:** C5.3 (complicates: the personal screen is a tyranny by nature), C1.4 (complicates), C5.2 (complicates: Burke answers "In one sense, yes" to the endless catalogue)
- **Location:** p. 52; lines 359–376
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text); "At" for the file's "A t"

> And now where are we? Must we merely resign ourselves to an endless catalogue of terministic screens, each of which can be valued for the light it throws upon the human animal, yet none of which can be considered central? In one sense, yes. For, strictly speaking, there will be as many different world views in human history as there are people. (Tot homines, tot sententiae.) We can safely take it for granted that no one's "personal equations" are quite identical with anyone else's. In the unwritten cosmic constitution that lies behind all man-made Constitutions, it is decreed by the nature of things that each man is "necessarily free" to be his own tyrant, inexorably imposing upon himself the peculiar combination of insights associated with his peculiar combination of experiences. At the other extreme, each of us shares with all other members of our kind (the often-inhuman human species) the fatal fact that, however the situation came to be, all members of our species conceive of reality somewhat roundabout, through various media of symbolism. Any such medium will be, as you prefer, either a way of "dividing" us from the "immediate" (thereby setting up a kind of "alienation" at the very start of our emergence from infancy into that state of articulacy somewhat misleadingly called the "age of reason"); or it can be viewed as a paradoxical way of "uniting" us with things on a "higher level of awareness," or some such.

The only place Burke speaks of an individual's screen, and he calls it tyranny "decreed by the nature of things". A self-tyranny that is universal cannot be the mutation; the thesis needs a cultural vocabulary, not a personal equation.

- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Whether such proneness to symbolic activity be viewed as a privilege or a calamity (or as something of both), it is a distinguishing characteristic of the human animal in general. Hence it can properly serve as the basis of a general, or philosophic definition of this animal. From this terministic beginning, this intuitive grounding of a position, many observations "necessarily follow." But are we not here "necessarily" caught in our own net? Must we not concede that a screen built on this basis is just one more screen; [p. 53] and that it can at best be permitted to take its place along with all the others? - **Location:** p. 53; lines 396–403, 404–415
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Reverting now to our original term, "dramatistic," I would offer this basic proposition for your consideration: Despite the evidences of primitive animism (that endows many sheer things with "souls") and the opposite modes of contemporary behaviorism (designed to study people as mere things), we do make a pragmatic distinction between the "actions" of "persons" and the sheer "motions" of "things." ### burke-ts.17 · pp. 54–55 — "the "perfection" principle": "And if conflict, then victimage"

### burke-ts.18 · p. 55 — the "happy route": "a single generating principle [...] tautologically or repetitively implicit in all the parts"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (for: a mechanism of totality within one vocabulary), C5.14 (complicates), C5.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 55; lines 480–489
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> There is also a happy route, along the lines of a Platonic dialectic. This "idealization" ends on two weighty words. One, "synecdoche," is used in the sense of "part for the whole." The other, "tautology," refers to the fact that, insofar as an entire structure is infused by a single generating principle, this principle will be tautologically or repetitively implicit in all the parts.
