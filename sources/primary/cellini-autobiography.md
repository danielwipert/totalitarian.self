# Cellini, *Autobiography* (1558–63)

- **Edition used:** Symonds
- **Text file:** `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` (clean; Project Gutenberg #4028, Harvard Classics vol. 31 text)
- **Citation form:** Book.chapter, e.g. I.LXXIV. Book I runs I–CXXVIII (file lines 156–9900); Book II ("Part II") runs I–CXIII (file lines 9906–16650). The chapter numbers are Symonds's and match the standard Italian division.
- **Claims to test:** C4.21, C4.26; also bears on C4.1, C4.15, C4.16

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

Cellini does tell his life as the story of an exceptional man, and he says so plainly: "perhaps there were not two such men alive upon this earth" (.12); the Pope says men "unique in their profession, stand above the law" (.09). That is real, and it is earlier than anything in the Reformation line. But three things limit it.

1. **What he values is excellence, not difference.** His uniqueness is rank on a shared scale: he surpasses Miliano and then "surpass[es] myself" (.07), and he has never left "the noble manner of Michel Agnolo" (.05). He is the best at what everyone is trying to do, not unlike everyone. Rousseau's claim ("not made like any one") is a different kind of claim, so C4.16 is not challenged on its own ground.
2. **He does not present the book as a new kind of thing.** The opening makes autobiography a duty of "all men of whatsoever quality they be, who have done anything of excellence" (.02): a rule for a class of people, not a claim to be the first.
3. **The self is bounded at every turn.** By God (the sonnet, .01; the prison and vision, .10, .11; the Perseus casting, .13), by the stars (.06), by lineage and city (.03), and by patrons, who supply both the exemption from the law (.08, .09) and the stage (.14). His one idea of his own inner make, his anger, is put down to the stars and a "choleric" humour (.06), not offered as something worth showing.

What is new is the *tone*: a craftsman's pride in himself and his works, with no apology, written down as the purpose of the book (.04, .12). That is a Renaissance root of self-assertion. It is not the different Self, and it is not self-origination.

## Passages

### cellini.01 · Introductory Sonnet — "none surpass me quite"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (complicates), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** Introductory Sonnet; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 65–74
- **Checked:** text file

> THIS tale of my sore-troubled life I write,
> To thank the God of nature, who conveyed
> My soul to me, and with such care hath stayed
> That divers noble deeds I’ve brought to light.
> ‘Twas He subdued my cruel fortune’s spite:
> Life glory virtue measureless hath made
> Such grace worth beauty be through me displayed
> That few can rival, none surpass me quite.
> Only it grieves me when I understand
> What precious time in vanity I’ve spent-

The boast ("none surpass me quite") and the frame come together: the book is written "To thank the God of nature," and the excellence is God's work "through me." It closes with regret for time spent "in vanity." He is exceptional, but not his own origin.

### cellini.02 · I.I — why he writes: a duty of the excellent

- **Claims:** C4.26 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.I; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 158–163
- **Checked:** text file

> ALL men of whatsoever quality they be, who have done anything of excellence, or which may properly resemble excellence, ought, if they are persons of truth and honesty, to describe their life with their own hand; but they ought not to attempt so fine an enterprise till they have passed the age of forty. This duty occurs to my own mind now that I am travelling beyond the term of fifty-eight years

The reason to tell a life is *excellence*, and the telling is a general duty ("ALL men [...] ought"), not a claim that his life is unlike others. The life is worth writing because of what was done, not because of who he inwardly is. This supports the "exceptional individual" half of C4.26 but not "first" or "for his own sake."

### cellini.03 · I.II — "natural bragging" begins with lineage

- **Claims:** C4.26 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.II; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 179–181, 235–241
- **Checked:** text file

> Yet one must needs live like others; and so in a work like this there will always be found occasion for natural bragging, which is of divers kinds, and the first is that a man should let others know he draws his lineage from persons of worth and most ancient origin. [...] As for the trifling honours which I have gained for my house, under the well-known conditions of our present ways of living, and by means of my art, albeit the same are matters of no great moment, I will relate these in their proper time and place, taking much more pride in having been born humble and having laid some honourable foundation for my family, than if I had been born of great lineage and had stained or overclouded that by my base qualities.

Two sides. The self is first placed in a lineage, a city (Florence, founded by Caesar) and a "house," and bragging is excused because "one must needs live like others." But he also takes "more pride in having been born humble" and rising by his art: worth earned, not inherited. That is a self-made note, though the honour is "for my house."

### cellini.04 · I.XXVI — art as the reason for writing; "marked originality"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (complicates), C4.21 (for)
- **Location:** I.XXVI; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 1803–1812
- **Checked:** text file

> I wish to keep both space and words for my art, which has been my chief inducement to write as I am doing, and about which I shall have only too much to say. The spirit of honourable rivalry impelled me to attempt some other masterpiece, which should equal, or even surpass, the productions of that able craftsman, Lucagnolo, whom I have mentioned. Still I did not on this account neglect my own fine art of jewellery; and so both the one and the other wrought me much profit and more credit, and in both of them I continued to produce things of marked originality.

He says the "chief inducement to write" is his *art*, not himself. "Marked originality" is valued (Symonds's wording; check the Italian), but inside "honourable rivalry" with other craftsmen: originality as excellence in a shared craft.

### cellini.05 · I.XII–XIII — Michelangelo's cartoon, "the school of the world"

- **Claims:** C4.15 (for), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.XII, lines 863–866; I.XIII, lines 900–901; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> These two cartoons stood, one in the palace of the Medici, the other in the hall of the Pope. So long as they remained intact, they were the school of the world. [...] All the while I was at Florence, I studied the noble manner of Michel Agnolo, and from this I have never deviated.

His art is formed in a school and a tradition he says he "never deviated" from. The model is a master, and "the school of the world" is a common one. Excellence is measured by a shared standard, the opposite of valuing difference as such.

### cellini.06 · I.XVII — "the stars do not so much sway as force our conduct"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (complicates), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.XVII; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 1166–1176
- **Checked:** text file

> He refused to come, which made me so angry, that, fuming with fury and swelling like an asp, I took a desperate resolve. At this point one may observe how the stars do not so much sway as force our conduct. When I reflected on the great obligations which this Annibale owed my family, my rage grew to such a pitch that, turning wholly to evil, and being also by nature somewhat choleric, I waited till the magistrates had gone to dinner; and when I was alone, and observed that none of their officers were watching me, in the fire of my anger, I left the palace, ran to my shop, seized a dagger and rushed to the house of my enemies

The violence is explained by the stars and by a "choleric" humour: an astrological and humoral self, not a self-authored one. The bound here is cosmic, not moral, and it works partly as an excuse. Against any reading of Cellini as the author of his own acts in a modern sense.

### cellini.07 · I.XCI — "let us see if I can surpass myself"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (for), C4.16 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.XCI; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 7083–7094
- **Checked:** text file

> However, that did not dismay me; but having sharpened my wits up, I succeeded not only in making something quite as good, but in exceeding it by far. Then, when I saw that I had surpassed him, I went about to surpass myself, and produced a foil by new processes which was a long way better than what I had previously made. [...] I rejoined: “Now that I have surpassed Miliano, let us see if I can surpass myself.”

The nearest thing in Cellini to a self taken as its own measure: once he beats his rival, the rival is himself. But it is still excellence by a common, checkable standard (the jewellers price the stone). This is distinction, the "fury of distinguishing ourselves" Rousseau's *Discourse* calls a vice (C4.16), not difference of make.

### cellini.08 · I.LI — revenge for his brother; the Pope's "Now that you are cured"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (against), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.LI; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 4071–4074, 4114–4117
- **Checked:** text file

> When I saw that the fever caused by always seeing him about was depriving me of sleep and appetite, and was bringing me by degrees to sorry plight, I overcame my repugnance to so low and not quite praiseworthy an enterprise, and made my mind up one evening to rid myself of the torment. [...] Then, looking me straight in the face, he added: “Now that you are cured, Benvenuto, take heed how you live.” [5] I, who understood his meaning, promised that I would.

A killing told as a cure for a "fever," with no sense of answering to anyone for it. The only check is the Pope's look, and his pardon comes after praising the work. Here the bound is not God or community but a patron's favour. This counts against C4.1's "bounded" for this strand: the Renaissance artist is answerable to a patron, not to conscience.

### cellini.09 · I.LXXIV — Paul III: "unique in their profession, stand above the law"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (for), C4.1 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.LXXIV; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 5650–5657
- **Checked:** text file

> The Pope turned to him and answered: “You know less about such matters than I do. Know then that men like Benvenuto, unique in their profession, stand above the law; and how far more he, then, who received the provocation I have heard of?” When my safe conduct had been drawn out, I began at once to serve him, and was treated with the utmost favour.

The strongest passage for C4.26: uniqueness lifts a man out of the common law. But note who says it and why: the Pope, who wants his coins struck. Uniqueness is "in their profession," the freedom is granted from above, and it is repaid by service ("I began at once to serve him"). This is the patron's exception, not the self's own law. The "against" here is to the claim that no early form put the individual above the common bound.

### cellini.10 · I.CXVIII–CXIX — the prison: "who lent thee that body"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.CXVIII, lines 9030–9034; I.CXIX, lines 9040–9049; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> Thereafter, when I searched my memory to find what could have diverted me from that design of suicide, I came to the conclusion that it must have been some power divine and my good guardian angel. [...] DURING the following night there appeared to me in dreams a marvellous being in the form of a most lovely youth, who cried, as though he wanted to reprove me: “Knowest thou who lent thee that body, which thou wouldst have spoiled before its time?” I seemed to answer that I recognized all things pertaining to me as gifts from the God of nature. “So, then,” he said, “thou hast contempt for His handiwork, through this thy will to spoil it? Commit thyself unto His guidance, and lose not hope in His great goodness!”

At his lowest point the self is "lent" and its powers are "gifts from the God of nature." It is the same frame as Calvin's "We are not our own" (C4.1), reached from Catholic piety and Bible reading in prison, not from the Reformation.

### cellini.11 · I.CXXI–CXXII — the vision of the sun: "free and fortunate and in the grace of God"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** I.CXXI, lines 9261–9266; I.CXXII, lines 9333–9337; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> then I prayed more devoutly than ever I had done in the past, fervently imploring Christ that He would at least grant me the favour of knowing by divine inspiration what sin I was so sorely expiating [...] I began at once to shout aloud: “The virtue of God hath deigned to show me all His glory, the which perchance no mortal eye hath ever seen before. Therefore I know surely that I am free and fortunate and in the grace of God; but you miscreants shall be miscreants still, accursed, and in the wrath of God.

Before the vision he asks what sin he is "expiating": answerability before God. After it he claims a sight "no mortal eye hath ever seen before." So his singularity is real, but it is a singularity of *grace*: chosen by God, not self-made. (The famous "halo" passage, a light he says rested over his head after the vision, is not in this text file; see To do.)

### cellini.12 · II.LV — "not two such men alive upon this earth"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (for), C4.16 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.LV; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 13239–13248
- **Checked:** text file

> Thereupon my blood was up, and I cried: “Mark my words, then, Ser Pier Francesco Riccio! I will tell you what sort of men are my equals, and who are yours--mere teachers of the alphabet to children!” His face contracted with a spasm, while he raised his voice and repeated the same words in a still more insulting tone. I, too, assumed an air of menace, and matching his own arrogance with something of the same sort, told him plainly that men of my kind were worthy to converse with popes and emperors, and great kings, and that perhaps there were not two such men alive upon this earth, while ten of his sort might be met at every doorway.

The plainest claim to singularity in the book, and it is a claim about *rank*: "men of my kind" belong with "popes and emperors." The words are said in a quarrel with the Duke's majordomo, about who is worth more at court. He is rare, but rare as a type ("men of my kind"), not unlike all others. He values being above, not being different.

### cellini.13 · II.LXXVII–LXXVIII — casting the Perseus: "the hand of God arranging and controlling all"

- **Claims:** C4.1 (for), C4.26 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.LXXVII, lines 14543–14548; II.LXXVIII, lines 14598–14599, 14613–14616; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt`
- **Checked:** text file

> while I, now here, now there, gave orders, helped with my own hands, and cried aloud: “O God! Thou that by Thy immeasurable power didst rise from the dead, and in Thy glory didst ascend to heaven!”&hellip;. even thus in a moment my mould was filled; and seeing my work finished, I fell upon my knees, and with all my heart gave thanks to God. [...] In my great astonishment I seemed to see in this the hand of God arranging and controlling all. [...] the bronze was hotter than our rules of art prescribe; also that I had been obliged to supplement the alloy with my pewter cups and platters, which no one else, I think, had ever done before.

His greatest work: his own hands, a thing "no one else [...] had ever done before," *and* "the hand of God arranging and controlling all." Both in one scene. The novelty is technical, measured against "our rules of art."

### cellini.14 · II.XCII — the Perseus unveiled: "pointing me out as something marvellous and strange"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (for), C4.25 (for), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.XCII; `texts/cellini-autobiography.txt` lines 15355–15357, 15370–15372, 15380–15381, 15386–15390
- **Checked:** text file

> NOW it pleased my glorious Lord and immortal God that at last I brought the whole work to completion: and on a certain Thursday morning I exposed it to the public gaze. [...] I passed a happy day, partly because of the Duke’s message, and also because the folk kept pointing me out as something marvellous and strange. [...] because the folk were stopping and staring at me more than at my Perseus. [...] “You give me much cause for wonder, seeking as you do to make me quit the service of a prince who is the greatest patron of the arts that ever lived; and I too here in my own birthplace, famous as the school of every art and science!

The public looks at the maker "more than at my Perseus": the artist as a spectacle (the "something marvellous and strange" is Symonds's wording; check the Italian). This is the nearest thing in the book to the self valued for itself, and it is a self in front of others' eyes (C4.25). Even so it opens with God ("it pleased my glorious Lord"), and he answers the offer with loyalty to a patron and a city.
