## Passages: Winthrop

### winthrop.01 · MHS 1838 p. 33 — inequality ordained by God

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** opening of the "Modell"; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 2551–2555
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; clean here)

> God Almighty in his most holy and wise providence, hath soe disposed of the condition of mankind, as in all times some must be rich, some poore, some high and eminent in power and dignitie ; others mean and in submission.

The first sentence carries a European order of rank across the Atlantic and makes it divine. The founders did not arrive without inherited structure: they brought a hierarchy on purpose (against C6.4; C6.2 has to allow for it).

### winthrop.02 · MHS 1838 p. 34 — "every man might have need of others"; wealth is God's, held for "the common good"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** "3 Reas."; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 2596–2606
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; "propperty", "theire" are the printed spellings)

> 3 Reas. Thirdly, that every man might have need of others, and from hence they might be all knitt more nearly together in the Bonds of brotherly affection. From hence it appears plainly that noe man is made more honourable than another or more wealthy &c., out of any particular and singular respect to himselfe, but for the glory of his creator and the common good of the creature, man. Therefore God still reserves the propperty of these gifts to himself as Ezek. 16. 17. he there calls wealthe, his gold and his silver , and Prov. 3. 9. he claims theire service as his due, honor the Lord with thy riches &c.

Inequality exists so that people need each other. A man's wealth or honour is never "out of any particular and singular respect to himselfe"; it stays God's property, held for the common good. This is stewardship, not wealth as a sign of election (C4.4: no work-as-proof here).

### winthrop.03 · MHS 1838 p. 38 — "community of perill": "lesse respect towards ourselves and our owne right"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against)
- **Location:** "Quest. What rule must wee observe and walke by in cause of community of perill?"; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 2865–2869
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> Ans. The same as before, but with more enlargement towards others and lesse respect towards ourselves and our owne right. Hence it was that in the primitive Churche they sold all, had all things in common, neither did any man say that which he possessed was his owne.

The emigration is a "community of perill", and in it a man's own right gives way. The model held up is the apostolic common purse. A Reformed preacher is setting out a communal economic ethic, with no Catholic source behind it.

### winthrop.04 · MHS 1838 p. 40 — "true Christians are of one body in Christ"; love as the "ligaments"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** conclusions from the definition of love; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 2992–3004
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "21y", "31y", "51y" = 2ly, 3ly, 4ly; "weal'e" = weale)

> of all, true Christians are of one body in Christ, 1 Cor. 12. 12. 13. 17. Ye are the body of Christ and members of their parte. All the partes of this body being thus vnited are made soe contiguous in a speciall relation as they must needes partake of each other’s strength and infirmity ; joy and sorrowe, weal'e and woe. 1 Cor. 12. 26. If one member suffers , all suffer with it, if one be in honor , all rejoyce with it. 21y. The ligaments of this body which knitt together are loue. 31y. Noe body can be perfect which wants its proper ligament. 51y. This sensibleness and sympathy of each other’s conditions will necessarily infuse into each parte a native desire and endeavour, to strengthen, defend, preserve and comfort the other.

"Knitt together" is verified (the word "knitt" recurs about ten times in the sermon: lines 2597, 2971, 2981, 2984, 3000, 3032, 3096, 3157, 3244, 3377). The person is a part of a body, which is the opposite of the separate Self.

### winthrop.05 · MHS 1838 p. 41 — self-love is Adam's fall: "to loue and seeke himselfe onely"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** "how this loue comes to be wrought"; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3063–3069
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> But Adam, rent himselfe from his Creator, rent all his posterity allsoe one from another ; whence it comes that every man is borne with this principle in him to loue and seeke himselfe onely, and thus a man continueth till Christ comes and takes possession of the soule and infuseth another principle, loue to God and our brother

Winthrop knows the self-seeking self and calls it the natural, fallen condition. Grace replaces it with "another principle". The separate self is the starting point to be overcome, not a value. Note the word "principle": Crèvecoeur's "new principles" and "SELF-INTEREST" (crevecoeur.06) turn this upside down.

### winthrop.06 · MHS 1838 p. 45 — "by a mutuall consent"; "the care of the publique must oversway all private respects"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** application, "2nly for the worke wee have in hand"; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3277–3287
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "special 1" = speciall; "meare" as printed)

> 2nly for the worke wee have in hand. It is by a mutuall consent, through a special 1 overvaluing providence and a more than an ordinary approbation of the Churches of Christ, to seeke out a place of cohabitation and Consorteshipp under a due forme of Government both ciuill and ecclesiasticall. In such cases as this, the care of the publique must oversway all private respects, by which, not only conscience, but meare civill pollicy, dothe binde us. For it is a true rule that particular Estates cannot subsist in the ruin of the publique.

The public comes first, by conscience and by policy alike. But the community is made "by a mutuall consent": it is joined, not inherited. That complicates C6.2. The check on the Self in New England was real, but it was chosen and covenanted, not a feudal or parish inheritance.

### winthrop.07 · MHS 1838 p. 46 — the covenant: "The Lord hath given us leave to drawe our own articles"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C6.2 (complicates), C6.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** application, the three reasons; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3355–3372
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "w-hich" = which; "[sinful]" is the 1838 editors' bracket)

> Thus stands the cause betweene God and us. We are entered into Covenant with Him for this worke. Wee haue taken out a commission. The Lord hath given us leave to drawe our own articles. [...] but if wee shall neglect the observation of these articles w-hich are the ends wee have propounded, and, dissembling with our God, shall fall to embrace this present world and prosecute our carnall intentions, seeking greate things for ourselves and our posterity, the Lord will surely breake out in wrathe against us ; be revenged of such a [sinful] people and make us knowe the price of the breache of such a covenant.

The bond goes both ways: the people bound to each other and, as one body, to God. Two twists. The people draw "our own articles", so the covenant is self-made in form. And the breach it names is the later American programme, "seeking greate things for ourselves and our posterity". At the founding, private gain is the danger, not the principle (C6.5).

### winthrop.08 · MHS 1838 pp. 46–47 — "knitt together, in this worke, as one man"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C6.2 (against)
- **Location:** application, the means; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3377–3403
- **Checked:** text file (OCR; page header between p. 46 and p. 47 omitted without [...]; [...] marks the words "Wee must entertaine each other in brotherly affection", which straddle the page break)

> For this end, wee must be knitt together, in this worke, as one man. [...] Wee must be willing to abridge ourselves of our superfluities, for the supply of other’s necessities. Wee must uphold a familiar commerce together in all meekeness, gentlenes, patience and liberality. Wee must delight in eache other ; make other’s conditions our oune ; rejoice together, mourne together, labour and suffer together, allwayes haueving before our eyes our commission and community in the worke, as members of the same body.

This is the clearest statement of the communal counter-tradition that C6.4 says was missing, at the very founding of Massachusetts. Against C6.2 too: whatever Europe's structures did, this was meant to do it more strictly.

### winthrop.09 · MHS 1838 p. 47 — "a citty upon a hill"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (complicates), C6.4 (against)
- **Location:** application; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3414–3425
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "w*ee" = wee; "by- word" = by-word)

> For wee must consider that wee shall be as a citty upon a hill. The eies of all people are uppon us. Soe that if wee shall deale falsely with our God in this worke w*ee haue undertaken, and soe cause him to withdrawe his present help from us, wee shall be made a story and a by- word through the world.

"Citty upon a hill" is verified. In context it is a warning, not a boast. What stands answerable is the community as one "wee", before God and the world, not each person alone. That complicates the answerability root of C1.1 for America's founding text. Later uses that make the phrase a boast are reception, not Winthrop.

### winthrop.10 · MHS 1838 p. 47 — "other Gods , our pleasure and proffitts"

- **Claims:** C6.5 (complicates), C6.4 (against)
- **Location:** closing exhortation (Deut. 30); `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 3435–3437
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "away 9" = away,; "wor shipp" = worshipp)

> But if our heartes shall turne away 9 soe that wee will not obey , but shall be seduced , and wor shipp and serue other Gods , our pleasure and proffitts, and serue them

Profit is named as an idol in the last lines. If America's economic conditions were its founding conditions (C6.5), its founding sermon treats them as the chief temptation.

### winthrop.11 · Winthrop, *History* II. 87 (1642), quoted in the 1838 prefatory remarks — "all, or most, would have forsaken thee"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (complicates), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** prefatory remarks by a member of the MHS, before p. 33, quoting Winthrop's *History* II. 87; `texts/winthrop-model-christian-charity-mhs1838.txt` lines 2501–2516
- **Checked:** text file (OCR: "where*" = where; this is Winthrop as quoted by the 1838 editor, not the sermon; check against the *Journal* before final citation)

> For such as come together into a wilderness, where* are nothing but wild beasts and beastlike men, and there confederate together in civil and church estate, whereby they do, implicitly at least, bind themselves to support each other, and all of them that Society, whether civil or sacred, whereof they are members, — how they can break from this without free consent, is hard to find, so as may satisfy a tender or good conscience in time of trial. [...] for if one may go, another may, and so the greater part, and so church and commonwealth may be left destitute in a wilderness, exposed to misery and reproach, and all for thy ease and pleasure

Twelve years on, Winthrop is pleading with settlers who leave the colony. The covenant bound "implicitly at least", but people were already breaking it for their "ease and pleasure". So the counter-tradition was strong, and it was under strain from the start.

## Passages: Crèvecoeur

### crevecoeur.01 · Letter III — "no aristocratical families, no courts, no kings, no bishops"

- **Claims:** C6.2 (for), C6.5 (for)
- **Location:** Letter III, "What is an American", ¶1; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1733–1747
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not composed, as in Europe, of great lords who possess everything, and of a herd of people who have nothing. Here are no aristocratical families, no courts, no kings, no bishops, no ecclesiastical dominion, no invisible power giving to a few a very visible one; no great manufacturers employing thousands, no great refinements of luxury. [...] We are a people of cultivators, scattered over an immense territory, communicating with each other by means of good roads and navigable rivers, united by the silken bands of mild government, all respecting the laws, without dreading their power, because they are equitable. We are all animated with the spirit of an industry which is unfettered and unrestrained, because each person works for himself.

The strongest evidence for C6.2 in either text: the European structures of rank and church are named and said to be missing. But he praises their absence, and "silken bands of mild government" and respect for "the laws" take their place. C6.2 says the checks were thin; Crèvecoeur says they were mild.

### crevecoeur.02 · Letter III — New England: "decency of their manners"; back-settlers kept in bounds "by the influence of religion"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (against), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1775–1787 and 2193–2197
- **Checked:** text file

> The eastern provinces must indeed be excepted, as being the unmixed descendants of Englishmen. [...] I respect them for what they have done; for the accuracy and wisdom with which they have settled their territory; for the decency of their manners; for their early love of letters; their ancient college, the first in this hemisphere; for their industry [...] Whatever has been said of the four New England provinces, no such degeneracy of manners has ever tarnished their annals; their back-settlers have been kept within the bounds of decency, and government, by means of wise laws, and by the influence of religion.

Crèvecoeur leaves New England out of his "new man": it is unmixed, and its frontier is held "within the bounds" by law and religion. A hundred and fifty years after Winthrop, the communal check is still visible to an outsider. His America is the middle colonies.

### crevecoeur.03 · Letter III — "Every thing has tended to regenerate them; new laws, a new mode of living, a new social system"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates), C6.2 (for)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1804–1818
- **Checked:** text file

> Every thing has tended to regenerate them; new laws, a new mode of living, a new social system; here they are become men: in Europe they were as so many useless plants, wanting vegetative mould, and refreshing showers; they withered, and were mowed down by want, hunger, and war; but now by the power of transplantation, like all other plants they have taken root and flourished! [...] By what invisible power has this surprising metamorphosis been performed? By that of the laws and that of their industry. The laws, the indulgent laws, protect them as they arrive, stamping on them the symbol of adoption; they receive ample rewards for their labours; these accumulated rewards procure them lands; those lands confer on them the title of freemen

The remaking is done to the emigrant ("regenerate them", passive) by laws, land and labour. Industry is his part, but the agents named first are the laws. This is a person remade by the new country, as C6.11 says, not a self-made one.

### crevecoeur.04 · Letter III — "What then is the American, this new man?"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (for, as worded; complicates any self-made reading)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1840–1852
- **Checked:** text file

> What then is the American, this new man? He is either an European, or the descendant of an European, hence that strange mixture of blood, which you will find in no other country. [...] He is an American, who, leaving behind him all his ancient prejudices and manners, receives new ones from the new mode of life he has embraced, the new government he obeys, and the new rank he holds. He becomes an American by being received in the broad lap of our great Alma Mater. Here individuals of all nations are melted into a new race of men

"New man", "ancient prejudices and manners" and "new mode of life" are all verified. The sentence is in two halves: he leaves behind, and he "receives new ones". He has new manners, not none, and they come from the government he "obeys". He is "received" by a mother. C6.11 as worded stands. A reading that makes him self-made does not.

### crevecoeur.05 · Letter III — "SELF-INTEREST"; "religion demands but little of him"; "new principles"

- **Claims:** C6.5 (for), C6.11 (for), C6.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1858–1871
- **Checked:** text file (capitals as printed)

> The American ought therefore to love this country much better than that wherein either he or his forefathers were born. Here the rewards of his industry follow with equal steps the progress of his labour; his labour is founded on the basis of nature, SELF-INTEREST: can it want a stronger allurement? Wives and children, who before in vain demanded of him a morsel of bread, now, fat and frolicsome, gladly help their father to clear those fields whence exuberant crops are to arise to feed and to clothe them all; without any part being claimed, either by a despotic prince, a rich abbot, or a mighty lord. Here religion demands but little of him; a small voluntary salary to the minister, and gratitude to God; can he refuse these? The American is a new man, who acts upon new principles

"SELF-INTEREST" is verified and is named as the basis of labour, "the basis of nature". It is Winthrop's fallen "principle" (winthrop.05) renamed as nature. But it is the interest of a household: wife and children share the work and the crops. Religion is thinned to "gratitude to God", but not gone.

### crevecoeur.06 · Letter III — "We are nothing but what we derive from [...] the government we obey, the system of religion we profess"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1886–1890
- **Checked:** text file

> Men are like plants; the goodness and flavour of the fruit proceeds from the peculiar soil and exposition in which they grow. We are nothing but what we derive from the air we breathe, the climate we inhabit, the government we obey, the system of religion we profess, and the nature of our employment.

This is the opposite of self-origination: the person is a product of soil, government, religion and work. Crèvecoeur's America makes its men; they do not make themselves.

### crevecoeur.07 · Letter III — "men are nothing of themselves"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III, before the Hebridean story; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 2522–2529
- **Checked:** text file

> Pray, what is the reason that the Scots are in general more religious, more faithful, more honest, and industrious than the Irish? I do not mean to insinuate national reflections, God forbid! It ill becomes any man, and much less an American; but as I know men are nothing of themselves, and that they owe all their different modifications either to government or other local circumstances, there must be some powerful causes which constitute this great national difference.

He says it again, in so many words. The "new man" is formed from outside.

### crevecoeur.08 · Letter III — "Industry, good living, selfishness, litigiousness [...] religious indifference"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (for, for the middle colonies), C6.5 (for)
- **Location:** Letter III, the middle settlements; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1911–1923
- **Checked:** text file

> As freemen they will be litigious; pride and obstinacy are often the cause of law suits; the nature of our laws and governments may be another. [...] As farmers they will be careful and anxious to get as much as they can, because what they get is their own. As northern men they will love the cheerful cup. As Christians, religion curbs them not in their opinions; the general indulgence leaves every one to think for themselves in spiritual matters; the laws inspect our actions, our thoughts are left to God. Industry, good living, selfishness, litigiousness, country politics, the pride of freemen, religious indifference, are their characteristics.

For the middle colonies this is close to C6.4's picture: religion does not curb opinion, and "selfishness" is a named trait. But the split is Lockean ("the laws inspect our actions, our thoughts are left to God"): action stays under law, and the inner life is answerable to God.

### crevecoeur.09 · Letter III — "religious indifference is imperceptibly disseminated [...] a vacuum fit to receive other systems"

- **Claims:** C6.4 (for, with a date), C6.1 (for)
- **Location:** Letter III, on the sects; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 2077–2081
- **Checked:** text file

> Thus all sects are mixed as well as all nations; thus religious indifference is imperceptibly disseminated from one end of the continent to the other; which is at present one of the strongest characteristics of the Americans. Where this will reach no one can tell, perhaps it may leave a vacuum fit to receive other systems.

The best support for C6.4, and for "conditions, not cause" (C6.1). But it describes a *process* under way by the 1770s, caused by mixing and distance, not something that "arrived" dominant. In his account the counter-tradition wore out; it was not missing at the start.

### crevecoeur.10 · Letter III — the frontier: "remote from the power of example and check of shame"

- **Claims:** C6.3 (complicates), C6.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III, the back settlements; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1952–1957 and 2138–2142
- **Checked:** text file

> where men are wholly left dependent on their native tempers, and on the spur of uncertain industry, which often fails when not sanctified by the efficacy of a few moral rules. There, remote from the power of example and check of shame, many families exhibit the most hideous parts of our society. [...] The Moravians and the Quakers are the only instances in exception to what I have advanced. The first never settle singly, it is a colony of the society which emigrates; they carry with them their forms, worship, rules, and decency

For Crèvecoeur, the self without bounds (no example, no shame, no worship) degenerates. It is not his American. The exceptions are communities that move as communities: the Winthrop pattern again.

### crevecoeur.11 · Letter III — the emigrant naturalised: "Pride steps in [...] he respects them"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 2353–2367
- **Checked:** text file

> It is in consequence of that change that he becomes an American. This great metamorphosis has a double effect, it extinguishes all his European prejudices, he forgets that mechanism of subordination, that servility of disposition which poverty had taught him; and sometimes he is apt to forget too much, often passing from one extreme to the other. If he is a good man, he forms schemes of future prosperity, he proposes to educate his children better than he has been educated himself; he thinks of future modes of conduct, feels an ardour to labour he never felt before. Pride steps in and leads him to everything that the laws do not forbid: he respects them; with a heart-felt gratitude he looks toward the east, toward that insular government from whose wisdom all his new felicity is derived, and under whose wings and protection he now lives. These reflections constitute him the good man and the good subject.

The danger is named ("apt to forget too much"), and the bounds are named too: the laws, gratitude to a government, the children's future. Pride runs up to the edge of the law and stops there.

### crevecoeur.12 · Letter III, "History of Andrew, the Hebridean" — the house-raising; road overseer and juror

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates), C6.3 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter III, Andrew's history; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 3076–3081 and 3126–3131
- **Checked:** text file

> As he had neither mowing nor reaping to do that year, I told him that the time was come to build his house; and that for the purpose I would myself invite the neighbourhood to a frolic; that thus he would have a large dwelling erected, and some upland cleared in one day. [...] He helped others as generously as others had helped him; and I have dined many times at his table with several of his neighbours. The second year he was made overseer of the road, and served on two petty juries, performing as a citizen all the duties required of him.

Crèvecoeur's model success is carried by neighbours: about forty raise his house in a day (lines 3082–3091). Then he takes on civic duty. The story is billed as success "not by virtue of any freaks of fortune, but by the gradual operation of sobriety, honesty, and emigration" (lines 2622–2623). Industry plus a helping community, not self-reliance alone.

### crevecoeur.13 · Letter II — "the lord of all land"; "I did not work for myself alone"; "tread his paths"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates), C6.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter II, "On the situation, feelings, and pleasures of an American farmer"; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 1218–1219, 1229–1232, 1242–1244
- **Checked:** text file

> I felt that I did not work for myself alone, and this encouraged me much. [...] I owe nothing, but a pepper corn to my country, a small tribute to my king, with loyalty and due respect; I know no other landlord than the lord of all land, to whom I owe the most sincere gratitude. [...] by a long series of industry and honest dealings, my father left behind him the name of a good man; I have but to tread his paths to be happy and a good man like him.

The farmer's own self-portrait is bounded on every side: by wife and children, by king and country (lightly), by God as the only landlord, and by an inherited farm and a father's example. He inherits; he is not self-made.

### crevecoeur.14 · Letter IX — slavery: "their master's interest; and to that god, all the laws of nature must give way"

- **Claims:** C6.5 (complicates), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter IX, "Description of Charles-Town; thoughts on slavery"; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 5400–5403
- **Checked:** text file

> the very instinct of the brute, so laudable, so irresistible, runs counter here to their master's interest; and to that god, all the laws of nature must give way. Thus planters get rich

Where interest goes unchecked, Crèvecoeur calls it an idol, as Winthrop did (winthrop.10). The South is the other America in the book, and Letter II's farmer owns slaves too ("My negroes", line 1241). So the "new man" of Letter III is a free white freeholder. Any claim about America's founding conditions has to say whose.

### crevecoeur.15 · Letter XII — "He cannot live in solitude, he must belong to some community"

- **Claims:** C6.11 (against a self-made reading), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter XII, "Distresses of a frontier man", ¶1; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 6456–6463 and 6472–6476
- **Checked:** text file

> of all animals that live on the surface of this planet, what is man when no longer connected with society; or when he finds himself surrounded by a convulsed and a half dissolved one? He cannot live in solitude, he must belong to some community bound by some ties, however imperfect. Men mutually support and add to the boldness and confidence of each other; the weakness of each is strengthened by the force of the whole. [...] or rather tired with feeling for the miseries of others, every one feels now for himself alone. When I consider myself as connected in all these characters, as bound by so many cords, all uniting in my heart

When the Revolution breaks his society apart, the "new man" finds he was bound all along: "bound by so many cords". "Every one feels now for himself alone" is, for him, what disaster looks like, not what freedom looks like.

### crevecoeur.16 · Letter XII — "one of the stones of a ruined arch"; flight to an Indian village

- **Claims:** C6.11 (complicates), C6.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Letter XII; `texts/crevecoeur-letters.txt` lines 6824–6831 and 7130–7134
- **Checked:** text file

> I resemble, methinks, one of the stones of a ruined arch, still retaining that pristine form that anciently fitted the place I occupied, but the centre is tumbled down; I can be nothing until I am replaced, either in the former circle, or in some stronger one. [...] and will endeavour that my wife, my children, and myself may be adopted soon after our arrival. Thus becoming truly inhabitants of their village, we shall immediately occupy that rank within the pale of their society, which will afford us all the amends we can possibly expect for the loss we have met with by the convulsions of our own.

The book ends without the self standing alone. The farmer "can be nothing" outside a circle, so he looks for adoption into another one: an Indian village, keeping the family, field labour and the Decalogue (lines 7144–7147, 7195–7202). Self-preservation is voiced ("Self-preservation, therefore, the rule of nature", lines 6642–6643), but it is the family's, and it ends in joining a community.

## Findings

1. **Winthrop is the communal counter-tradition C6.4 says was missing, and it is Protestant.** Inequality is God's order; each person is a member of "one body" bound by love; "the care of the publique must oversway all private respects"; the city on a hill is answerable together. C6.4 assumes the Protestant inheritance and communal traditions were opposites. In Massachusetts the strongest communal tradition *was* the Protestant (Reformed, covenantal) inheritance.
2. **But the New England community was made by consent, not inherited** (winthrop.06, .07), and it was already being left by 1642 (winthrop.11). It is not one of the "feudal, guild, parish and estate structures" of C6.2. It is a voluntary, covenanted check, which fits the Enlightenment pattern in `status.md` §2 (institutions joined by consent, the self still bounded).
3. **Crèvecoeur's "new man" is remade, not self-made.** Laws, land, government, labour and "the broad lap of our great Alma Mater" make him; "men are nothing of themselves". He stays bounded by God (thinly), family, inherited land, law and neighbours. Unbounded frontier men are his picture of degeneration.
4. **"SELF-INTEREST" is in Letter III** as the "basis of nature" for labour. It is a household interest inside law, and Crèvecoeur calls interest without check an idol (slavery, Letter IX).
5. **Religious indifference is real in Crèvecoeur, but it is a process** in the mixed middle colonies, explicitly not New England. The counter-tradition wore away; it did not arrive missing.
