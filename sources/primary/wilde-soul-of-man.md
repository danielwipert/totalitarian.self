# Wilde, "The Soul of Man under Socialism" (1891)

- **Edition used:** original English. The Gutenberg file (#1017) is transcribed from the 1909 Arthur L. Humphreys reprint (title page: *The Soul of Man*, "Second Impression"), not from the *Fortnightly Review* text of February 1891. Whether the two differ is not yet checked (see To do).
- **Text file:** `texts/wilde-soul-of-man.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): *Complete Works of Oscar Wilde* (Oxford), vol. IV
- **Citation form:** paragraph, plus this file's line
- **Paragraph numbering:** counted in this file from the first prose paragraph ("THE chief advantage", line 58 = ¶1) to the last ("Nor will man miss it", line 1269 = ¶55). Check against OET IV before final citation.
- **Claims to test:** C5.9, C5.12, C8.*

## Reading summary

**Frame.** Socialism is explicitly instrumental: it "will be of value simply because it will lead to Individualism" (¶7). Wilde then removes, one by one, every claim the §4 thinkers kept: God is optional ("if men do not desire that, it will develop none the less surely", ¶18), the past is "of no importance" (¶46), family and marriage go (¶22), public opinion is "of no value whatsoever" (¶20), duty and self-sacrifice are "cant" (¶48), and personality admits "no laws but its own laws" (¶18). The answerability frame is gone completely: no judge, no repentance, no public jury, not even an inner voice that is common to all (contrast Rousseau, C4.17, and Emerson's Over-Soul, C4.19). This is the closest any §5b text has come to C5.9's "answerable to nothing beyond itself."

**But relation is not cut.** Difference stays relational in four ways. (1) The perfected personality "will love them because they will be different" and helps all "by being what it is" (¶17). (2) Individualism brings a wider sympathy, "sympathy with a friend's success" (¶49); "man is naturally social" (¶52). (3) A new impersonal frame takes the Over-Soul's place: Evolution, "the differentiation to which all organisms grow" (¶48), and "harmony with himself and his environment" (¶55). (4) The artist's Self is defined *against* the public, which is a relation, if a negative one (¶31). Wilde drops answerability; he does not drop relation. That splits C5.9 in two.

**§8.** Wilde treats self-expression as the cure and conformity as imposed from outside: by authority that bribes (¶24), by property (¶14), by Public Opinion and the press, "the new authority" (¶36–38). The public's vocabulary of control is moral and medical ("immoral", "unhealthy", "morbid"), not the vocabulary of self-expression, so C8.2's distinction holds for Wilde as it did for Mill. Three pressure points: Wilde already sees value words turned to their "obverse" under authority, with conformity in dress called naturalness (¶49); his path from artist to "the few" to "the many" has no perversion step, and when taste spreads without feeling he calls it civilisation (¶39, ¶42, ¶43); and he makes public approval a sign that the artist "had [not] really been himself" (¶31), which opens a door to counter-conformity.

## Passages

### wilde-soul.01 · ¶2, ¶7–8 — "Socialism itself will be of value simply because it will lead to Individualism"

- **Claims:** C5.12 (complicates the pressure point: socialism is the means, not the frame), C5.9 (complicates), C4.20 (compare)
- **Location:** ¶2, ¶7–8; `texts/wilde-soul-of-man.txt` lines 66–70, 119–139
- **Checked:** text file

> [...] and so to realise the perfection of what was in him, to his own incomparable gain, and to the incomparable and lasting gain of the whole world.

> Upon the other hand, Socialism itself will be of value simply because it will lead to Individualism.
>
> Socialism, Communism, or whatever one chooses to call it, by converting private property into public wealth, and substituting co-operation for competition, will restore society to its proper condition of a thoroughly healthy organism, and insure the material well-being of each member of the community.  It will, in fact, give Life its proper basis and its proper environment.  But for the full development of Life to its highest mode of perfection, something more is needed.  What is needed is Individualism. [...] These are the poets, the philosophers, the men of science, the men of culture—in a word, the real men, the men who have realised themselves, and in whom all Humanity gains a partial realisation.

The C5.12 pressure point ("framed by socialism") is too generous: socialism has value "simply" as a means. What survives as a frame is "Humanity", which "gains a partial realisation" in each realised man, and "the gain of the whole world." That is a benefit-to-all frame like Mill's (C4.20), but it is now a by-product, not the justification.

### wilde-soul.02 · ¶17 — "It will love them because they will be different"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (against: difference stays relational), C5.6 (against), C4.15 (for, as a later check: the frame thins but holds)
- **Location:** ¶17; lines 337–348
- **Checked:** text file

> It will be a marvellous thing—the true personality of man—when we see it.  It will grow naturally and simply, flowerlike, or as a tree grows.  It will not be at discord.  It will never argue or dispute. [...] It will not be always meddling with others, or asking them to be like itself.  It will love them because they will be different.  And yet while it will not meddle with others, it will help all, as a beautiful thing helps us, by being what it is.

This is SPEC §2's relational difference almost word for word: difference is *between* persons who love and help one another. The relation has changed form (non-interference plus the gift of example, not answerability), but it is not severed.

### wilde-soul.03 · ¶18–19, ¶46 — "no laws but its own laws"; "Be thyself"; "the past is of no importance"

- **Claims:** C5.12 (for), C5.9 (for: own ground), C4.19 (for the contrast: God made optional), C5.7 (for: history repressed), C5.6 (for: the Self prior to its past)
- **Location:** ¶18–19, lines 350–361; ¶46, lines 1090–1093
- **Checked:** text file

> In its development it will be assisted by Christianity, if men desire that; but if men do not desire that, it will develop none the less surely.  For it will not worry itself about the past, nor care whether things happened or did not happen.  Nor will it admit any laws but its own laws; nor any authority but its own authority.  Yet it will love those who sought to intensify it, and speak often of them.  And of these Christ was one.
>
> ‘Know thyself’ was written over the portal of the antique world.  Over the portal of the new world, ‘Be thyself’ shall be written.  And the message of Christ to man was simply ‘Be thyself.’  That is the secret of Christ.

> But the past is of no importance.  The present is of no importance.  It is with the future that we have to deal.  For the past is what man should not have been.  The present is what man ought not to be.  The future is what artists are.

C5.12 holds as written. More important for §5b: Emerson moved God *inside* the Self; Wilde makes God optional ("if men do not desire that"), and Christ becomes one helper among others. The Self is its own law and indifferent to history. This is the strongest "own ground" text so far.

### wilde-soul.04 · ¶20 — "Man is complete in himself"; "Public opinion is of no value whatsoever"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (for), C5.12 (for), C4.17 (for the contrast: the court is abolished, not moved), C4.1 (for, as later evidence: the answerability structure dropped)
- **Location:** ¶20; lines 402–420
- **Checked:** text file

> He tells them to be themselves, and not to be always worrying about other things.  What do other things matter?  Man is complete in himself.  When they go into the world, the world will disagree with them.  That is inevitable.  The world hates Individualism. [...] The things people say of a man do not alter a man.  He is what he is.  Public opinion is of no value whatsoever. [...] And, above all things, they are not to interfere with other people or judge them in any way.  Personality is a very mysterious thing.  A man cannot always be estimated by what he does.  He may keep the law, and yet be worthless.  He may break the law, and yet be fine.  He may be bad, without ever doing anything bad.  He may commit a sin against society, and yet realise through that sin his true perfection.

Rousseau moved the court outward to the public, with God as nominal judge (C4.17). Wilde abolishes the court: no one is to judge, the person is not "estimated by what he does", and a sin can be the way to perfection. This is Locke's forensic person (C4.10) explicitly undone. With ¶21 (the adulteress forgiven "not because she repented, but because her love was so intense", lines 422–425), salvation gives way to self-realisation.

### wilde-soul.05 · ¶22–23 — "no claim whatsoever"; "There is no one type for man"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (for), C5.12 (for), C4.19 (compare: Emerson's "imitation is suicide" kept, the "general soul" dropped)
- **Location:** ¶22–23; lines 446–466
- **Checked:** text file

> When one of his followers asked leave to go and bury his father, ‘Let the dead bury the dead,’ was his terrible answer.  He would allow no claim whatsoever to be made on personality.
>
> And so he who would lead a Christlike life is he who is perfectly and absolutely himself. [...] It does not matter what he is, as long as he realises the perfection of the soul that is within him.  All imitation in morals and in life is wrong. [...] There is no one type for man.  There are as many perfections as there are imperfect men.  And while to the claims of charity a man may yield and yet be free, to the claims of conformity no man may yield and remain free at all.

The community's claim (family) is refused outright, where Emerson replaced it with a "common nature." "As many perfections as there are imperfect men" removes any common standard of perfection. Montaigne called "singular and particular fashions" folly (montaigne-essays.03); here the plural, unshared perfection is the ideal.

### wilde-soul.06 · ¶24 — authority "by bribing people to conform"; "other people’s second-hand clothes"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (for the contrast: Wilde's engine is external), C8.2 (for: conformity here wears no vocabulary of self-expression), C4.19 (compare: echo of "Whoso would be a man, must be a nonconformist")
- **Location:** ¶24; lines 482–492
- **Checked:** text file

> When it is used with a certain amount of kindness, and accompanied by prizes and rewards, it is dreadfully demoralising. People, in that case, are less conscious of the horrible pressure that is being put on them, and so go through their lives in a sort of coarse comfort, like petted animals, without ever realising that they are probably thinking other people’s thoughts, living by other people’s standards, wearing practically what one may call other people’s second-hand clothes, and never being themselves for a single moment.  ‘He who would be free,’ says a fine thinker, ‘must not conform.’  And authority, by bribing people to conform, produces a very gross kind of over-fed barbarism amongst us.

Wilde's account of conformity: unconscious, comfortable, worn like clothes. It is close to C8.1's "accessory", but the engine is authority's bribes, and the conformists do not think they are expressing themselves. They simply never are themselves. Wilde is a baseline for C8.2/C8.4, not a precursor.

### wilde-soul.07 · ¶28 — "A work of art is the unique result of a unique temperament"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (for), C5.3 (for: the Self as origin), C8.1 (complicates: corruption comes from attending to demand)
- **Location:** ¶28; lines 599–613
- **Checked:** text file

> A work of art is the unique result of a unique temperament.  Its beauty comes from the fact that the author is what he is.  It has nothing to do with the fact that other people want what they want.  Indeed, the moment that an artist takes notice of what other people want, and tries to supply the demand, he ceases to be an artist, and becomes a dull or an amusing craftsman, an honest or a dishonest tradesman. [...] Art is the most intense mode of Individualism that the world has known. [...] But alone, without any reference to his neighbours, without any interference, the artist can fashion a beautiful thing; and if he does not do it solely for his own pleasure, he is not an artist at all.

This is singularity in SPEC §2's sense, for the artist: "unique", "without any reference to his neighbours", the work flowing *from* the Self (C5.3). For C8.1: Wilde already sees expression turned into craft or trade once it looks to an audience. That is a fall from expression to supply, though, not to belonging.

### wilde-soul.08 · ¶31 — approval as a sign that the artist "had [not] really been himself"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (complicates: singularity defined against the public is still relational), C8.1 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates: distinction, not belonging)
- **Location:** ¶31; lines 725–733
- **Checked:** text file

> An artist is, of course, not disturbed by it.  The true artist is a man who believes absolutely in himself, because he is absolutely himself.  But I can fancy that if an artist produced a work of art in England that immediately on its appearance was recognised by the public, through their medium, which is the public press, as a work that was quite intelligible and highly moral, he would begin to seriously question whether in its creation he had really been himself at all, and consequently whether the work was not quite unworthy of him, and either of a thoroughly second-rate order, or of no artistic value whatsoever.

Half joke, but it gives the logic: the public's rejection certifies the Self. The singular Self still needs the public, as the thing it must not please. This is the mirror image of accessorizing (expression measured by others' reaction) and is closer to Rousseau's wish for distinction (C8.4) than to a wish to belong.

### wilde-soul.09 · ¶36–38 — "Public Opinion"; journalism "the new authority"; supply and demand

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates: Wilde already tells the "standard story", with media as engine, but grounds it in the public's own appetite), C8.2 (for: the public's vocabulary is moral, not self-expressive), C6.* (America: "Journalism governs for ever and ever"; tag if §6 needs it)
- **Location:** ¶36, lines 797–803; ¶37, lines 813–821; ¶38, lines 830–831, 839–842, 874–879
- **Checked:** text file

> It comes from the barbarous conception of authority.  It comes from the natural inability of a community corrupted by authority to understand or appreciate Individualism.  In a word, it comes from that monstrous and ignorant thing that is called Public Opinion, which, bad and well-meaning as it is when it tries to control action, is infamous and of evil meaning when it tries to control Thought or Art.

> They at once sought for the journalist, found him, developed him, and made him their industrious and well-paid servant. [...] But what is there behind the leading-article but prejudice, stupidity, cant, and twaddle?  And when these four are joined together they make a terrible force, and constitute the new authority.

> In America the President reigns for four years, and Journalism governs for ever and ever. [...] The fact is, that the public have an insatiable curiosity to know everything, except what is worth knowing.  Journalism, conscious of this, and having tradesman-like habits, supplies their demands. [...] [they] only do it because the unhealthy conditions under which their occupation is carried on oblige them to supply the public with what the public wants, and to compete with other journalists in making that supply as full and satisfying to the gross popular appetite as possible.

By 1891 the press is already the engine of conformity, and America the extreme case. For §8's drafting note this matters: the "standard story" (media from outside) is older than the culture industry. Wilde's engine is not purely external, though: the public "developed" the journalist and the press serves its appetite. What the public enforces is *its* standard ("immoral", "unhealthy"), not self-expression.

### wilde-soul.10 · ¶39, ¶42, ¶43 — "At first he appealed to the few: now he has educated the many"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (against as a necessary sequence: expression → few → many, with no perversion), C8.3 (complicates), C8.2 (complicates: diffusion without feeling counts as civilisation)
- **Location:** ¶39, lines 899–903; ¶42, lines 987–994; ¶43, lines 1010–1022
- **Checked:** text file

> His object was to realise his own perfection as an artist, under certain conditions, and in certain forms of Art.  At first he appealed to the few: now he has educated the many.  He has created in the public both taste and temperament.

> [...] made them for his own pleasure, and has never asked the public what they wanted, has never cared to know what they wanted, has never allowed the public to dictate to him or influence him in any way but has gone on intensifying his own personality, and producing his own individual work.  At first none came to him.  That did not matter.  Then the few came to him.  That did not change him.  The many have come now.  He is still the same.

> It is only fair to state, however, that the extraordinary success of the revolution in house-decoration and furniture and the like has not really been due to the majority of the public developing a very fine taste in such matters.  It has been chiefly due to the fact that the craftsmen of things so appreciated the pleasure of making what was beautiful, and woke to such a vivid consciousness of the hideousness and vulgarity of what the public had previously wanted, that they simply starved the public out. [...] The things are no longer made.  However they may object to it, people must nowadays have something charming in their surroundings.

C8.1's first three steps are here (genuine expression, a few connect, many follow), but Wilde's outcome is the opposite of perversion: the expresser "is still the same" and the many are "educated." In the decorative arts the public adopts the forms *without* the feeling ("not [...] a very fine taste"), which is C8.1's accessory stage, and Wilde counts it a gain ("People have been to a very great extent civilised", lines 1009–1010). C8.1 needs to say why convergence leads to perversion rather than education.

### wilde-soul.11 · ¶41 — the spectator "is to be receptive [...] the violin on which the master is to play"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (complicates: singularity is for the maker; the receiver must give up his views), C8.* (complicates: self-expression is not offered to everyone in every role)
- **Location:** ¶41; lines 923–931, 954–957
- **Checked:** text file

> If a man approaches a work of art with any desire to exercise authority over it and the artist, he approaches it in such a spirit that he cannot receive any artistic impression from it at all.  The work of art is to dominate the spectator: the spectator is not to dominate the work of art.  The spectator is to be receptive.  He is to be the violin on which the master is to play.  And the more completely he can suppress his own silly views, his own foolish prejudices, his own absurd ideas of what Art should be, or should not be, the more likely he is to understand and appreciate the work of art in question.

> He is not the arbiter of the work of art.  He is one who is admitted to contemplate the work of art, and, if the work be fine, to forget in its contemplation and the egotism that mars him—the egotism of his ignorance, or the egotism of his information.

Wilde's Self is not totalized in reception: the receiver must suppress himself and "forget [...] the egotism that mars him." Self-assertion is right in making and wrong in receiving. The audience that "exercises authority" over art (later, the self-expressing consumer) is exactly what Wilde condemns.

### wilde-soul.12 · ¶48 — no "cant about duty"; "the differentiation to which all organisms grow"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (complicates: a new shared frame, Evolution), C4.19 (compare: Over-Soul naturalized), C5.3 (for: "comes [...] out of man"), C8.5 (for, as an antecedent: rival vocabularies discredited)
- **Location:** ¶48; lines 1112–1129
- **Checked:** text file

> It is to be noted also that Individualism does not come to man with any sickly cant about duty, which merely means doing what other people want because they want it; or any hideous cant about self-sacrifice, which is merely a survival of savage mutilation.  In fact, it does not come to man with any claims upon him at all.  It comes naturally and inevitably out of man.  It is the point to which all development tends.  It is the differentiation to which all organisms grow.  It is the perfection that is inherent in every mode of life, and towards which every mode of life quickens. [...] Evolution is the law of life, and there is no evolution except towards Individualism.

Every claim is dropped ("not [...] any claims upon him at all") and the vocabularies of duty and self-sacrifice are discredited, which supports C8.5's "no other vocabulary" as a deliberate program. But difference is again grounded in something universal and impersonal: a "law of life" that every organism shares. Emerson's universal soul becomes Darwinian differentiation. The frame changes from spirit to nature; it is not abolished.

### wilde-soul.13 · ¶49 — "Affectation [...] consists in dressing according to the views of one’s neighbour"; selfishness redefined

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates: Wilde already sees conformity performed under the opposite value word), C5.9 (against: variety is enjoyed in common), C7.8 (against, for Wilde's ideal: individualism brings sympathy)
- **Location:** ¶49; lines 1131–1148, 1158–1162
- **Checked:** text file

> It has been pointed out that one of the results of the extraordinary tyranny of authority is that words are absolutely distorted from their proper and simple meaning, and are used to express the obverse of their right signification.  What is true about Art is true about Life.  A man is called affected, nowadays, if he dresses as he likes to dress.  But in doing that he is acting in a perfectly natural manner.  Affectation, in such matters, consists in dressing according to the views of one’s neighbour, whose views, as they are the views of the majority, will probably be extremely stupid. [...] Selfishness is not living as one wishes to live, it is asking others to live as one wishes to live.  And unselfishness is letting other people’s lives alone, not interfering with them.  Selfishness always aims at creating around it an absolute uniformity of type.  Unselfishness recognises infinite variety of type as a delightful thing, accepts it, acquiesces in it, enjoys it.

> [...] When man has realised Individualism, he will also realise sympathy and exercise it freely and spontaneously.

The most serious pressure point on C8.2's originality. Wilde describes a vocabulary used for "the obverse of [its] right signification," with conformity in dress passed off as the natural and the unselfish. What C8.2 adds is the *specific* word: in Wilde, conformity hides under "natural", "healthy" and "unselfish", never under "self-expression"; it is the individualist who is called "affected." C8.2 survives if it says this inversion is old and its newness is the word used. Separately, "infinite variety of type" enjoyed by all is a shared good, and sympathy is the promised result: against C7.8 as a consequence of Wilde's ideal.

### wilde-soul.14 · ¶52, ¶55 — "man is naturally social"; "harmony with himself and his environment"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (against), C5.6 (against: the Self *in* its environment)
- **Location:** ¶52, lines 1194–1201; ¶55, lines 1269–1282
- **Checked:** text file

> For it is through joy that the Individualism of the future will develop itself.  Christ made no attempt to reconstruct society, and consequently the Individualism that he preached to man could be realised only through pain or in solitude.  The ideals that we owe to Christ are the ideals of the man who abandons society entirely, or of the man who resists society absolutely.  But man is naturally social.  Even the Thebaid became peopled at last.  And though the cenobite realises his personality, it is often an impoverished personality that he so realises.

> When he can do so without exercising restraint on others, or suffering it ever, and his activities are all pleasurable to him, he will be saner, healthier, more civilised, more himself.  Pleasure is Nature’s test, her sign of approval.  When man is happy, he is in harmony with himself and his environment. [...] It will be complete, and through it each man will attain to his perfection.  The new Individualism is the new Hellenism.

Wilde's last word corrects his own Christ. Solitary individualism (abandoning or resisting society) is "impoverished" because "man is naturally social"; this is why socialism is needed at all. The goal is harmony with the environment, under "Nature's test", and modelled on a shared culture (Hellenism). Severance from relation is the condition Wilde wants to end, not the ideal.
