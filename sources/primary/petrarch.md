# Petrarch: letters (incl. "The Ascent of Mont Ventoux") and *Secretum*

- **Edition used:** letters: J. H. Robinson and H. W. Rolfe, *Petrarch: The First Modern Scholar and Man of Letters* (2nd ed., 1914), selections, often abridged by the editors; *Secretum*: trans. William H. Draper, *Petrarch's Secret, or the Soul's Conflict with Passion* (1911)
- **Text file:** `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` (clean), `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` (clean)
- **Citation form:** letters by collection and number (*Fam.* = *Familiares*, *Sen.* = *Seniles*) as Robinson's footnotes give them (Fracassetti's numbering; verify against Rossi's edition, which may differ). *Secretum* by book (Proem, I, II, III); Draper uses "Dialogue the First" etc.
- **Claims to test:** C4.23, C4.21, C4.8, C1.1, C4.13

**Note on the editors.** Robinson's headnotes are secondary and partisan in both directions: before the Ventoux letter he says that "the relapse into mediæval sentiment was speedy and complete" (line 7513), and before the *Secretum* excerpts he calls it "the earliest unmistakable example of cool, fair, honest and comprehensive self-analysis that we possess" (lines 10042–10044). Neither is used as evidence below.

**Note on Ventoux wording.** The *Confessions* X sentence in this translation does not say men "pass themselves by". It reads "but themselves they consider not" (line 7744). Quote that.

## Summary of findings (2026-10-23)

Petrarch does turn inward, and he says so in plain words: on the summit "I turned my inward eye upon myself" (petrarch-letters.02). But in every text read the inward turn is **Augustine's**, used *against* the world and *against* Petrarch's own love of fame. It is the soul as noble because God-given and fallen by its own will (petrarch-letters.03), the self as a sinner to be known (.04), and in the *Secretum* the self is the object of study only as a defendant before Truth as "the silent Judge" (petrarch-secret.01). Answerability on one's own (C1.1) is fully present: no one is unhappy "except through his own fault" (petrarch-secret.02).

The rival story has real support, but it is in **Franciscus's resistance**, not in the doctrine anyone wins with. He loves glory and the escape from "the vulgar crowd" (petrarch-secret.05), he says outright "Such glory as belongs to man is enough for me" and defends worldly glory first and eternity after as "the true order" (petrarch-secret.06), and he praises "a style of his own" (petrarch-letters.07) and striving "to become greater, even the greatest" (petrarch-letters.08). Every one of these is bounded at once, usually by himself (secret.06 is retracted within a sentence; letters.08 ends "praying and in tears"). Augustine wins every argument, and Franciscus still walks off to his books: "I have not strength to resist that old bent for study altogether" (petrarch-secret.09). That ending is the key result. The self's desire for fame and work is not *valued as a principle*. It is *confessed as a weakness* that the speaker will not give up.

Difference is noticed early ("infinite diversities", petrarch-letters.06) but put down to God ("our natures are the gift [...] of our Eternal Father"). The judge he appeals to for fame is posterity (petrarch-letters.05), which is a new kind of court, not the absence of one.

## Passages

### petrarch-letters.01 · *Fam.* iv.1 "The Ascent of Mount Ventoux" — "My only motive was the wish to see"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Fam.* iv.1, to Dionisio da Borgo San Sepolcro, Malaucène, April 26 [1336]; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 7525–7527
- **Checked:** text file

> To-day I made the ascent of the highest mountain in this region, which is not improperly called Ventosum. My only motive was the wish to see what so great an elevation had to offer.

The letter starts from curiosity about the world, sparked by Livy (lines 7532–7538). The inward turn comes as a correction of this wish. It is not where Petrarch started.

### petrarch-letters.02 · *Fam.* iv.1 — "but themselves they consider not"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (for), C4.8 (for), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** *Fam.* iv.1; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 7738–7752
- **Checked:** text file

> Now it chanced that the tenth book presented itself. [...] where I first fixed my eyes it was written: "And men go about to wonder at the heights of the mountains, and the mighty waves of the sea, and the wide sweep of rivers, and the circuit of the ocean, and the revolution of the stars, but themselves they consider not." I was abashed, and [...] I closed the book, angry with myself that I should still be admiring earthly things who might long ago have learned from even the pagan philosophers that nothing is wonderful but the soul, which, when great itself, finds nothing great outside itself. Then, in truth, I was satisfied that I had seen enough of the mountain; I turned my inward eye upon myself

This is the inward turn of C4.23, stated in plain words. But its source is a sentence of Augustine, met by a conversion-style chance opening of the book (Petrarch compares it to Augustine's and Anthony's, lines 7752–7771), plus the "pagan philosophers" (Seneca). The object is "the soul", not the individual. That supports C4.8 (Augustinian and classical roots) and goes against reading this as a new valuing of individuality.

### petrarch-letters.03 · *Fam.* iv.1 — "what is to be found only within"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (for), C4.21 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** *Fam.* iv.1; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 7771–7777, 7643–7646
- **Checked:** text file

> I thought in silence of the lack of good counsel in us mortals, who neglect what is noblest in ourselves, scatter our energies in all directions, and waste ourselves in a vain show, because we look about us for what is to be found only within. I wondered at the natural nobility of our soul, save when it debases itself of its own free will, and deserts its original estate, turning what God has given it for its honour into dishonour.

The "nobility" is in "our soul", common to all mortals, and it is "what God has given it". The fall is "of its own free will", which makes the soul answerable (C1.1). Earlier the climb itself is an allegory of "the journey toward the blessed life" (line 7623), and the goal is for "the swift immortal soul" to "reach its goal" (lines 7643–7646). Inward, yes. Self-valuing, no.

### petrarch-letters.04 · "Letter to Posterity" — "we shall learn to know ourselves"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates), C4.13 (for)
- **Location:** *Epistola ad posteros* (late, unfinished); `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 1443–1457, 1504–1506
- **Checked:** text file

> I was, in truth, a poor mortal like yourself, neither very exalted in my origin, nor, on the other hand, of the most humble birth [...] a riper age brought me to my senses and taught me by experience the truth I had long before read in books, that youth and pleasure are vanity--nay, that the Author of all ages and times permits us miserable mortals, puffed up with emptiness, thus to wander about, until finally, coming to a tardy consciousness of our sins, we shall learn to know ourselves.

> I have taken pride in others, never in myself, and however insignificant I may have been, I have always been still less important in my own judgment.

The act points to C4.21: a man writes his own life, down to the hour of his birth (line 1471), for readers yet unborn. The content points the other way. He is a "poor mortal like yourself" (the Latin motto, line 1429, says "Vestro de grege unus fui": I was one of your flock; our translation), and to know oneself is to come to "consciousness of our sins". Equal standing, not distinction (C4.13).

### petrarch-letters.05 · *Fam.* i.1 (per Robinson) to Tommaso di Messina — "appeal [...] to posterity"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** "On the Impossibility of Acquiring Fame during one's Lifetime", Bologna, April 18 [c. 1326; Robinson's date]; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 9894–9898, 9974–9982
- **Checked:** text file

> Let the present age harbour any opinion it will of us. If it be just, let us receive it with equanimity; if unjust, we must appeal to unprejudiced judges,--to posterity, seeing that a fair-minded verdict can be obtained nowhere else.

> But what is it, after all, that we are so anxiously striving for? The fame we reach after is but a breath, a mist, a shadow, a nothing. A sharp and penetrating mind will therefore easily learn to scorn it. But if, perchance,--since it is a pest which commonly pursues the generous soul,--thou canst not radically extirpate this longing, thou mayest at least check its growth with the sickle of reason. [...] seek virtue while thou livest, and thou shalt find fame in thy grave.

The earliest letter shows both sides already. The writer is answerable to a human court, "posterity", and not only to God: that is new, and it partly supports C4.21. But fame is called "a nothing" and "a pest", to be kept down "with the sickle of reason" and put below virtue. The self is still judged, only by a different court.

### petrarch-letters.06 · *Fam.* x.5 to his brother Gherardo — "the infinite diversities which distinguish mortals"

- **Claims:** C4.13 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Fam.* x.5, "On a Religious Life", "In Solitude", June 11 [1352]; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 9663–9670, 9683–9689
- **Checked:** text file

> We are sprung from the same womb; but how unlike, how unequal! This serves to show us that our natures are the gift, not of our earthly parents, but of our Eternal Father. [...] from God we receive our soul, our life, our intellect, our desire for good, our free will.

> I have looked at the race as a whole, and have examined individuals in detail. What can, in truth, be said that will apply to all; or who can possibly enumerate the infinite diversities which distinguish mortals from one another, so that men do not seem to belong to the same species or even to the same genus?

Petrarch notices individual difference strongly, in the 14th century, long before the "bridge" of C4.15. But he does not value it. The difference here is one of *rank* (his monk brother is better), it is given by God, and it serves a letter in praise of the monastic life.

### petrarch-letters.07 · *Fam.* xxiii.19 to Boccaccio — "a style of his own"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Fam.* xxiii.19, "The Young Humanist of Ravenna"; `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 7111–7116, 7148–7153, 7095–7097
- **Checked:** text file

> I am confident that he will develop vigour of thought and expression, and work out, as the result of his experiments, a style of his own, and learn to avoid imitation, or, better, to conceal it, so as to give the impression not of copying but rather of bringing to Italy from the writers of old something new.

> we must write just as the bees make honey, not keeping the flowers but turning them into a sweetness of our own, blending many very different flavours into one, which shall be unlike them all, and better.

The clearest support for C4.21 in these letters: being "unlike them all, and better" is a value, and a writer should have "a style of his own". It is limited to style, and the doctrine is Seneca's. In the same letter Petrarch praises the boy because "all his study is to obey, to follow not his own inclinations but my will" (lines 7096–7097).

### petrarch-letters.08 · *Sen.* xvi.2 to Boccaccio — "to become greater, even the greatest"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.27 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Sen.* xvi.2, Padua, April 28, 1373 (first half omitted by Robinson); `texts/petrarch-letters-robinson.txt` lines 11160–11162, 11241–11245, 11257–11262
- **Checked:** text file

> who am convinced that I have rivalled no one, except, perhaps, the common herd, and rather than be like it I should choose to remain entirely unknown.

> I must still endeavour, if I am a nullity, to become something; if already of some account, to become a little more worthy; and if I were really great, which I am not, I should strive, so far as in me lay, to become greater, even the greatest.

> I seem to myself to have but begun; whatever you and others may think, this is my verdict. [...] I desire that death find me reading and writing, or, if it please Christ, praying and in tears.

In the last year of his life Petrarch values striving for excellence, refuses to be "like" the "common herd", and sets his own "verdict" above his friends'. This is the self-shaping individual of C4.21, in Petrarch's own voice. But the last clause still puts Christ above the work, and the striving is stated as a duty, not as a claim to be original.

### petrarch-secret.01 · *Secretum*, Proem — "Truth listened as the silent Judge"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (for), C4.23 (complicates)
- **Location:** Proem; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 555–558, 565–570
- **Checked:** text file

> Then while Truth listened as the silent Judge, none other beside her being present, we held long converse on one side and the other; and because of the greatness of the theme, the discourse between us lasted over three days.

> not that I wish to class it with my other works, or desire from it any credit. My thoughts aim higher. What I desire is that I may be able by reading to renew as often as I wish the pleasure I felt from the discourse itself. So, little Book, I bid you flee the haunts of men and be content to stay with me, true to the title I have given you of "My Secret"

The whole self-examination is set as a trial, with Truth as judge (again at line 910: "Truth, who is our Judge"). The book is private and not written for glory. Inwardness here means being questioned, not being valued.

### petrarch-secret.02 · *Secretum* I — "no one can become or be unhappy except through his own fault"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (for), C4.1 (for)
- **Location:** Book I; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 762–764, 843–846
- **Checked:** text file

> _S. Augustine_. Since we are agreed on this, that no one can become or be unhappy except through his own fault, what need of more words is there?

> _Petrarch._ I perceive that by degrees I am getting away from my proposition and am being compelled to acknowledge that the beginning of my misery did arise from my own will. I feel it is true in myself, and I conjecture the same to be true of others.

This is answerability on one's own, before the Reformation, in a Stoic and Augustinian form: the self is the cause of its own misery. It is also an early form of C7.4 (blame comes from making the self the cause), and it is bounded by Truth as judge. Note "the same to be true of others": the rule is general.

### petrarch-secret.03 · *Secretum* I — "the story of my own self"

- **Claims:** C4.8 (for), C4.23 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book I; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 970–974
- **Checked:** text file

> So that as often as I read the book of your _Confessions_, and am made partaker of your conflict between two contrary emotions, between hope and fear, (and weep as I read), I seem to be hearing the story of my own self, the story not of another's wandering, but of my own.

Petrarch finds his own self by reading Augustine's. The self he turns to is shaped like the *Confessions*: a divided will before God. This is direct evidence for C4.8's Augustinian groundwork.

### petrarch-secret.04 · *Secretum* II — "and then be ignorant of yourself?"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (for), C4.8 (for), C1.1 (for), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** Book II; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 1755–1758, 1768–1769
- **Checked:** text file

> Suppose you shall have learned all the circuits of the heavens and the earth, the spaces of the sea, the courses of the stars, the virtues of herbs and stones, the secrets of nature, and then be ignorant of yourself? Of what profit is it?

> what boots it that others shall approve what you have said if in the court of your own conscience it stands condemned?

C4.23's "the self, not the world" in so many words, but spoken by Augustine, in a speech against Petrarch's pride in intellect, eloquence and looks (lines 1739–1742). The self is to be studied so that it can be judged in "the court of your own conscience", not so that it can be admired.

### petrarch-secret.05 · *Secretum* III — "Love and glory" and "escape the vulgar crowd"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for, as Franciscus's position; refuted in the text)
- **Location:** Book III; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` line 3329; lines 3654–3663
- **Checked:** text file

> _S. Augustine._ Love and glory.

> It is no wonder, then, if such a glory as hers should have fostered in my heart the longing for more conspicuous glory, and should have sweetened those hard toils which I had to endure if I would attain that which I desired. [...] now you bid me forget or diminish somewhat of my love for her who first taught me how to escape the vulgar crowd, who guided all my steps, spurred on my lagging mind, and wakened into life my drowsy spirit.

Here is the rival self: love as the force that shaped him, glory as the goal, and distinction from the crowd as a good. Augustine calls these the two "chains" (lines 3292–3329) and answers that she "has prevented you being any more than you are" (lines 3680–3681). The view exists, but only as the defendant's case.

### petrarch-secret.06 · *Secretum* III — "Such glory as belongs to man is enough for me"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for, then complicates), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book III; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 4924–4941, 5021–5030
- **Checked:** text file

> I do not think to become as God, or to inhabit eternity, or embrace, heaven and earth. Such glory as belongs to man is enough for me. That is all I sigh after. Mortal myself, it is but mortal blessings I desire.

> _S. Augustine._ Oh, if that is what you truly mean, how wretched are you! [...] _Petrarch._ Heaven defend me from such folly! But my conscience is witness, and knows what have been my desires, that never have I ceased to love with burning zeal the things eternal. I said--or if, perchance, I am mistaken, I intended to say--that my wish was to use mortal things for what they were worth [...] and so to follow after human fame as knowing that both myself and it will perish.

> it is in the true order that mortal men should first care for mortal things; and that to things transitory things eternal should succeed

This is the strongest sentence for C4.21 in Petrarch: human glory claimed as enough. It is withdrawn at once ("Heaven defend me from such folly!") and turned into a plea for an *order*: earth first, heaven after. That is a bounded worldliness inside a Christian frame, not a self that answers to nothing.

### petrarch-secret.07 · *Secretum* III — "You write books on others, but yourself you quite forget"

- **Claims:** C4.23 (complicates), C4.21 (against)
- **Location:** Book III; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 4867–4868, 5213–5216
- **Checked:** text file

> You write books on others, but yourself you quite forget.

> For you are spending all your efforts on things that concern others, and neglecting those that are your own; and so, through this vain hope of glory, the time, so precious, though you know it not, is passing away.

In the *Secretum* the self and fame are opposites. To care for "yourself" means caring for the soul in the face of death; to seek glory (the *Africa*, the Roman history) means neglecting it. So C4.23's inward turn is the turn *away from* C4.21's self of excellence and fame, not the same movement.

### petrarch-secret.08 · *Secretum* III — "glory is in a sense the shadow of virtue"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.27 (for)
- **Location:** Book III; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 5181–5192
- **Checked:** text file

> _S. Augustine._ I will never advise you to live without ambition; but I would always urge you to put virtue before glory. You know that glory is in a sense the shadow of virtue. And therefore, just as it is impossible that your body should not cast a shadow if the sun is shining, so it is impossible also in the light of God Himself that virtues should exist and not make their glory to appear. [...] follow after virtue and let glory take care of itself

Even Petrarch's Augustine allows ambition and glory, as long as they follow virtue "in the light of God Himself". The humanist value is let in, but kept inside a Christian and Ciceronian frame, as C4.27 predicts for Erasmus.

### petrarch-secret.09 · *Secretum* III, ending — "I have not strength to resist that old bent for study"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Book III, last exchange; `texts/petrarch-secret.txt` lines 5395–5401, 5412–5423
- **Checked:** text file

> _S. Augustine._ You may count your prayer already granted, if you will only to yourself be true: for how shall any one be constant to him who is inconstant to himself? _Petrarch._ I will be true to myself, so far as in me lies.

> _Petrarch._ I confess they are so. And I now return to attend to those other concerns only in order that, when they are discharged, I may come back to these. I am not ignorant that, as you said a few minutes before, it would be much safer for me to attend only to the care of my soul, to relinquish altogether every bypath and follow the straight path of the way of salvation. But I have not strength to resist that old bent for study altogether. _S. Augustine_. We are falling into our old controversy. Want of will you call want of power. Well, so it must be, if it cannot be otherwise.

Who wins? Augustine wins the argument: Petrarch confesses it, and "be true to myself" means be constant to the soul's good. The self's desire wins the life: he goes back to his books. The desire is not defended as a right. It is admitted as a weakness under judgment, and that is an answerable self that disobeys, not a self that values itself.
