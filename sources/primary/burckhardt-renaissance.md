# Burckhardt, *The Civilisation of the Renaissance in Italy* (1860), with Pico's *Oration* as Burckhardt quotes it

- **Edition used:** trans. S. G. C. Middlemore (Gutenberg #2074). Burckhardt's German, in English.
- **Text file:** `texts/burckhardt-renaissance.txt`
- **Claims to test:** C4.21, C4.22, C4.24; also bears on C1.1, C4.1, C4.13, C4.15, C4.26, C4.30, §7
- **Status of the evidence:** Burckhardt is a *secondary* source, a historian's thesis (C4.22 is textual-secondary). His claims about Renaissance individuals are tested here as what Burckhardt says, not as what the Renaissance was (see the C4.25/C4.26 pressure point).
- **Pico:** Burckhardt gives Pico's key passage twice. In the body (Part IV ch. viii, lines 10063–10080) Middlemore translates *Burckhardt's German paraphrase* of Pico, which is abridged. In footnote 805 (lines 21099–21126) Burckhardt prints *Pico's Latin* in full for that passage, including the ending the English omits. The Latin footnote is the nearest thing to the primary text we have; `texts/README.md` says no public-domain English Pico exists. Passages tagged `burckhardt-pico` are Pico via Burckhardt. The Latin glosses below are mine and sit outside quotation marks.

## Passages

### burckhardt.01 · Part II ch. i — "man became a spiritual individual"

- **Claims:** C4.22 (for), C4.21 (for), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II, ch. i "The Italian State and the Individual"; `texts/burckhardt-renaissance.txt` lines 4344–4357
- **Checked:** text file

> In the Middle Ages both sides of human consciousness--that which was turned within as that which was turned without--lay dreaming or half awake beneath a common veil. The veil was woven of faith, illusion, and childish prepossession, through which the world and history were seen clad in strange hues. Man was conscious of himself only as member of a race, people, party, family, or corporation--only through some general category. In Italy this veil first melted into air; an _objective_ treatment and consideration of the state and of all the things of this world became possible. The _subjective_ side at the same time asserted itself with corresponding emphasis; man became a spiritual _individual_,[289] and recognised himself as such. [...] It will not be difficult to show that this result was owing above all to the political circumstances of Italy.

The phrase is verified, but it sits at the *opening* of Part II ch. i (line 4352), not the end. The claim is exactly the rival story: a self that knows itself apart from "some general category", in Italy, before the Reformation. Burckhardt's cause is political (the states), not religious; note that "faith" is part of the veil.

### burckhardt.02 · Part II ch. i and notes 289–290 — "not afraid of singularity"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.13 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II ch. i, lines 4372–4380; notes 289–290, lines 17960–17965
- **Checked:** text file

> But at the close of the thirteenth century Italy began to swarm with individuality; the charm laid upon human personality was dissolved; and a thousand figures meet us each in its own special shape and dress. [...] The Italians of the fourteenth century knew little of false modesty or of hypocrisy in any shape; not one of them was afraid of singularity, of being and seeming[290] unlike his neighbours.

> [289] Observe the expressions ‘uomo singolare’ and ‘uomo unico’ for the higher and highest stages of individual development.

> [290] By the year 1390 there was no longer any prevailing fashion of dress for men at Florence, each preferring to clothe himself in his own way.

On Burckhardt's account *difference* (being unlike one's neighbours) is valued in 14th-century Italy, two centuries before the Rousseau–Emerson bridge, and outside the answerability line. That cuts against "answerability first, difference later" (C4.13, C4.15). Two caveats. Burckhardt's "singularity" means distinctness, not SPEC's difference cut from relation. And the evidence offered at this point is thin: men's dress in 1390 and, in n. 291, a remark of Montaigne's.

### burckhardt.03 · Part II ch. i — the conditions, and the lack of documents

- **Claims:** C4.22 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II ch. i, lines 4400–4414
- **Checked:** text file

> Wealth and culture, so far as display and rivalry were not forbidden to them, a municipal freedom which did not cease to be considerable, and a Church which, unlike that of the Byzantine or of the Mohammedan world, was not identical with the State--all these conditions undoubtedly favoured the growth of individual thought, for which the necessary leisure was furnished by the cessation of party conflicts. The private man, indifferent to politics, and busied partly with serious pursuits, partly with the interests of a _dilettante_, seems to have been first fully formed in these despotisms of the fourteenth century. Documentary evidence cannot, of course, be required on such a point.

Burckhardt himself concedes the central claim rests on no documents but on an impression drawn from biography, novels and art. The Church still figures, as a condition (being separate from the State) rather than as a frame the individual leaves.

### burckhardt.04 · Part II ch. ii, note 301 — "to shape the spiritual life for oneself"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.24 (for)
- **Location:** Part II ch. ii, note 301, lines 18047–18051
- **Checked:** text file

> [301] This awakening of personality is also shown in the great stress laid on the independent growth of character, in the claim to shape the spiritual life for oneself, apart from parents and ancestors. Boccaccio (_De Cas. Vir. Ill._ Paris, s. a. fol. xxix. _b_) points out that Socrates came of uneducated, Euripides and Demosthenes of unknown, parents, and exclaims: ‘Quasi animos a gignentibus habeamus!’

The strongest "self-shaping" statement in Part II, and it is about independence from *lineage* (Boccaccio: as if we had our souls from our parents!), not from God. Self-shaping against birth and family, the same point Pico makes about fixed natures.

### burckhardt.05 · Part II ch. ii — Alberti: "Men can do all things if they will"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.27 (for, as Burckhardt reports it)
- **Location:** Part II ch. ii "The Perfecting of the Individual", lines 4570–4573 and 4580–4591
- **Checked:** text file

> himself a disciple of Greek science, he maintained the doctrine, that without Christianity the world would wander in a labyrinth of error. [...] But the deepest spring of his nature has yet to be spoken of--the sympathetic intensity with which he entered into the whole life around him. At the sight of noble trees and waving corn-fields he shed tears [...] It need not be added that an iron will pervaded and sustained his whole personality; like all the great men of the Renaissance, he said, ‘Men can do all things if they will.’

Burckhardt's model "all-sided man" has the self-made will of the rival story, but also Christianity as the condition of truth and a "deepest spring" that is sympathy with the life around him. Burckhardt's own exemplar is related and framed, not separated.

### burckhardt.06 · Part II ch. iii — fame: personal distinction, and its demonic form

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.25 (complicates), §7 (for: the dark side)
- **Location:** Part II ch. iii "The Modern Idea of Fame", lines 4798–4801 and 4838–4853
- **Checked:** text file

> showing no traces of the idea of fame, that is, of distinction won by a man’s personal efforts.

> Amid all these preparations outwardly to win and secure fame, the curtain is now and then drawn aside, and we see with frightful evidence a boundless ambition and thirst after greatness, independent of all means and consequences. [...] This motive is not a mere extreme case of ordinary vanity, but something demonic, involving a surrender of the will, the use of any means, however atrocious, and even an indifference to success itself.

The individual is valued for his own excellence (C4.21's wording fits), but fame depends on others: on the poet-scholars who give it and on posterity. A self made for others' eyes, close to C4.25. The "demonic" form, ambition with no limit of means, is a Renaissance case of the unbounded self that the current story treats as late.

### burckhardt.07 · Part II ch. iv — ridicule as the corrective of the developed individual

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II ch. iv "Modern Wit and Satire", lines 4878–4889
- **Checked:** text file

> The corrective, not only of this modern desire for fame, but of all highly developed individuality, is found in ridicule, especially when expressed in the victorious form of wit.[357] [...] But wit could not be an independent element in life till its appropriate victim, the developed individual with personal pretentions, had appeared.

Burckhardt's one structural *limit* on the individual inside Part II is social: the mockery of other individuals. Not God or community, but a check from others.

### burckhardt.08 · Part VI ch. i — honour, "Fay ce que vouldras", and the 18th century

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C1.1 (complicates), C4.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. i "Morality", lines 11924–11932 and 11965–11986
- **Checked:** text file

> The highly gifted men of that day thought to find it in the sentiment of honour. This is that enigmatic mixture of conscience and egoism which often survives in the modern man after he has lost, whether by his own fault or not, faith, love, and hope. This sense of honour is compatible with much selfishness and great vices, and may be the victim of astonishing illusions

> ‘En leur reigle n’estoit que ceste clause: Fay ce que vouldras. Parce que gens liberes, bien nayz,[988] bien instruictz, conversans en compaignies honnestes, ont par nature ung instinct et aguillon qui toujours les poulse à faitz vertueux, et retire de vice; lequel ilz nommoyent honneur.’ This is that same faith in the goodness of human nature which inspired the men of the second half of the eighteenth century, and helped to prepare the way for the French Revolution. [...] If the boundless development of individuality, stronger than the will of the individual, be the work of a historical providence, not less so is the opposing force which then manifested itself in Italy.

Two findings. Burckhardt draws the rival story's own line from the Renaissance to the late Enlightenment (faith in the goodness of human nature). And the limit he names is internal, not God: honour, "conscience and egoism" mixed, which survives the loss of faith. Rabelais's own wording keeps a social condition, though: good birth, good education, "compaignies honnestes".

### burckhardt.09 · Part VI ch. i — "excessive individualism" and "his own sovereignty"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C4.1 (complicates), C4.15 (complicates), §7 (for)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. i, lines 12358–12361 and 12571–12593
- **Checked:** text file

> The restraints of which men were conscious were but few. Each individual, even among the lowest of the people, felt himself inwardly emancipated from the control of the State and its police, whose title to respect was illegitimate, and itself founded on violence

> The fundamental vice of this character was at the same time a condition of its greatness, namely, excessive individualism. The individual first inwardly casts off the authority of a state which, as a fact, is in most cases tyrannical and illegitimate [...] In face of all objective facts, of laws and restraints of whatever kind, he retains the feeling of his own sovereignty, and in each single instance forms his decision independently, according as honour or interest, passion or calculation, revenge or renunciation, gain the upper hand in his own mind. If therefore egoism in its wider as well as narrower sense is the root and fountain of all evil, the more highly developed Italian was for this reason more inclined to wickedness than the member of other nations of that time.

Burckhardt's "unbridled" individual: self-sovereign against every law and restraint, around 1500. On this account the limit-dropping self is at the *start*, not the end of the story (against the shape of C4.1). But what is cast off is an illegitimate *state*; Burckhardt says nothing here of casting off God or family, and he calls the result a vice.

### burckhardt.10 · Part VI ch. i — individualism as the source of moral responsibility

- **Claims:** C1.1 (against), C4.21 (for), C4.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. i, lines 12595–12608
- **Checked:** text file

> But this individual development did not come upon him through any fault of his own, but rather through an historical necessity. It did not come upon him alone, but also, and chiefly by means of Italian culture, upon the other nations of Europe, and has constituted since then the higher atmosphere which they breathe. In itself it is neither good nor bad, but necessary; within it has grown up a modern standard of good and evil--a sense of moral responsibility--which is essentially different from that which was familiar to the Middle Ages.

Burckhardt reverses the current story's order: "moral responsibility" (answerability) grows *within* individual development, which reached the rest of Europe through Italian culture. This is a historian's assertion, not shown in the text, but it is the rival story in Burckhardt's own words.

### burckhardt.11 · Part VI ch. iii — religion "altogether subjective"; "conscious of no sin"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C1.1 (against), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. iii "Religion and the Spirit of the Renaissance", lines 13573–13581 and 13601–13617
- **Checked:** text file

> These modern men, the representatives of the culture of Italy, were born with the same religious instincts as other mediæval Europeans. But their powerful individuality made them in religion, as in other matters, altogether subjective, and the intense charm which the discovery of the inner and outer universe exercised upon them rendered them markedly worldly. In the rest of Europe religion remained, till a much later period, something given from without

> Distinguishing keenly between good and evil, they yet are conscious of no sin. Every disturbance of their inward harmony they feel themselves able to make good out of the plastic resources of their own nature, and therefore they feel no repentance. The need of salvation thus becomes felt more and more dimly [...] And closer investigation often reveals to us that underneath this outward shell much genuine religion could still survive.

The sharpest contrast with the Reformation root. Burckhardt's Renaissance self is *not* answerable: it repairs itself from "the plastic resources of their own nature" and needs no salvation. That is a different self from Luther's, not an earlier version of it, so if Burckhardt is right there are two roots, one of them self-sufficient. He hedges at once: genuine religion survives "underneath".

### burckhardt.12 · Part VI ch. iii — religion "an affair of the individual", beside the German reformers

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for: parallel strand), C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. iii, lines 13624–13637
- **Checked:** text file

> That religion should again become an affair of the individual and of his own personal feeling was inevitable when the Church became corrupt in doctrine and tyrannous in practice, and is a proof that the European mind was still alive. [...] While the mystical and ascetical sects of the North lost no time in creating new outward forms for their new modes of thought and feeling, each individual in Italy went his own way, and thousands wandered on the sea of life without any religious guidance whatever. All the more must we admire those who attained and held fast to a personal religion. [...] nor would it be reasonable to expect that they should all of them go through that mighty spiritual labour which was appointed to the German reformers.

Burckhardt treats Italy and the Reformation as two answers to one crisis: the North built new forms, Italy went without. That supports C4.21's "parallel strand", but the Italian individual's religion is personal rather than absent, and for "thousands" the result is drift, not self-shaping.

### burckhardt.13 · Part VI ch. v — "to repent of nothing": "the consciousness of individual force"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (for), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. v "General Spirit of Doubt", lines 15110–15132
- **Checked:** text file

> The notion of sin and of salvation must have almost entirely evaporated. [...] When the powerful natures of the Renaissance tell us that their principle is to repent of nothing,[1286] they may have in their minds only matters that are morally indifferent, faults of unreason or imprudence; but in the nature of the case this contempt for repentance must extend to the sphere of morals, because its origin, namely the consciousness of individual force, is common to both sides of human nature. The passive and contemplative form of Christianity, with its constant reference to a higher world beyond the grave, could no longer control these men.

Repeats burckhardt.11 with a cause: "the consciousness of individual force". The frame Burckhardt says was lost is the *passive, otherworldly* form of Christianity, not God as such (see .14). Limited to "the powerful natures" and "the individually developed classes", an elite.

### burckhardt.14 · Part VI ch. v — the better minds: theism, God "its eternal mover"

- **Claims:** C4.21 (complicates), C4.15 (for: frame kept), C4.24 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part VI ch. v, the end of the work, lines 15184–15206
- **Checked:** text file

> It is true that Lorenzo, from his youth till he died, expressed himself dogmatically as a Christian,[1291] and that Pico was drawn by Savonarola’s influence to accept the point of view of a monkish ascetic.[1292] [...] the doctrine is upheld that the visible world was created by God in love, that it is the copy of a pattern pre-existing in Him, and that He will ever remain its eternal mover and restorer. The soul of man can by recognising God draw Him into its narrow boundaries, but also by love to Him itself expand into the Infinite--and this is blessedness on earth. [...] One of the most precious fruits of the knowledge of the world and of man here comes to maturity, on whose account alone the Italian Renaissance must be called the leader of modern ages.

Burckhardt ends the whole book with the Renaissance self at its best *inside* God: the soul expands "into the Infinite" by love of God, much like Emerson's Over-Soul. The individual he praises most is framed. The unbounded one (.09, .13) he treats as the vice.

### burckhardt.15 · Part IV ch. vii — autobiography: Cellini "carries his measure in himself"

- **Claims:** C4.26 (for, as Burckhardt reads Cellini), C4.21 (for), C1.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. vii, lines 9505–9510 and 9536–9550; Cardano, 9579–9581
- **Checked:** text file

> Even autobiography takes here and there in Italy a bold and vigorous flight, and puts before us, together with the most varied incidents of external life, striking revelations of the inner man. Among other nations, even in Germany at the time of the Reformation, it deals only with outward experiences, and leaves us to guess at the spirit within from the style of the narrative.

> Nor is the autobiography of Benvenuto Cellini, any more than that of Pius II., founded on introspection. [...] He is a man who can do all and dares do all, and who carries his measure in himself.[757] Whether we like him or not, he lives, such as he was, as a significant type of the modern spirit.

> He has still left him a grandson, immense learning, the fame of his works, money, rank and credit, powerful friends, the knowledge of many secrets, and, best of all, belief in God.

Burckhardt claims Italy, not Reformation Germany, first wrote the inner man. Yet his star case, Cellini, is "not founded on introspection": self-measure, not self-examination. Cardano keeps "belief in God". Check against `cellini-autobiography.md` before relying on Burckhardt's reading.

### burckhardt.16 · Part IV ch. viii — what Pico stands for: humanity, not the single man

- **Claims:** C4.24 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. viii, lines 10056–10065
- **Checked:** text file

> In the next part of this work we shall show how differences of birth had lost their significance in Italy. Much of this was doubtless owing to the fact that men and man were here first thoroughly and profoundly understood. This one single result of the Renaissance is enough to fill us with everlasting thankfulness. The logical notion of humanity was old enough--but here the notion became a fact. The loftiest conceptions on this subject were uttered by Pico della Mirandola in his speech on the dignity of man

Burckhardt brings in Pico for *humanity* and against differences of birth: a claim about man as such, equal and common, not about the singular individual. On Burckhardt's own framing Pico is closer to the equality of C4.13 than to singularity.

### burckhardt-pico.01 · *Oration* (Burckhardt's German paraphrase, in Middlemore's English)

- **Claims:** C4.24 (complicates), C4.21 (for)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. viii, lines 10065–10078. Pico via Burckhardt via Middlemore: Burckhardt's abridged German rendering of the Latin, translated
- **Checked:** text file

> God, he tells us, made man at the close of the creation, to know the laws of the universe, to love its beauty, to admire its greatness. He bound him to no fixed place, to no prescribed form of work, and by no iron necessity, but gave him freedom to will and to move. ‘I have set thee,’ says the Creator to Adam, ‘in the midst of the world, that thou mayst the more easily behold and see all that is therein. I created thee a being neither heavenly nor earthly, neither mortal nor immortal only, that thou mightest be free to shape and to overcome thyself. Thou mayst sink into a beast, and be born anew to the divine likeness. The brutes bring from their mother’s body what they will carry with them as long as they live; the higher spirits are from the beginning, or soon after,[806] what they will be for ever. To thee alone is given a growth and a development depending on thine own free will. Thou bearest in thee the germs of a universal life.’

Self-shaping, yes: no fixed place, form or necessity, growth by "thine own free will". Own origin, no: God makes man, God "set thee", God gives the freedom, and the upward aim is "the divine likeness". Man shapes himself but does not originate himself.

### burckhardt-pico.02 · *Oration* (Pico's Latin, Burckhardt's note 805) — "plastes et fictor"

- **Claims:** C4.24 (for: self-shaping; against: self-origination)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. viii, note 805, lines 21099–21113. Pico's Latin as printed by Burckhardt; "augustiis" is the text's misprint for *angustiis*
- **Checked:** text file

> ‘Statuit tandem optimus opifex ut cui dari nihil proprium poterat commune esset quidquid privatum singulis fuerat. Igitur hominem accepit indiscretae opus imaginis atque in mundi posito meditullio sic est allocutus; Nec certam sedem, nec propriam faciem, nec munus ullum peculiare tibi dedimus, O Adam, ut quam sedem, quam faciem, quae munera tute optaveris, ea pro voto pro tua sententia habeas et possideas. Definita caeteris natura inter praescriptas a nobis leges coercetur, tu nullis augustiis coercitus pro tuo arbitrio, in cujus manus te posui, tibi illam praefinies. [...] Nec te caelestem neque terrenum, neque mortalem neque immortalem fecimus, ut tui ipsius quasi arbitrarius honorariusque plastes et fictor in quam malueris tute formam effingas.

The Latin is stronger than the English on self-shaping. My gloss: man is to fix his own nature "by your own judgment", and to be, as it were, the free and honoured *moulder and maker* (plastes et fictor) of himself, in whatever form he prefers. But every verb of origin belongs to God: the best workman "decreed", "took" man, "set" him, "made" him; the self-making is assigned. And man's lack of a nature of his own is filled with what is *common* (commune) to all creatures: not singular.

### burckhardt-pico.03 · *Oration* (Pico's Latin, note 805) — the ending the English leaves out

- **Claims:** C4.24 (against: "first philosophical claim to self-origination"), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. viii, note 805, lines 21113–21126
- **Checked:** text file

> Poteris in inferiora quae sunt bruta degenerare, poteris in superiora quae sunt divina ex tui animi sententia regenerari. O summam dei patris liberalitatem, summam et admirandam hominis felicitatem. Cui datum id habere quod optat, id esse quod velit. [...] Nascenti homini omnifaria semina et omnigenae vitæ germina indidit pater; quæ quisque excoluerit illa adolescent et fructus suos ferent in illo. Si vegetalia, planta fiet, si sensualia, obbrutescet, si rationalia, coeleste evadet animal, si intellectualia, angelus erit et dei filius, et si nulla creaturarum sorte contentus in unitatis centrum suae se receperit, unus cum deo spiritus factus in solitaria patris caligine qui est super omnia constitutus omnibus antestabit.’

Being what he wills ("id esse quod velit") is called "summam dei patris liberalitatem", the supreme generosity of God the Father (our translation): a gift, not a self-origin. The seeds are put in by the Father ("patris"), and the top of the scale is "angelus erit et dei filius" (he will be an angel and son of God) and, withdrawn into one's own centre, "unus cum deo spiritus factus" (made one spirit with God; our translations). Burckhardt's English stops at "germs of a universal life" and drops this God-ward ending. Pico's freedom is given by God and aimed at God, as the pressure point said.

### burckhardt-pico.04 · Note 805 — Brunetto Latini, and a contemporary's correction

- **Claims:** C4.24 (complicates), C4.21 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part IV ch. viii, note 805, lines 21128–21138 (Burckhardt's own note)
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not altogether suitable, since a great part of the discourse is devoted to the defence of the peculiar philosophy of Pico, and the praise of, the Jewish Cabbalah. [...] More than two hundred years before, Brunetto Latini (_Tesoro_, lib. i. cap. 13, ed. Chabaille, p. 20) had said: ‘Toutes choses dou ciel en aval sont faites pour l’ome; mais li hom at faiz pour lui meisme.’ The words seemed to a contemporary to have too much human pride in them, and he added: ‘e por Dieu amer et servir et por avoir la joie pardurable.’

Burckhardt warns that the "dignity of man" title does not fit a speech mostly about Pico's philosophy and the Cabbala. His 13th-century parallel (man is made "for himself") was felt as pride and corrected at once: man is made to love and serve God. The bound was reasserted the moment the claim was made.

## Findings in brief

- **C4.22:** verified verbatim (line 4352). The evidence behind it is impressionistic, by Burckhardt's own admission (.03): biography, fashion, fame, art.
- **C4.24:** self-shaping holds; self-origination does not. Pico's man is made, placed and freed by God and aims at union with God (pico.01–.03). Burckhardt's English trims the God-ward ending; the Latin note restores it.
- **C4.21:** Burckhardt does support a Renaissance self that predates, and is distinct from, Reformation answerability (.01, .02, .10, .11, .15). He even derives "moral responsibility" from it (.10) and links it to the 18th century (.08). But the self he praises stays inside God and sympathy (.05, .14). The self that drops every limit (.09, .13) he calls "the fundamental vice", and its limits are honour (.08) and ridicule (.07).
