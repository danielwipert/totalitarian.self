# Émile Coué, *Self Mastery Through Conscious Autosuggestion* (1922); Edward L. Bernays, *Propaganda* (1928)

- **Edition used:** Coué: English translation, New York: American Library Service, 1922 (title page, file lines 45–61; "All Translation Rights Reserved"). **No translator is named** in the file. Bernays: original English, New York: Horace Liveright, 1928 (file lines 21, 55–63).
- **Text file:** `texts/coue-self-mastery.txt` (Gutenberg #27203), `texts/bernays-propaganda.txt` (Gutenberg #78634) (both clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): Coué: first English edition; Bernays: 1928 Liveright edition
- **Citation form:** chapter or section heading, plus file and line
- **Claims to test:** C5.14, C8.*, C5.3
- **Note on the Coué volume:** only the first four pieces are by Coué (file lines 66–69). "Thoughts and Precepts" were "taken down literally by Mme. Emile Leon, his disciple" (line 1203). The séance survey, letters, "The Miracle Within" and the last two pieces are by patients and disciples. Passages below say whose words they are.

## Reading summary

**Coué.** The self is not its own author. Coué splits it in two (line 109). The conscious will is powerless, "nothing but wretched puppets" (line 276). The unconscious that really acts is "credulous" and takes in what it is told "with unreasoning docility" (lines 149–150). In practice the self is the object of a technique: an operator uses "a tone of command which suffers no disobedience" (line 573). Words are "printed, engraved, and encrusted" in the subject (line 623). The formula is recited "mechanically" on a knotted string (line 2086). And "It is not the person who acts, it is the method" (line 1372). Coué's key theoretical move is to call every outside suggestion an *auto*suggestion ("the implanting of an idea in oneself by oneself", line 312). That relabels an outside engine as an inside one. It does not settle C8.4's inside/outside question; it dissolves it. The aphorisms do come close to the self-originating Self: "We are what we make ourselves and not what circumstances make us" (line 1271), and "you have only yourself to blame" (line 1289). But Coué keeps the frames: reason, duty, obedience, "think more of others than of themselves" (line 1768). His admirers add God. Self-mastery here is a popular *technique* of answerable self-improvement. It is not a metaphysics of self-origin. Verdict for C5.14: the move into popular literature is confirmed. The "self-originating, answerable to nothing" content is not.

**Bernays.** Bernays places the engine outside and on top: "an invisible government" of a "relatively small number of persons" (lines 120, 137). He names the effect C8.2 describes, conformity experienced as personal choice. The suit buyer "imagines that he is choosing, according to his taste and his personality" (lines 749–750). The music room "will come to him as his own idea" (line 1255). Making a choice feel self-originated is a deliberate technique in 1928. But Bernays **never uses the vocabulary of self-expression for the consumer**. A grep of the file finds no "self-expression", "individuality", "identity" (except line 128, in another sense) or "unique". Goods are sold as symbols of *status*: "a symbol of social position" (line 1149), the neighbors, fraternity colors. That is Mill's vocabulary of station, which supports C8.2's contrast. Belonging is everywhere ("unconsciously belong to a sorority", line 310), but it runs on a natural "gregarious" herd instinct (line 1081), not on the ideology of the Self. That is against C8.5. Where "express" and "personality" do occur, they belong to *corporations and public figures*: "express its true personality" (line 1533), "treat personality as he would treat any other objective fact" (line 3716). The self here is a managed object, not a source. Two complications: (1) Bernays's outside engine needs inside fuel ("a mutual need has to exist", line 2539; the Ivory soap campaign runs on children's own "esthetic" and "exhibitionist" pleasure in making things, lines 1321–1329). (2) The book is a sales pitch for his own profession, so its claims of power should be discounted.

**For §8.** By 1928 the following were already described as deliberate techniques: manufactured "originality" in a product (line 1970), the accessory bought to belong (the Klan costume, line 535), and choice made to feel one's own. What is missing is the *idiom of self-expression* in the consumer's mouth. So C8.2 can stand only as a claim about a later vocabulary, and C8.4's "engine inside the ideology" must be argued against Bernays's documented outside engine, not assumed.

## Passages

### coue-selfmastery.01 · "The Conscious Self and the Unconscious Self"; "Will and Imagination" — "wretched puppets of which our imagination holds all the strings"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (against), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, sections "The Conscious Self and the Unconscious Self" and "Will and Imagination"; `texts/coue-self-mastery.txt` lines 148–162, 272–278
- **Checked:** text file

> Further, it is credulous and accepts with unreasoning docility what it is told. [...] Not only does the unconscious self preside over the functions of our organism, but also over _all our actions whatever they are_. It is this that we call imagination, and it is this which, contrary to accepted opinion, _always_ makes us act even, and _above all_, against _our will_ when there is antagonism between these two forces.

> Thus we who are so proud of our will, who believe that we are free to act as we like, are in reality nothing but wretched puppets of which our imagination holds all the strings. We only cease to be puppets when we have learned to guide our imagination.

The self is split, and the part that acts is a credulous receiver. It is not an origin. Freedom is steering, not authorship. This cuts against the self-as-source of C5.3, even in the founding text of popular self-mastery.

### coue-selfmastery.02 · "Suggestion and Autosuggestion" — "the implanting of an idea in oneself by oneself"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, "Suggestion and Autosuggestion"; lines 307–321
- **Checked:** text file

> What then is suggestion? It may be defined as "the act of imposing an idea on the brain of another". Does this action really exist? Properly speaking, no. Suggestion does not indeed exist by itself. It does not and cannot exist except on the _sine qua non_ condition of transforming itself into _autosuggestion_ in the subject. This latter word may be defined as "the implanting of an idea in oneself by oneself."

Coué's central move: every outside influence counts as the subject's own act. This theory lets an outside engine pass as an inside one. It does not pick a side in C8.4's inside/outside contrast; it blurs it. Yet the operator never disappears (see .03).

### coue-selfmastery.03 · "How to Teach Patients"; "Method of Procedure in Curative Suggestion" — "you yourself and your whole organism are going to obey"

- **Claims:** C5.3 (against), C5.14 (against)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, "How to Teach Patients to Make Autosuggestions" and "Method of Procedure in Curative Suggestion"; lines 573, 622–627, 747–753
- **Checked:** text file

> Always use a tone of command which suffers no disobedience.

> Now tell yourself that every word I say is going to fix itself in your mind, and be printed, engraved, and encrusted in it, that, there, it is going to stay fixed, imprinted, and encrusted, and that without your will or knowledge, in fact perfectly unconsciously on your part, you yourself and your whole organism are going to obey.

> Before sending away your patient, you must tell him that he carries within him the instrument by which he can cure himself, and that you are, as it were, only a professor teaching him to use this instrument, and that he must help you in your task. Thus, every morning before rising, and every night on getting into bed, he must shut his eyes and in thought transport himself into your presence, and then repeat twenty times consecutively [...]

In practice the self is a surface to be imprinted by an operator. Even the "auto" exercise is done "in thought" in the operator's presence. The rhetoric says "the instrument [is] within him"; the procedure treats him as the object of a technique.

### coue-selfmastery.04 · The formula; "Thoughts and Precepts" — "It is not the person who acts, it is the method."

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C5.3 (against)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, end of "Method of Procedure", lines 757–760; "Thoughts and Precepts" (recorded by Mme. Leon), line 1372; "A Survey of the 'Séances'" (by a disciple, reporting Coué), lines 2083–2088
- **Checked:** text file

> "EVERY DAY, IN EVERY RESPECT, I AM GETTING BETTER AND BETTER." In his mind he should emphasize the words "_in every respect_" which applies to every need, mental or physical.

> It is not the person who acts, it is the method.

> [...] repeat twenty times following, moving your _lips_ (that is indispensable) and counting _mechanically_ on a string with twenty knots in it the following phrase: _'Every day, in every respect, I am getting better and better.'"_

This verifies the formula (line 757). The "Education" essay translates it differently: "Day by day, in all respects, I grow better" (line 1805). The formula is a mechanical recitation, and the precept puts agency in the method, not the person. The famous first-person phrase is spoken by a self that is being worked on.

### coue-selfmastery.05 · "Thoughts and Precepts" — "We are what we make ourselves and not what circumstances make us."

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for), C7.4 (for), C7.1 (for)
- **Location:** "Thoughts and Precepts" (recorded by Mme. Leon), lines 1222–1227, 1263–1272, 1288–1289; "A Survey of the 'Séances'" (disciple reporting Coué), lines 2111–2115
- **Checked:** text file

> To become master of oneself it is enough to think that one is becoming so. [...] It is not in me but in yourself that you must have confidence, for it is in yourself alone that dwells the force which can cure you.

> By believing oneself to be the master of one's thoughts one becomes so.
> [...]
> Everyone of our thoughts, good or bad, becomes concrete, materializes, and becomes in short a reality.
>
> We are what we make ourselves and not what circumstances make us.

> Do not then blame fate, you have only yourself to blame.

> M. Coué also adds what follows: "If you formerly allowed yourself to make bad autosuggestions because you did it unconsciously, now that you know what I have just taught you, you must no longer let this happen. And if, in spite of all, you still do it, you must only accuse yourself, and say _'Mea culpa, mea maxima culpa.'"_

This is the strongest "for" C5.14 in either text. Circumstance is denied as a cause, thought makes reality, and failure is laid on the self alone (C7.4), in the language of confession (C7.1). But these are aphorisms recorded by a disciple, not argued. Even here, the self that "makes itself" does so by a taught technique.

### coue-selfmastery.06 · End of "Self Mastery"; "Will and Imagination"; "Education As It Ought To Be" — "the child arrives at making of himself what his parents desire him to be"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates), C5.14 (against), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, before "Conclusion", lines 1175–1180; "Will and Imagination", lines 252–254; "Education As It Ought To Be", lines 1687–1689
- **Checked:** text file

> When the child is asleep his body and his conscious self are at rest and, as it were, annihilated; his unconscious self however is awake; it is then to the latter alone that one speaks, and as it is very credulous it accepts what one says to it without dispute, so that, little by little, the child arrives at making of himself what his parents desire him to be.

> We human beings have a certain resemblance to sheep, and involuntarily, we are irresistibly impelled to follow other people's examples, _imagining_ that we cannot do otherwise.

> For you must know that all our words, all our acts, are only the result of autosuggestions caused, for the most part, by the suggestion of example or speech.

The language of self-authorship ("making of himself") names content that others implant while the conscious self is "annihilated". The form is close to C8.2: conformity performed in the language of the self. But the content is obedience, and it is openly intended. Imitation is natural and comes from "example", not from an ideology of the Self (C8.1, C8.4).

### coue-selfmastery.07 · "Method of Procedure"; "Education As It Ought To Be" — "think more of others than of themselves"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C5.9 (compare)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, "Method of Procedure", lines 695–700; "Education As It Ought To Be", lines 1764–1771, 1820–1822
- **Checked:** text file

> Without it one can accomplish nothing, with it one can accomplish whatever one likes, (within reason, of course). You are then going to have confidence in yourself, and this confidence gives you the assurance that you are capable of accomplishing perfectly well whatever you wish to do, --_on condition that it is reasonable_,--and whatever it is your duty to do.

> Teach them to love all mankind, without distinction of caste. That one must always be ready to succor those who are in need of help, and that one must never be afraid of spending time and money for those who are in need; in short, that they must think more of others than of themselves.
>
> In so doing an inner satisfaction is experienced that the egoist ever seeks and never finds.

> Telling them to shut their eyes, they should say: "Children, I expect you always to be polite and kind to everyone, obedient to your parents and teachers, when they give you an order [...]

The confidence Coué installs is bounded by reason and duty, aimed at others, and for children aimed at obedience. The disciples' letters add a Christian frame (e.g. lines 2780–2789). This is the C5.14 pressure point for Smiles and New Thought again: the popular self-mastery text keeps its frame.

### coue-selfmastery.08 · "The Superiority of This Method"; "The Use of Suggestion for... Moral Ailments"; letters — suggestion, advertising and society

- **Claims:** C8.4 (compare), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** *Self Mastery*, lines 838–844, 961–964; letter from Josephine M. Richardson, lines 2525–2529
- **Checked:** text file

> It seems to me then that the doctor should always prescribe medicines to his patient, and, as much as possible, medicines made up by himself rather than the standard remedies so much advertised and which owe their only value to the advertisement.

> I am absolutely convinced that if suggestion were daily applied to vicious children, more than 50 per cent could be reclaimed. Would it not be an immense service to render society, to bring back to it sane and well members of it who were formerly corroded by moral decay?

> [...] there seems no limits to the possibilities and future scope of the principles you enunciate, not only in the physical life of children but also in possibilities for changing the ideas now prevalent in punishment of crime, in government, in fact, in all the relations of life. . . .

Coué sees that advertised remedies work by suggestion, and he tells doctors to use the effect (prescribe "even if they are not necessary", line 829). From the start suggestion is a tool for shaping people to society, and admirers already picture it in government. This is the bridge to Bernays: the same psychology turned outward.

### bernays-propaganda.01 · Ch. I "Organizing Chaos" — "an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (against), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. I; `texts/bernays-propaganda.txt` lines 117–123, 139–140
- **Checked:** text file

> The conscious and intelligent manipulation of the organized habits and opinions of the masses is an important element in democratic society. Those who manipulate this unseen mechanism of society constitute an invisible government which is the true ruling power of our country.
>
> We are governed, our minds are molded, our tastes formed, our ideas suggested, largely by men we have never heard of.

> It is they who pull the wires which control the public mind, who harness old social forces and contrive new ways to bind and guide the world.

Bernays's engine is outside and on top: a small number of manipulators. Taste is listed among the things governed. If C8.4 claims an inside engine, it has to say how that engine differs from this one, which is documented in 1928.

### bernays-propaganda.02 · Ch. I — "Thousands of women may unconsciously belong to a sorority"

- **Claims:** C8.5 (against), C8.4 (against), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. I; lines 163–167, 172–175, 307–311
- **Checked:** text file

> From our leaders and the media they use to reach the public, we accept the evidence and the demarcation of issues bearing upon public questions; from some ethical teacher, be it a minister, a favorite essayist, or merely prevailing opinion, we accept a standardized code of social conduct to which we conform most of the time.

> To avoid such confusion, society consents to have its choice narrowed to ideas and objects brought to its attention through propaganda of all kinds.

> Ideas are sifted and opinions stereotyped in the neighborhood bridge club. Leaders assert their authority through community drives and amateur theatricals. Thousands of women may unconsciously belong to a sorority which follows the fashions set by a single society leader.

Belonging and fashion already go together here, steered by one leader. But the belonging is "unconscious", and the vocabulary is conformity and consent, not self-expression. Belonging works with no self-expression routing, which is against C8.5's "because no other vocabulary remains".

### bernays-propaganda.03 · Ch. II "The New Propaganda" — "rubber stamps"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. II; lines 384–393
- **Checked:** text file

> Universal literacy was supposed to educate the common man to control his environment. Once he could read and write he would have a mind fit to rule. So ran the democratic doctrine. But instead of a mind, universal literacy has given him rubber stamps, rubber stamps inked with advertising slogans, with editorials, with published scientific data, with the trivialities of the tabloids and the platitudes of history, but quite innocent of original thought. Each man’s rubber stamps are the duplicates of millions of others, so that when those millions are exposed to the same stimuli, all receive identical imprints.

Bernays calls standardization standardization. No one in his account claims to be expressing himself; the 1928 vocabulary of conformity is mass psychology. That fits C8.2's claim that accessorizing speaks a newer idiom. But it shows that idiom is *not* yet present in 1928, so its arrival has to be dated from other sources.

### bernays-propaganda.04 · Ch. II — the Klan: "makes it his own. He buys the sheet and pillow-case costume"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. II; lines 529–538
- **Checked:** text file

> When an Imperial Wizard, sensing what is perhaps hunger for an ideal, offers a picture of a nation all Nordic and nationalistic, the common man of the older American stock, feeling himself elbowed out of his rightful position and prosperity by the newer immigrant stocks, grasps the picture which fits in so neatly with his prejudices, and makes it his own. He buys the sheet and pillow-case costume, and bands with his fellows by the thousand into a huge group powerful enough to swing state elections and to throw a ponderous monkey wrench into a national convention.

This is the passage closest to §8's sequence: an inner need, a picture "made his own", a bought costume as the badge, and a mass group. But the need is grievance over "rightful position" (station, not self-expression), and a leader who exploits it supplies the picture. The engine is an inside need worked by an outside operator.

### bernays-propaganda.05 · Ch. III "The New Propagandists"; Ch. II — "imagines that he is choosing, according to his taste and his personality"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for, as a description of the effect), C8.4 (against)
- **Location:** Ch. III, lines 747–752, 768–770; Ch. II (velvet campaign), lines 620–621
- **Checked:** text file

> In some departments of our daily life, in which we imagine ourselves free agents, we are ruled by dictators exercising great power. A man buying a suit of clothes imagines that he is choosing, according to his taste and his personality, the kind of garment which he prefers. In reality, he may be obeying the orders of an anonymous gentleman tailor in London.

> The fashionable men in New York, Chicago, Boston and Philadelphia wear them. And the Topeka man, recognizing this leadership, does the same.

> She bought the velvet because she liked it and because it was in fashion.

This is the closest Bernays comes to C8.2: conformity experienced as one's own taste and "personality". But it is the buyer's private illusion, not a public vocabulary, and Bernays puts the engine outside (dictators, leadership). So C8.2's core observation was already available in 1928. What C8.2 adds, the explicit idiom of *self-expression*, Bernays does not use.

### bernays-propaganda.06 · Ch. IV "The Psychology of Public Relations" — "he feels himself to be member of a herd"

- **Claims:** C8.5 (against), C8.4 (against)
- **Location:** Ch. IV; lines 1077–1084, 1099–1101
- **Checked:** text file

> If you can influence the leaders, either with or without their conscious coöperation, you automatically influence the group which they sway. But men do not need to be actually gathered together in a public meeting or in a street riot, to be subject to the influences of mass psychology. Because man is by nature gregarious he feels himself to be member of a herd, even when he is alone in his room with the curtains drawn. His mind retains the patterns which have been stamped on it by the group influences.

> In making up its mind its first impulse is usually to follow the example of a trusted leader.

Here the wish to belong is a natural instinct (Trotter's herd), not a product of the ideology of the Self, and it needs no self-expression routing. This is against C8.5. C8.4 must explain why a herd instinct, plus leaders, is not already enough.

### bernays-propaganda.07 · Ch. IV — the car as "a symbol of social position"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for the contrast with station; against any claim that goods were already sold as self-expression), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. IV; lines 1131–1138, 1143–1150
- **Checked:** text file

> Men are rarely aware of the real reasons which motivate their actions. A man may believe that he buys a motor car because, after careful study of the technical features of all makes on the market, he has concluded that this is the best. He is almost certainly fooling himself. He bought it, perhaps, because a friend whose financial acumen he respects bought one last week; or because his neighbors believed he was not able to afford a car of that class; or because its colors are those of his college fraternity.

> A thing may be desired not for its intrinsic worth or usefulness, but because he has unconsciously come to see in it a symbol of something else, the desire for which he is ashamed to admit to himself. [...] He may really want it because it is a symbol of social position, an evidence of his success in business, or a means of pleasing his wife.

Goods work as symbols, but of status, emulation and group colors. That is Mill's vocabulary of station, not self-expression. It supports C8.2's contrast (the station idiom still dominates in 1928). It counts against reading Bernays as selling goods for self-expression or identity.

### bernays-propaganda.08 · Ch. IV — the music room: "It will come to him as his own idea."

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C8.4 (against), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. IV; lines 1229–1231, 1252–1260
- **Checked:** text file

> The modern propagandist therefore sets to work to create circumstances which will modify that custom. He appeals perhaps to the home instinct which is fundamental.

> The music room will be accepted because it has been made the thing. And the man or woman who has a music room, or has arranged a corner of the parlor as a musical corner, will naturally think of buying a piano. It will come to him as his own idea.
>
> Under the old salesmanship the manufacturer said to the prospective purchaser, “Please buy a piano.” The new salesmanship has reversed the process and caused the prospective purchaser to say to the manufacturer, “Please sell me a piano.”

Making conformity ("made the thing") feel self-originated is a deliberate technique in 1928. This supports C8.2's description. It also bears on C5.14 with a twist: the *feeling* of self-origination is manufactured from outside. It is against C8.4, since the engine is outside, though it works through "the home instinct".

### bernays-propaganda.09 · Ch. IV — Ivory soap: "for the sake of the gratification obtained in the sculpture work itself"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. IV; lines 1316–1329
- **Checked:** text file

> The good will was greatly enhanced by the fact that this soap had become not merely the concern of the housewife but also a matter of personal and intimate interest to her children.
>
> A number of familiar psychological motives were set in motion in the carrying out of this campaign. The esthetic, the competitive, the gregarious (much of the sculpturing was done in school groups), the snobbish (the impulse to follow the example of a recognized leader), the exhibitionist, and—last but by no means least—the maternal.
>
> All these motives and group habits were put in concerted motion by the simple machinery of group leadership and authority. As if actuated by the pressure of a button, people began working for the client for the sake of the gratification obtained in the sculpture work itself.

This is the case closest to an engine that uses people's own wish to express themselves: children's making, done in groups and for show, is harnessed for a brand. But expression is one motive among six, named "esthetic" and "exhibitionist", not self-expression. And the process runs top-down (sponsor → leaders → groups), the reverse of §8's genuine-expression-first sequence.

### bernays-propaganda.10 · Ch. V "Business and the Public" — "some element of originality that will distinguish it"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. V; lines 1967–1977
- **Checked:** text file

> The logical way out of this dilemma is for the manufacturer to develop some sales appeal other than mere cheapness, to give the product, in the public mind, some other attraction, some idea that will modify the product slightly, some element of originality that will distinguish it from products in the same line. Thus, a manufacturer of typewriters paints his machines in cheerful hues. These special types of appeal can be popularized by the manipulation of the principles familiar to the propagandist—the principles of gregariousness, obedience to authority, emulation, and the like. A minor element can be made to assume economic importance by being established in the public mind as a matter of style.

Accessorizing is a deliberate technique by 1928, but applied to products, not persons. "Originality" and distinction are designed into a standardized product and spread through "gregariousness, obedience to authority, emulation". Simmel's imitation/distinction pair has become a business method. §8's process (genuine expression → accessory) runs the other way: Bernays starts from the accessory.

### bernays-propaganda.11 · Ch. V; Ch. XI "The Mechanics of Propaganda" — "express its true personality"; "treat personality as he would treat any other objective fact"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C5.14 (complicates), C5.3 (compare)
- **Location:** Ch. V, lines 1454–1457, 1533, 1621–1623; Ch. XI, lines 3716–3725
- **Checked:** text file

> Business must express itself and its entire corporate existence so that the public will understand and accept it. It must dramatize its personality and interpret its objectives in every particular in which it comes into contact with the community (or the nation) of which it is a part.

> Big business studies every move which may express its true personality.

> It is not a question of ballyhoo, of creating a picturesque fiction for public consumption. It is merely a question of finding the appropriate modes of expressing the personality that is to be dramatized.

> The propagandist must treat personality as he would treat any other objective fact within his province.
> [...]
> Once a public figure has decided what ends he wishes to achieve, he must regard himself objectively and present an outward picture of himself which is consistent with his real character and his aims.

The vocabulary of self-expression ("express its true personality") is present in 1928, but it belongs to corporations and public figures, as a managed technique. Personality is an "objective fact" to be presented: the self as object, not source. This suggests a possible route for C8.2's idiom, from corporate PR down to persons, which would give it an outside origin. That is a hypothesis to test.

### bernays-propaganda.12 · Ch. V, VI, XI — limits of the outside engine: "a mutual need has to exist"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. V, lines 1481–1487; Ch. VI, lines 2537–2540; Ch. XI, lines 3750–3752
- **Checked:** text file

> The public has its own standards and demands and habits. You may modify them, but you dare not run counter to them. You cannot persuade a whole generation of women to wear long skirts, but you may, by working through leaders of fashion, persuade them to wear evening dresses which are long in back. The public is not an amorphous mass which can be molded at will, or dictated to.

> There has to be fertile ground for the leader and the idea to fall on. But the leader also has to have some vital seed to sow. To use another figure, a mutual need has to exist before either can become positively effective.

> No matter how sophisticated, how cynical the public may become about publicity methods, it must respond to the basic appeals, because it will always need food, crave amusement, long for beauty, respond to leadership.

Bernays's outside engine runs on inside fuel: existing desires and a "mutual need". So the pressure point "Bernays's engine is outside" is not clean. The real question is *which* inner desire the operator uses. In 1928 it is herd, station, home and beauty, not the wish to express oneself. Caveat: Bernays has a motive to claim both power (to clients) and harmlessness (to critics).
