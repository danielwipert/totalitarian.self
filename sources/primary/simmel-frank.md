- **Edition used:** English as published (both OCR). Simmel: "Fashion," *International Quarterly* 10 (1904), pp. 130–155 (translator not named). Frank: *The Re-discovery of America* (New York: Scribner's, 1929).

- **Claims to test:** C8.1, C8.2, C8.4, C8.5, C8.7, C5.14, C6.13

## Summary of the reading

- **Simmel** has most of §8's social machinery in 1904: fashion joins imitation (belonging) and differentiation (distinction) at once (simmel.01, .02); it spreads downward until it dies (simmel.05, .06); it detaches contents, "even socialism and individualism," from the motives they rose from (simmel.03, .04); and it lets a person feel singled out while obeying (simmel.07, .10). He even has the rebel's refusal as inverted imitation (simmel.09). What he does *not* have: (a) a genuine feeling at the origin (fashion has no reason but social form, simmel.02); (b) conformity felt as the self (for him fashion stays at the "periphery of personality," a mask that protects the inner self, simmel.11, .12). His engine is both needs together, sorted by class.
- **Frank** is not a witness for the self as its own origin. Dewey's quotation is accurate in its words, but it is Frank's restatement of his ch. II, and Frank's next sentence calls it a "simplification" that "was not accurate": "Selfhood is falsehood, unless it is experienced as a relative focus in the unity of all men and all being" (frank.02). Frank's self is bounded by the Whole, which is its origin (frank.01). He *describes* the separate ego as America's disease (frank.05, .06) and *prescribes* the Whole, beginning in the person (frank.07). Dewey's real point, that the cure starts in individuals, stands.

## Passages: Simmel

### simmel.01 · pp. 133, 137 — fashion satisfies both needs at once; "Should one of these be absent, fashion will not be formed"

- **Claims:** C8.4 (for), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 133, 137; lines 6365–6377, 6551–6554
- **Checked:** text file

> Fashion is the imitation of a given example and satisfies the demand for social adaptation; it leads the individual upon the road which all travel, it furnishes a general condition, which resolves the conduct of every individual into a mere example. At the same time it satisfies in no less degree the need of differentiation, the tendency towards dissimilarity, the desire for change and contrast [...] because fashions differ for different classes — the fashions of the upper stratum of society are never identical with those of the lower; in fact, they are abandoned by the former as soon as the latter prepares to appropriate them. [p. 137] Two social tendencies are essential to the establishment of fashion, namely, the need of union on the one hand and the need of isolation on the other. Should one of these be absent, fashion will not be formed

Simmel's engine is both needs together, not belonging alone: union and distinction are equally necessary. §8's step 4 (attachment to belong) is half of his formula; the other half, distinction from below, is missing from §8.

### simmel.02 · pp. 134 — "Fashion is merely a product of social demands"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (against, step 1), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** p. 134; lines 6406–6416, 6420–6423, 6430
- **Checked:** text file. OCR "uno aciu" read as "uno actu".

> Thus fashion on the one hand signifies union with those in the same class, the uniformity of a circle characterized by it, and, uno actu, the exclusion of all other groups. Union and segregation are the two fundamental functions which are here inseparably united [...] Fashion is merely a product of social demands, even though the individual object which it creates or recreates may represent a more or less individual need. This is clearly proved by the fact that very frequently not the slightest reason can be found for the creations of fashion from the standpoint of an objective, aesthetic, or other expediency. [...] in the case of fashion there is a complete separation of the two elements, and there remains for the individual only this general acceptance as the deciding motive to appropriate it. [...] The only motivations with which fashion is concerned are formal social ones.

Against §8's first step: Simmel's fashion has no genuine feeling at its origin; it is social form from the start. §8's sequence (a real expression that is later emptied) is a different story from his, and that difference is §8's best claim to originality.

### simmel.03 · p. 135 — "even socialism and individualism" become fashion once cut from "the deeper human motives from which they have risen"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (for, step 5), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 135; lines 6443–6447
- **Checked:** text file. OCR "such 4s" read as "such as"; "but, it" read as "but it".

> Fashion occasionally will accept objectively determined subjects such as religious faith, scientific interests, even socialism and individualism; but it does not become operative as fashion until these subjects can be considered independent of the deeper human motives from which they have risen. For this reason the rule of fashion becomes in such fields unendurable.

This is §8's "perversion" step in one sentence: a content becomes fashion when cut from the motive it rose from. And individualism is already named as a possible fashion in 1904, which pressures C8.2's claim that the disguise word is new.

### simmel.04 · p. 132 — imitation spreads creations "without the aid of the forces which were originally the very condition of their birth"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (for, steps 4–5), C8.4 (for)
- **Location:** p. 132; lines 6314–6322
- **Checked:** text file. OCR "affiords" read as "affords".

> It affords the pregnant possibility of continually extending the greatest creations of the human spirit, without the aid of the forces which were originally the very condition of their birth. Imitation, furthermore, gives to the individual the satisfaction of not standing alone in his actions. Whenever we imitate, we transfer not only the demand for creative activity, but also the responsibility for the action from ourselves to another. Thus the individual is freed from the worry of choosing and appears simply as a creature of the group, as a vessel of the social contents.

The imitator carries a creation without the force that made it (§8 step 5) and does so for "the satisfaction of not standing alone" (the belonging motive, C8.4). Note that Simmel calls this a "pregnant possibility," not only a loss.

### simmel.05 · p. 135 — "the upper classes turn away from this style and adopt a new one"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 135; lines 6468–6472, 6478–6480
- **Checked:** text file

> Just as soon as the lower classes begin to copy their style, thereby crossing the line of demarcation the upper classes have drawn and destroying the uniformity of their coherence, the upper classes turn away from this style and adopt a new one, which in its turn differentiates them from the masses; and thus the game goes merrily on. [...] Indeed, we may often observe that the more nearly one set has approached another, the more frantic becomes the desire for imitation from below and the seeking for the new from above.

The same arc as §8's steps 3–6 (convergence, attachment by outsiders, the first group's loss), but driven by class rank and run as an endless cycle. In §8 the first group expresses; in Simmel it distinguishes itself and moves on.

### simmel.06 · pp. 138–139 — "As fashion spreads, it gradually goes to its [p. 139] doom"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (for, steps 3–6)
- **Location:** pp. 138–139; lines 6616–6629
- **Checked:** text file

> The very character of fashion demands that it should be exercised at one time only by a portion of the given group, the great majority being merely on the road to adopting it. As soon as an example has been universally adopted [...] we no longer speak of fashion. As fashion spreads, it gradually goes to its [p. 139] doom. The distinctiveness which in the early stages of a set fashion assures for it a certain distribution is destroyed as the fashion spreads, and as this element wanes, the fashion also is bound to die.

Convergence destroys what made the form worth joining: §8's steps 3–5 in Simmel's terms. His end point is death of the fashion, not §8's lasting "accessory."

### simmel.07 · pp. 140–141 — "a social obedience, which at the same time is a form of individual differentiation"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** pp. 140–141; lines 6676–6681, 6701–6705, 6719–6722
- **Checked:** text file

> From the fact that fashion as such can never be generally in vogue, the individual derives the satisfaction of knowing that as adopted by him it still represents something special and striking, while at the same time he feels inwardly supported by a set of persons who are striving for the same thing [...] From all this we see that fashion furnishes an ideal field for individuals with dependent natures, whose self-consciousness, however, requires a certain amount of prominence, attention, and singularity. Fashion raises even the unimportant individual by making him the representative of a class, the embodiment of a joint spirit. [...] It is peculiarly characteristic of fashion that it renders possible a social obedience, which at the same time is a form of individual differentiation.

Yes, Simmel says fashion lets one feel singular while conforming. This is the core of C8.2's "conformity performed" as individuality, stated in 1904. What he describes is a feeling of being "special and striking," not a claim to be expressing one's self.

### simmel.08 · p. 141 — the dude: "the leader allows himself to be led"

- **Claims:** C8.1 (complicates), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** p. 141; lines 6726–6728, 6732–6740, 6746–6750
- **Checked:** text file

> In the dude the social demands of fashion appear exaggerated to such a degree that they completely assume an individualistic and peculiar character. [...] Thus he represents something distinctly individual, which consists in the quantitative intensification of such elements as are qualitatively common property of the given set or class. He leads the way, but all travel the same road. Representing as he does the most recently conquered heights of public taste, he seems to be marching at the head of the general procession. In reality, however, what is so frequently true of the relation between individuals and groups applies also to him: as a matter of fact, the leader allows himself to be led. [...] The spirit of democracy causes persons to seek the dignity and sensation of command in this manner; it tends to a confusion and ambiguity of sensations, which fail to distinguish between ruling the mass and being ruled by it.

The most "individual" figure in fashion is an intensified conformist, and Simmel ties the confusion to democracy. It complicates C8.1's "Chris": in Simmel even the apparent originator follows.

### simmel.09 · p. 142 — "conscious neglect of fashion represents similar imitation, but under an inverse sign"

C8.2 says the new disguise is the vocabulary once used to expose conformity. Simmel already sees the refusal of conformity become a group form in 1904. What remains new for C8.2 is the specific word "self-expression," not the move itself.

### simmel.10 · p. 145 — fashion "supplements a person's lack of importance"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C8.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 145; lines 6919–6928
- **Checked:** text file

> The individuality of the class often supplements or replaces that of the member. This identical thing fashion accomplishes with other means. Fashion also supplements a person's lack of importance, his inability to individualize his existence purely by his own unaided efforts, by enabling him to join a set characterized and singled out in the public consciousness by fashion alone. Here also, to be sure, the personality as such is reduced to a general formula, yet this formula itself, from a social standpoint, possesses an individual tinge, and thus makes up through the social way what is denied to the personality in a purely individual way.

Individuality borrowed from a group: close to §8's "accessory." For C8.5 it shows a route to belonging through felt individuality in 1904, before the vocabulary of self-expression reaches persons.

### simmel.11 · p. 146 — fashion "always stands [...] at the periphery of personality"; a "mask"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (against)
- **Location:** p. 146; lines 6949–6960, 6962–6971
- **Checked:** text file. OCR "piece de resistance" kept as printed.

> Fashion insists, to be sure, on treating all individualities alike, yet it is always done in such a way that one's whole nature is never affected. Fashion always continues to be regarded as something external, even in spheres outside of mere styles of apparel, for the form of mutability in which it is presented to the individual is under all circumstances a contrast to the stability of the ego-feeling. [...] But for this very reason fashion always stands, as I have pointed out, at the periphery of personality [...] It is this phase of fashion that is received by sensitive and peculiar persons, who use it as a sort of mask. They consider blind obedience to the standards of the general public in all externals as the conscious and desired means of reserving their personal feeling and their taste, which they are eager to reserve for themselves alone

The decisive difference from §8. For Simmel fashion is felt as external, set against the "ego-feeling"; nobody takes it for their self. §8's disguise (conformity felt and spoken as one's own self) is absent, and that absence is what §8 can claim as new.

### simmel.12 · p. 148 — Goethe: "a maximum of inner freedom" through "willing obedience to the conventions of society"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (against, on the damage), C8.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** p. 148; lines 7028–7030, 7052–7066
- **Checked:** text file. OCR "societyj" read as "society,".

> Fashion also is only one of the forms by the aid of which men seek to save their inner freedom all the more completely by sacrificing externals to enslavement by the general public. [...] Speaking broadly, we may say that the most favorable result for the aggregate value of life will be obtained when all unavoidable dependence is transferred more and more to the periphery, to the externals of life. Perhaps Goethe, in his later period, is the most eloquent example of a wholly great life, for by means of his adaptability in all externals, his strict regard for form, his willing obedience to the conventions of society, he attained a maximum of inner freedom [...] It provides us with a formula by means of which we can unequivocally attest our dependence upon what is generally adopted, our obedience to the standards established by our time, our class, and our narrower circle, and enables us to withdraw the freedom given us in life from externals and concentrate it more and more in our innermost natures.

Simmel judges outward conformity *good* when it shelters an inner self: the opposite of SPEC §8's verdict that accessorizing negates expression. The essay must answer him: its case is that the inner reserve he relies on is what self-expression gives away.

> We can discover one of the reasons why in these latter days fashion exercises such a powerful influence on our consciousness in the circumstance that the great, permanent, unquestionable convictions are continually losing strength, as a consequence of which the transitory and vacillating elements of life acquire more room for the display of their activity. > But dissolution brought release to what was held within the controlling Pattern. A greater freedom came to many elements, created a new ebullient life in Europe. These traits of the death of an old world are the modern virtues. Liberty, freedom of thought, discovery, adventure, national independence, personal self-expression, are the kind of noble name they won.

By 1929 "personal self-expression" is, for Frank, one of the modern era's highest value words, and he already treats it as a noble name for dissolution. This supports C8.7's pattern (a value word dressing something else). But what it dresses here is atomization, not conformity.
