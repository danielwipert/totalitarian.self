## Summary of the reading (2026-10-27)

**What the data can and cannot carry (read this first).** The study measures *individualism* as a single country-level index built from two composites. *Practices*: smaller households, living alone, older adults living alone, divorce (santos.07). *Values*: three WVS items, friends relative to family, teaching children independence, and choosing a political "self-expression" goal (more say in government, free speech) (santos.08). Its own definition of individualism is a self "self-directed, autonomous, and separate from others" (santos.02). That is the *healthy* Self of C5.2 (independence, separate standing), or at most its spread. The thesis's *Totalitarian* Self is a frame that admits no rival and answers to nothing (C5.3, C5.14 stage 3, C9.1). **No item here measures that.** Nothing asks whether a person recognizes any authority, duty or frame above their own choice, or whether rival frames (family, religion, nation) are still *available*. Living alone, divorcing, prizing a friend or wanting free speech are all compatible with strong answerability to God, family and community. So the study can carry, at Tier 2: (a) that individualist *practices and values* rose between 1960 and 2011, (b) in most countries, not only Western ones, (c) in step with, and after, socioeconomic development, and (d) that national differences stayed large. It **cannot** carry: that the Self *calcified* into a dogma (C1.2), that the Self became self-originating (C5.14), that America leads or differs (the country results are in supplements we do not have), or any shape of the trend beyond a straight line (the models are linear). Used as evidence for the totalizing claim, it would be a category mistake: practices are not frames.

**C1.2 (the West, above all America). Complicates, close to against for the post-1960 trend.** The rise is global. Of the practices countries, 34 of 41 rose substantially; of the values countries, 37 of 51 (or 52; the paper gives both) (santos.10, .13). The authors' own conclusion is that the shift "is not confined to the developed world" (santos.13), and they frame the older picture, built from "a handful of highly-developed countries" and above all US data, as a sampling limit (santos.01, .03, .04). **Where the US sits cannot be read from this text.** The US is in both samples (Table 1, santos.06), but the main text never reports its level or slope; those are in Supplementary Tables S5–S7. So the data neither show America leading nor show it changing differently. What they do show is that the *direction* of change since 1960 is not distinctively Western or American. C1.2 survives only as a claim about *level* (the West started and stays more individualist), and the large and persistent differences between countries are consistent with that (santos.10), but the excerpt does not show the ranking.

**C6.1 and C6.13 (America as host). Complicates; supports the mechanism of C6.13.** If individualism rises wherever white-collar work, schooling, income and cities rise (santos.12), then the *conditions* of the host are generic to development, not peculiar to America's voluntary checks. That is one reading. The other, which these data cannot rule out, is that America was the *first* host and its conditions then spread. The data say nothing about 1630–1930 or about checks such as covenant, township and association, so they neither test nor refute erosion by consent. They do fit C6.13's causal story (industrial structure, not choice, dissolved the checks): in the US over 150 years, a shift to white-collar work "preceded" individualist living arrangements (a prior study reported here; santos.05). The essay should not let "host" imply that America is needed to explain post-1960 individualism. On this evidence it is not.

**C4.5 (economics as background, not cause). Complicates; would be against if C4.5 were extended past its period.** For 1960–2011 the authors treat socioeconomic development as the *driver*. It explains "between 35% and 58%" of the change, almost four times the next factor (santos.12); it precedes individualism by ten years and mediates the effect of time (santos.12, santos.11). They conclude this "lend[s] support to a causal relationship" (santos.11). That is the opposite of "background, not cause" for the modern period. But: (1) C4.5 is about the *origin* of the calling ethos (16th–17th c.), which this study does not touch, and the authors name distal historical causes they did not test (santos.16); (2) they concede the MLM results "do not necessarily imply a causal relationship" and that development explains the rise only "in part" (santos.11); (3) they say cultural transmission "likely plays a role", and that changes in "norms and institutions" may then "promote" individualism (santos.15). The fair statement: the data favour an economic-structural account of *spread* since 1960, and leave room for culture as transmitter and amplifier. A purely cultural-cause story for the late period is weakened; Weber's thesis for the origin is untouched. (The authors' "can be attributed" reads variance explained, R²m, as cause; that is stronger than a correlation in panel data warrants. My observation, not theirs.)

**Dating. Since 1960; no evidence on acceleration or plateau.** Practices are from census rounds 1960–2011, values from WVS waves over a shorter span (santos.06). The authors report a single linear slope, and say their analyses "were constrained to linear models of change" with "a limited number of data points" per country (santos.16). The figures draw only a line from each country's first to last point (santos.09). So the study cannot say whether the rise sped up (e.g. after 1980) or levelled off. Caution on the headline: the "about 12 percent" increase (santos.09, .13) sits next to R²m = .12 for year in both models, which is variance explained, not a measured 12 percent rise in anything. Do not quote it as a magnitude.

**Exceptions and caveats. Against a uniform story.** Practices fell in Cameroon, Malawi, Malaysia and Mali; values fell in Armenia, China, Croatia, Ukraine and Uruguay; several countries hardly changed (santos.10, .14). Most of these were among the least developed, which the authors read as confirming the development account, but China falls on both indices despite rapid growth, and they leave it open (santos.14). Climate stress pushed *against* individualism in poorer countries (santos.12). They warn that IC "may be more multifaceted than previously believed" and that "some aspects of individualism may deviate" from the general pattern (santos.16). A single index can hide a rising strand and a falling one.

**Collectivism persists.** "Cultural differences remain sizeable up to this day" (santos.01, .10); between-country differences are 54 percent of the variance in practices and 30 percent in values (santos.10). And the method's own note: individualism and collectivism may be "independent from each other" at the individual level (santos.06), so a person can rise on one without falling on the other. For the thesis this means the data show a global drift in one direction while the old frames remain strongly in place in most of the world: the opposite of a frame "admitting no rival".

**C7.2 (isolation, rootlessness, asociality). For on isolation in living arrangements; against on asociality.** Living alone, older adults living alone and smaller households rise (santos.07). That is isolation in the plain demographic sense, and Tier 2 for the "rootless" half. But the values index counts a *rise in the importance of friends* relative to family as individualism (santos.08): relation is redirected from kin to chosen ties, not lost. Living alone is not the same as being asocial. And the theory the authors cite says the reliance on the group really falls with development (santos.04), which reads the change as a response to a changed situation, not as a Self that hides its dependence (C5.7 complicates).

**C8.6 (the word "self-expression").** A warning for sources, not a test. In this literature "self-expression" means choosing political voice (more say, free speech) as a national goal (santos.08). It is not the consumer's word of C8.6, and data labelled "self-expression" (Inglehart and successors) must not be read as evidence for it without looking at the items.

**C6.3 and C6.6.** The authors name "migration to frontiers" (Kitayama et al. 2010) as a distal explanation they did not test (santos.16): a lead for C6.3 (a Tier 2 frontier study to read), not evidence. On C6.6 (the Self helped America become an empire) the study's arrow runs the other way, from development to individualism, for 1960–2011 only. It neither supports nor tests the claim.

**Weight under the evidence standard.** One Tier 2 study. It can stand as the evidence for how widespread, and when for a claim about individualist *practices and values* since 1960. It cannot stand in the evidence audit as Tier 2 for C1.2's "calcified into a totalizing dogma" or for C5.14, because it does not measure the thing those claims name. To use it there, the essay needs a Tier 1 critic who argues that these practices *are* the dogma (or a data source that measures recognized authority, e.g. WVS items on obedience, religion, deference to parents), and it must answer the global pattern, which cuts against the Western and American focus.

The effect of socioeconomic development also held when controlling for year, disaster frequency, pathogen prevalence, and climate.

Development (white-collar work, prestige, schooling, income, cities) is the main predictor; education has the largest single R²m (.60 for practices, line 834). "Can be attributed" turns variance explained into cause, which the analysis does not establish. If job and schooling are what move individualism, the host's conditions are those of any developed economy, not America's alone.

### santos.13 · Discussion, ms. pp. 22–23 — "a shift toward greater individualism is not confined to the developed world"

- **Claims:** C1.2 (against, for the post-1960 rise as specifically Western or American), C6.1 (complicates), C4.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** Discussion, ms. p. 22, lines 930–936; ms. p. 23, lines 940–956
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Is the rise of individualism a global phenomenon? Our analysis of data across 51 years and 77 countries suggests that the answer is yes. Thirty-four (out of 41) countries showed a substantial rise in individualist practices. Thirty-seven (out of 52) countries showed a similar rise on a subset of markers assessing individualist values. The IC change for practices and values [p. 23] were similar at around 12%. Overall, these results show that a shift toward greater individualism is not confined to the developed world.
>
> Increasing individualism appears to be linked to several previously theorized sources of cultural variation, including socioeconomic development, disaster frequency, pathogen prevalence, and climatic variations. Notably, the last three factors only affected individualistic practices, not values. Of these ecological dimensions, socioeconomic development emerged as the key predictor, explaining between 35 and 58% of the variance in IC-related change over time. Moreover, we found that changes in socioeconomic development mediated the effect of time on individualism, and increases in socioeconomic development preceded increases in individualism.

The plainest "against" for C1.2 as a claim about *where* the change happens after 1960. Here the 35–58% is correctly called "variance". C1.2 can still hold as a claim about level or about an earlier and more extreme form, but this paper cannot show either.

### santos.14 · Discussion, ms. p. 23 — the exceptions; "China is an exception to this pattern"

- **Claims:** C1.2 (complicates), C4.5 (complicates: development does not explain China)
- **Location:** Discussion, ms. p. 23; lines 962–978
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> We observed a few exceptions to the global rise in individualism. Cameroon, Malawi, Malaysia, and Mali showed a decline in individualistic practices and Armenia, China, Croatia, Ukraine, and Uruguay showed a non-negligible decline in individualistic values. Also, several countries did not change much over time (see Supplementary Analyses). Consistent with the observation that socioeconomic development is driving the rise in individualism, most of these countries were among the lowest in socioeconomic development over the same time period. China is an exception to this pattern, showing a decrease in IC-practices and -values, respectively. Notably, China has a complex socioeconomic history, making it worthwhile to investigate them in more detail in future research.

The exceptions mostly fit the economic story, but not China, where individualism fell on both measures during fast growth. A case where politics or culture seems to override economics: a counterweight to a purely economic reading, and a reminder that a state can push the other way.

### santos.15 · Discussion, ms. pp. 23–24 — "cultural transmission likely plays a role"; "norms and institutions that reflect and promote an individualistic orientation"

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates: culture as transmitter), C1.2 (complicates: a route to calcification, untested), C8.6 (no bearing)
- **Location:** Discussion, ms. p. 23, lines 980–984; ms. p. 24, lines 988–1006
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> In the current work cultural changes in IC are mostly viewed as evoked responses, whereby environmental cues or conditions lead to adaptive behavioral and psychological responses. However, cultural transmission likely plays a role in the phenomena we observed. [p. 24] Ecologically-driven changes in individual responses may also lead to changes in norms and institutions that reflect and promote an individualistic orientation. The authors' model is adaptive response; they grant culture a second role, as norms and institutions that "promote" what the economy began. That is the nearest this paper comes to the thesis's "calcified": an orientation built into institutions. But it is a suggestion for future work, not a finding.

### santos.16 · Discussion, caveats, ms. pp. 24–25 — distal causes, "migration to frontiers"; "more multifaceted"; "constrained to linear models"

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates: origins untested), C6.3 (no bearing: a lead), C1.2 (complicates: dating and shape cannot be read), C5.14 (complicates: one index may hide strands)
- **Location:** Discussion, ms. p. 24, lines 1008–1032; ms. p. 25, lines 1036–1038
- **Checked:** excerpt (PDF text)

> Before concluding, let us consider some caveats. First, the present work focused on the role of relatively proximal ecological factors for changes in individualism-collectivism. There are also several distal historical explanations that have been advanced to explain variations and potentially shifts in IC, including modes of subsistence (e.g., Talhelm et al., 2014) and migration to frontiers (Kitayama et al., 2010). Ecological factors might drive such trends or mediate the effects of such events on IC. [...] Further, though present research focused on overall shifts of common features of IC over time, some aspects of individualism may deviate from these general patterns (Hamamura, 2012; Kashima, 2014; Kitayama et al., 2010). Indeed, there is emerging evidence that IC may be more multifaceted than previously believed (Vignoles et al., 2016). Moreover, we had a limited number of data points available per country, and our analyses were constrained to linear models of change. With the prospect of greater availability of cross-temporal data, future [p. 25] work may explore more fine-grained models integrating social ecology and a multi-faceted IC construct, making the study of cultural change an exciting scientific endeavor in the years ahead.

The authors' own limits, and they bear on every claim above: distal history (where Weber and Turner live) is left out; the single index may hide aspects that "deviate"; the models are linear. Kitayama et al. 2010 (frontier) and Vignoles et al. 2016 (many-faceted selfhood) are leads for C6.3 and for what a "Self" measure would need.
