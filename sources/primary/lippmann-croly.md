# Lippmann, *Drift and Mastery* (1914); Croly, *The Promise of American Life* (1909)

- **Edition used:** original English. Lippmann: New York, Mitchell Kennerley, 1914 (file lines 21, 54–55). Croly: New York, The Macmillan Company, November 1909 (file lines 33–40; the file is the 1912 reprint).
- **Text file:** `texts/lippmann-drift-and-mastery.txt` (Gutenberg #79553), `texts/croly-promise-american-life.txt` (Gutenberg #14422); both clean. Neither file keeps the original page numbers, so passages are cited by chapter and section plus file line.
- **Citation form:** Lippmann by chapter (Introduction, I–XVI); Croly by chapter and section (e.g. V.II), with section titles.
- **Claims to test:** C9.6, C9.8, C6.13, C6.7 (also touched: C8.5)

## Reading summary

**Read.** Lippmann: Introduction, chs. II ("New Incentives"), III ("The Magic of Property"), VII ("A Nation of Villagers", second half), the close of VIII, the close of IX ("Drift"), XIV ("Mastery"), XV ("Modern Communion"), XVI ("Fact and Fancy"), and the labor passage in V. There is no chapter called "The Promise of the Corporation"; the corporation chapters are II and III. Not read: I, IV, most of V, VI, the first half of VII, VIII, X–XIII. Croly: ch. I.I–II (opening), V.I–II, V.IV (labor union passage), V.VI, VI.II, VII.III (opening), the close of VII.IV, XII.II–III (opening), XIII.II (opening), XIII.IV (close), XIII.V. Not read: chs. II–IV, VIII–XI, most of VI, VII, XII and XIII.

**"Hamiltonian means to Jeffersonian ends."** The phrase does not occur in the Croly file (grep, across line breaks). It is a later summary. Croly's own wording is the attempt to unite "the Hamiltonian principle of national political responsibility and efficiency with a frank democratic purpose" (VI.II, lines 5678–5679), and he says the union will do "more harm to the Jeffersonian group of political ideas than it will to the Hamiltonian" (VII.IV, lines 7872–7873).

**C9.6: values stated, not denied.** Neither founding text claims value-free objectivity. Croly names his end in the first chapter, the "democratic ideal" (I.I), calls American faith in the nation "religious" (line 128), says a state policy of being "impartial" has "no real existence" (XII.II), and ends on "the religion of human brotherhood" (XIII.V). Lippmann (1914) goes further: a vision "will inevitably reveal the bias of its author" (Introduction); science means asking an economist about "his class bias" (XIV); the merely disinterested student "is a pedant", and trying to be "absolutely objective" makes us "the unconscious victims of our wishes" (XVI). Both also diagnose and reject the closed technocratic class: Lippmann's "benevolent governing class" of State Socialism (XVI), Croly's "favored minority" whose standards turn "narrow, artificial, and lax" (VII.III). **But there is an in-group, and it is openly owned:** the professions' "esprit de corps" (Lippmann II), "Those of us who hold such a belief" against the "village" Democrats (VII), a "fellowship with scientists whose books we cannot read" (XV); in Croly, specialists who "mold their followers after their own likeness" and make the well-meaning "willing prisoners" (XIII.IV–V). Croly also says the experts' "self-assertion is analogous to that of the millionaire and the 'Boss'" (V.VI): a group interest, not neutral reason.

**C9.8: the corporation is a machine and a school, not a Self.** Lippmann's corporation empties ownership (the shareholder "as a person is of no account whatever", III), hands management to salaried men for whom profit "is not their personal motive" (II), and trains them through a professional "discipline" (II). He calls the "collective mind" above individuals "simply a myth" (VII). Croly's corporations are "Children [...] of the traditional American individualistic institutions" (V.II), efficient mechanisms to be recognized, regulated and, if they last, converted into "express economic agents of the whole community" (XII.III). In Croly the collective that absorbs a personality is the labor union ("His own personality is merged in that of the union", V.IV), and the bearer of purpose is the nation. The unbounded will in Croly belongs to the pre-corporate competitive businessman (V.II), and the trust is his escape from that warfare.

**C6.7: the pioneer ideal is obsolete in both.** Croly: "The pioneer had enjoyed his day, and his day was over" (V.I); the Boss and the tainted millionaire practise an idea that "during the pioneer period had not worked badly" (VI.II). Lippmann: business now needs more than the "ambitious office boy, who saves his money" (II), and the "rugged virtues" of property "have gone a-glimmering" (III). **The complication:** in Lippmann the vocabulary of the man "on the make" is kept by Wilson's small businessmen and farmers against the trusts (VII), not by corporate leaders.

**C6.13.** Lippmann in 1914 already says the old foundations "survive only as habits or by default" (Introduction), so the wearing-away is dated before 1914. He sees a new check rising for the business class: professional training, ethics and esprit de corps (II). That is a redirected check C6.13 does not list. Croly (1909) has the union as the worker's strongest attachment, "more important [...] than his attachment to the American ideal" (V.IV). That cuts against C6.13's working class losing the union to isolation, unless the loss is dated after 1909 (as in *Middletown*).

**Lippmann 1914 vs. 1929.** The shift is sharp. In 1914 disinterestedness is a fault ("If the student is merely disinterested, he is a pedant"), and the ideal is "interesting truth critical of one's interest". Science is the "discipline of democracy" and a forward-looking "substitute for authority" (XIV), and the mood is mastery and purpose. In 1929 (`lippmann-preface-to-morals.md`, lippmann.14, .15, .17) disinterestedness is the core of "high religion", and the executive becomes "a relatively disinterested person". So if the claim of detached objectivity enters this line of thought, it enters with the 1929 Lippmann, and even there it is offered as a moral ideal, not as a denial of values. Two lines run straight through: the salaried manager as no longer a profiteer (1914 II, 1929 XII), and the self thrown on its own resources ("Never before have we had to rely so completely upon ourselves", 1914 IX; compare lippmann.02).

**Aside (C8.5).** Croly uses "individual self-expression" in 1909 as a democratic aim for persons (VII.IV, XIII.II, XIII.V). It is the producer's or expert's idiom (excellence of work), not the consumer's. This fits status §8 (self-expression belongs to the producer through 1930) but pushes back the date at which the idiom is applied to persons.

## Passages

### lippmann-drift.01 · Introduction — "the rock of ages [...] has been blasted for us"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Introduction; `texts/lippmann-drift-and-mastery.txt` lines 355–362, 400–403
- **Checked:** text file

> The issues that we face are very different from those of the last century and a half. The difference, I think, might be summed up roughly this way: those who went before inherited a conservatism and overthrew it; we inherit freedom, and have to use it. The sanctity of property, the patriarchal family, hereditary caste, the dogma of sin, obedience to authority,--the rock of ages, in brief, has been blasted for us. Those who are young to-day are born into a world in which the foundations of the older order survive only as habits or by default.

> The battle for us, in short, does not lie against crusted prejudice, but against the chaos of a new freedom.

By 1914 the old checks are already gone, or live on only as habit. The wearing-away is earlier than C6.13's 1890s–1930 window implies, and Lippmann treats it as finished.

### lippmann-drift.02 · Introduction — "a human hypothesis, not an oracular revelation"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** Introduction; lines 430–436, 484–491
- **Checked:** text file

> All we can do is to search the world as we find it, extricate the forces that seem to move it, and surround them with criticism and suggestion. Such a vision will inevitably reveal the bias of its author; that is to say it will be a human hypothesis, not an oracular revelation.

> All the thinking done by troubled dead men for the cocksure living; no class to consider but your own; no work that counts but yours; every party but your party composed of fools and rascals; only a formula to accept and a specific fight to win,--it would be easy. It might work on the moon.

The founding text of mastery opens by owning its bias and mocking in-group thinking. It does not claim objectivity.

### lippmann-drift.03 · ch. II, "New Incentives" — the office boy obsolete; "an esprit de corps"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.6 (complicates), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 1236–1250, 1259–1266
- **Checked:** text file

> Just as it is no longer possible to become a physician by living with doctors, just as law cannot be grasped by starting as a clerk in some attorney's office, so business requires a greater preparation than a man can get by being a bright, observant, studious, ambitious office boy, who saves his money and is good to his mother.

> What it will mean to have business administered by men with a professional training is a rather difficult speculation. That it is a very far-reaching psychological change, I have no doubt. The professions bring with them a fellowship in interest, a standard of ethics, an esprit de corps, and a decided discipline. They break up that sense of sullen privacy which made the old-fashioned business man so impervious to new facts and so shockingly ignorant of the larger demands of civilized life. [...] But doctors and engineers and professional men, generally, have something more than a desire to accumulate and outshine their neighbors. [...] the civilizing passions are given a chance to temper the primal desire to have and to hold and to conquer.

The self-made type is declared obsolete (C6.7). The new managerial class is an avowed in-group with its own ethics (C9.6), and the corporation is a school of discipline that checks private ambition, not a Self (C9.8).

### lippmann-drift.04 · ch. II, "New Incentives" — managers "not profiteers"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II; lines 1212–1223
- **Checked:** text file

> The real news about business, it seems to me, is that it is being administered by men who are not profiteers. The managers are on salary, divorced from ownership and from bargaining. They represent the revolution in business incentives at its very heart. For they conduct gigantic enterprises and they stand outside the higgling of the market, outside the shrewdness and strategy of competition. The motive of profit is not their personal motive. That is an astounding change. [...] They have to live on their salaries, and hope for promotion, but their day's work is not measured in profit.

Inside the giant corporation, Lippmann sees the self-seeking motive weakening, not growing. This is the 1914 root of the 1929 "relatively disinterested" executive (lippmann.15).

### lippmann-drift.05 · ch. III, "The Magic of Property" — "rugged virtues" gone

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III; lines 1363–1372
- **Checked:** text file

> For where in the name of sanity have all the courage, foresight, initiative gone to, what has happened to all the rugged virtues that are supposed to be inherent in the magic of property?

> They have gone a-glimmering with the revolutionary change that the great industry has produced. Those personal virtues belong to an earlier age when men really had some personal contact with their property. But to-day the central condition of business is that capital shall be impersonal, "liquid," "mobile." The modern shareholder as a person is of no account whatever.

The proprietor's virtues are tied to a vanished age (C6.7). The corporation is pictured as impersonal capital in which the owning person counts for nothing. That is the opposite of an organization that acts as one sovereign Self.

### lippmann-drift.06 · ch. III, "The Magic of Property" — "without democratic interruption"; rule by experts refused

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III; lines 1464–1477, 1488–1497
- **Checked:** text file

> It is due to the fact that the management is autocratic, that administrators are highly paid, and given power adequate to their responsibility. When governments are willing to pursue that course, they can be just as efficient as private management. The construction of the Panama Canal is a classic example of what government can do if it is ready to centralize power and let it work without democratic interruption.

> The real problem of collectivism is the difficulty of combining popular control with administrative power. [...] What would remain for discussion would be the conflict between democracy and centralized authority.

> It is obvious that the trusts have created a demand for a new type of business man--for a man whose motives resemble those of the applied scientist and whose responsibility is that of a public servant. [...] The notion seems to be that workers, politicians, consumers and the rest are to have no real part in the glorious revolution which is to be consummated for their benefit.

The technocratic temptation is here: he admires power that works without democratic interruption. But he names its conflict with democracy openly and rejects a revolution made by experts for everyone else.

### lippmann-drift.07 · ch. VII, "A Nation of Villagers" — Wilson's "man on the make"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII; lines 2607–2616, 2719–2727
- **Checked:** text file

> His feeling is, as he says, for "the man on the make." "For my part, I want the pigmy to have a chance to come out" ... "Just let some of the youngsters I know have a chance and they'll give these gentlemen points. Lend them a little money. They can't get any now. See to it that when they have got a local market they can't be squeezed out of it."

> I submit that it is an unworthy dream. I submit that the intelligent men of my generation can find a better outlet for their energies than in making themselves masters of little businesses. [...] They have the whole new field of industrial statesmanship before them, and those who prefer the egotism of some little business are not the ones whose ambitions we need most to cultivate.

The self-made ideal is outdated here, as C6.7 says. But its 1912–14 carrier is Wilson's small-business, village Democracy using government against the trusts, not the corporate leaders. Lippmann's corporate side speaks of plan and statesmanship.

### lippmann-drift.08 · ch. VII, "A Nation of Villagers" — the village out-group; "Those of us"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII; lines 2748–2759, 2783–2788
- **Checked:** text file

> its temper has always been hostile to specialization and expert knowledge, because it admires a very primitive man-to-man democracy. [...] But at the same time his speeches are marked with contempt for the specialist: they play up quite obviously to the old democratic notion that any man can do almost any job.

> It looks to the infusion of scientific method, the careful application of administrative technique, the organization and education of the consumer for control, the discipline of labor for an increasing share of the management. Those of us who hold such a belief are pushed from behind by what we think is an irresistible economic development, and lured by a future which we think is possible.

The closest these texts come to C9.6's in-group/out-group: the expert-minded "us" against the village culture of Bryan and Wilson. But the group names its belief as a belief ("what we think"), and it is not hidden.

### lippmann-drift.09 · ch. VII, "A Nation of Villagers" — the "collective mind" a myth

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VII; lines 2817–2831
- **Checked:** text file

> It is this realization which has made men speculate on the development of what they call a "collective mind." They hope that somehow we shall develop an intelligence larger than the individual.

> I see no evidence for that. There are no minds but human minds so far as our problems go. It seems to me that this notion of a collective mind over and above men and women is simply a myth created to meet difficulties greater than men and women are as yet capable of handling. It is a _deus ex machina_ invented to cover an enormous need,--a hope that something outside ourselves will do our work for us.

The chief Progressive advocate of large organization explicitly denies it any mind or self above its members. Against reading the Progressive case for organization as "the organization as a singular Self" (SPEC §9.3).

### lippmann-drift.10 · ch. XIV, "Mastery" — "his class bias"; science as self-government

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XIV; lines 4871–4882, 4886–4902
- **Checked:** text file

> We ask now of an economist, who his friends are, what his ambitions, his class bias. When one thinker exalts absolute freedom, another violent repression, we have ceased to take such ideas at their face value [...].

> The power of criticizing the scientific mind is, I believe, our best guarantee for the progress of scientific discovery. This is the inner sanctuary of civilized power. For when science becomes its own critic it assures its own future. It is able, then, to attack the source of error itself; to forestall its own timidities, and control its own bias.

> A mere emotion of futurity, that sense of "vital urge" which is so common to-day, will fritter itself away unless it comes under the scientific discipline, where men use language accurately, know fact from fancy, search out their own prejudice, are willing to learn from failures, and do not shrink from the long process of close observation. Then only shall we have a substitute for authority. [...] There is nothing accidental then in the fact that democracy in politics is the twin-brother of scientific thinking. They had to come together. As absolutism falls, science arises. It _is_ self-government.

Mastery's science is defined by searching out one's own bias, and it serves a stated value, democracy. Against C9.6 as written. Note that science is offered as a "substitute for authority", the frame that replaces the old ones.

### lippmann-drift.11 · ch. XV, "Modern Communion" — devotion, loyalty, fellowship

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XV; lines 5020–5026, 5093–5095, 5106–5109
- **Checked:** text file

> This work has aroused in many men the old sense of cosmic wonder, and called forth devotion to impersonal ends. Nor can it be denied that in the study of institutions, in laboratories of research, there have appeared the same loyalty and courage to which the old religions could point as to their finest flower.

> We begin to recognize a vague spirit which may suggest a common purpose. We live in a fellowship with scientists whose books we cannot read, with educators whose work we do not understand.

> They enable men to share their hopes with strangers, to travel about and talk to people of widely different professions and origin, yet to find the assurance that they are part of a great undertaking.

The scientific-professional class is offered openly as a replacement communion, with loyalty, devotion and fellowship. That is a belief system plainly avowed, not a denied one. It complicates C9.6: the in-group exists, but its faith is on the surface.

### lippmann-drift.12 · ch. XVI, "Fact and Fancy" — "merely disinterested, he is a pedant"; "absolutely objective"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XVI; lines 5403–5416, 5570–5574
- **Checked:** text file

> One of the myths that modern critics are overthrowing is the notion that science is a passionless pursuit of dead facts. For even in the most "disinterested" inquiry, there is, as Bertrand Russell says, some interest that determines the direction of our curiosity. [...] Science is a very human thing. It springs from a need, is directed by curiosity to choose an interesting field of study, and in that field seeks results that concern men. The ideal of science, it seems to me, is to seek interesting truth critical of one's interest. If the student is merely disinterested, he is a pedant; if he seeks only what catches his passing fancy, he is romantic. The true scientist is inspired by a vision without being the victim of it.

> But if we try to ignore the desire that moves our thought, if we try in short to be "absolutely objective," we succeed only in accumulating useless facts, or we become the unconscious victims of our wishes. If thinking didn't serve desire, it would be the most useless occupation in the world.

The strongest "against" in this file. Lippmann rejects claimed objectivity, and he predicts C9.6's mechanism himself: the would-be objective thinker becomes the unconscious victim of his wishes. It is also the sharpest contrast with his 1929 ideal of disinterestedness (lippmann.14).

### lippmann-drift.13 · ch. XVI, "Fact and Fancy" — State Socialism's "benevolent governing class"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XVI; lines 5606–5609, 5628–5637, 5773–5775
- **Checked:** text file

> Even in the utopias which pretend to be democratic, that is, where the citizens vote, the assumption always exists that the citizens vote as the dreamer would have them vote. He simply calls his will the will of the people.

> The bureaucratic dreams of reformers often bear a striking resemblance to the honest fantasies of the Utopians. What we are coming to call "State Socialism" is in fact an attempt to impose a benevolent governing class on humanity. Oh, for wise and powerful officials to bring order out of chaos, end the "muddle," and make men clean, sober and civic-minded. There is no real understanding of democracy in the State Socialist, for he doesn't attempt to build with the assent and voluntary coöperation of men and women. But he avoids the laborious and disheartening method of popular education, and takes satisfaction in devising a ruling class, inspired by him, as a short-cut to perfection.

> Mastery, whether we like it or not, is an immense collaboration, in which all the promises of to-day will have their vote.

Lippmann already describes the C9.6 type: a ruling class that passes its own will off as the people's. He places it among bureaucratic reformers he opposes. The diagnosis is contemporary with the Progressives, so the type existed, but here as a target of the founding text, not its program.

### croly.01 · ch. I.I, "What Is the Promise of American Life?" — "committed to the realization of the democratic ideal"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I.I; `texts/croly-promise-american-life.txt` lines 286–302
- **Checked:** text file

> Such a promise is to be fulfilled, not by sanguine anticipations, not by a conservative imitation of past achievements, but by laborious, single-minded, clear-sighted, and fearless work. If the promising career of any individual is not determined by a specific and worthy purpose, it rapidly drifts into a mere pursuit of success; and even if such a pursuit is successful, whatever promise it may have had, is buried in the grave of its triumph. So it is with a nation. If its promise is anything more than a vision of power and success, that addition must derive its value from a purpose; because in the moral world the future exists only as a workshop in which a purpose is to be realized. [...] but the American nation is committed to a purpose which is not merely of historical manufacture. It is committed to the realization of the democratic ideal; and if its Promise is to be fulfilled, it must be prepared to follow whithersoever that ideal may lead.

The case for expert, national government starts from a declared end. Croly also warns that success without purpose is empty. This is the opposite of a technocracy that denies its values.

### croly.02 · ch. V.I, "The Contemporary Situation and Its Problems" — "his day was over"; "His individuality tended to disappear"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates), C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V.I; lines 3812–3837, 3868–3870
- **Checked:** text file

> The pioneer had enjoyed his day, and his day was over. The Jack-of-all-trades no longer possessed an important economic function. [...] Industrial economy demanded the expert with his high and special standards of achievement. [...] Like all experts, they had to depend, not upon mere energy, untutored enthusiasm, and good-will, but upon careful training and single-minded devotion to a special task, and at the same time proper provision had to be made for coördinating the results of this highly specialized work. More complete organization necessarily accompanied specialization. The expert became a part of a great industrial machine. His individuality tended to disappear in his work. His interests became those of a group.

> For a long time American social and economic conditions were not merely fluid, but consistent and homogeneous, and the vision of the pioneer was fulfilled. Nevertheless, this condition was essentially transient.

The pioneer ideal outlived its conditions (C6.7). The organization submerges the expert's individuality into a group interest. That is a machine absorbing selves, not an organization behaving as one Self (C9.8). The experts' interests are group interests (C9.6).

### croly.03 · ch. V.II, "The Development of the Business Specialist" — "this will had him in complete possession"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V.II; lines 3981–4004
- **Checked:** text file

> The business man in seeking to realize his ambitions and purposes was checked neither by government control nor social custom. He had nothing to do and nothing to consider except his own business advancement and success. [...] He could not conquer except by virtue of a strong, tenacious, adventurous, and unscrupulous will; and after he had conquered, this will had him in complete possession. The nearest thing in Croly to an unbounded Self: checked by neither state nor custom, possessed by its own will. But it is the individual competitive businessman before the trust, and the trust is described as his escape from that warfare (lines 4253–4255).

### croly.04 · ch. V.II — corporations as "Children [...] of the traditional American individualistic institutions"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V.II; lines 4325–4336
- **Checked:** text file

> The rich men and the big corporations have become too wealthy and powerful for their official standing in American life. They have not obeyed the laws. They have attempted to control the official makers, administrators, and expounders of the law. [...] In short, while their work has been constructive from an economic and industrial standpoint, it has made for political corruption and social disintegration. Children, as they are, of the traditional American individualistic institutions, ideas, and practices, they have turned on their parents and dealt them an ugly wound. Either these economic monsters will destroy the system of ideas, institutions, and practices out of which they have issued or else be destroyed by them.

Croly links the corporation to individualism by descent: it is individualism's offspring that has outgrown and turned on it. That supports C6.7's continuity and gives C9.8 a genealogy. But "monsters" too big for the law is a picture of power, not of a self-originating Self.

### croly.05 · ch. V.IV, "The Labor Union and the Democratic Tradition" — "merged in that of the union"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V.IV; lines 4699–4721
- **Checked:** text file

> He believes in unionism, consequently, as he believes in nothing else. He is, indeed, far more aggressively preoccupied with his class, as contrasted with his individual interests, than are his employers. He has no respect for the traditional American individualism as applied to his own social and economic standing. [...] His own personality is merged in that of the union. No individual has any rights as opposed to the interests of the union. [...] His attachment to his union has come to be the most important attachment of his life--more important in most cases than his attachment to the American ideal and to the national interest.

In 1909 the collective that takes up a person's personality is the union, not the corporation (C9.8). ### croly.06 · ch. V.VI, "American Democracy and the Social Problem" — experts' "self-assertion is analogous to that of the millionaire"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V.VI; lines 5106–5133
- **Checked:** text file

> The millionaire, the "Boss," the union laborer, and the lawyer, have all taken advantage of the loose American political organization to promote somewhat unscrupulously their own interests [...]. Technical experts of all kinds--engineers, men of letters, and artists--have all of them been asserting much more vigorously their own special interests and purposes. [...] But different as is the situation of these technical experts, the fundamental meaning of their self-assertion is analogous to that of the millionaire and the "Boss." The vast incoherent mass of the American people is falling into definite social groups, which restrict and define the mental outlook and social experience of their members. The best "for" in Croly: experts are an interest group like the others, and groups "restrict and define the mental outlook" of their members. But Croly says it in the open and does not hold experts above interest, so the denial C9.6 needs is missing. For C6.13: the redirection into class and occupational groups is already under way by 1909.

### croly.07 · ch. VI.II, "The Logic of Reform" — the pioneer idea, respectable then, ruinous now; the real Hamilton–Jefferson formula

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. VI.II; lines 5446–5460, 5605–5610, 5676–5680
- **Checked:** text file

> The unscrupulous energy of the "Boss" or the "tainted" millionaire is vitally related to the moral indifference of the "plain people." Both of them have been encouraged to believe by the nature of our traditional ideas and institutions that a man could be patriotic without being either public-spirited or disinterested. [...] Consequently when the "Boss" and the "tainted" millionaire took advantage of this situation to secure for themselves an unusually large amount of political and economic power, they were putting into practice an idea which traditionally had been entirely respectable, and which during the pioneer period had not worked badly.

> The experience of the last generation plainly shows that the American economic and social system cannot be allowed to take care of itself, and that the automatic harmony of the individual and the public interest, which is the essence of the Jeffersonian democratic creed, has proved to be an illusion.

> Above all, it may discover that the attempt to unite the Hamiltonian principle of national political responsibility and efficiency with a frank democratic purpose will give a new meaning to the Hamiltonian system of political ideas and a new power to democracy.

C6.7 in Croly's words: the big-money men practise the respectable pioneer idea after its conditions are gone. The program's formula (this, not the phrase "Hamiltonian means to Jeffersonian ends", which is not in the text) joins efficiency to a "frank democratic purpose". The values are stated.

### croly.08 · ch. VII.III, "Constructive Discrimination" — the "favored minority" grows lax

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII.III; lines 7128–7134, 7141–7152
- **Checked:** text file

> Only a comparatively small minority are capable at any one time of exercising political, economic, and civil liberties in an able, efficient, or thoroughly worthy manner; and a régime wrought for the benefit of such a minority would become at best a state, in which economic, political, and social power would be very unevenly distributed--a state like the Orleans Monarchy in France of the "Bourgeoisie" and the "Intellectuals."

> As long as it does endure, moreover, it tends to undermine the virtue of its own beneficiaries. The favored minority, feeling as they do tolerably sure of their position, can scarcely avoid a habit of making it somewhat too easy for one another. The political, economic, and intellectual leaders begin to be selected without any sufficient test of their efficiency. Some sort of a test continues to be required; but the standards which determine it drift into a condition of being narrow, artificial, and lax. Political, intellectual, and social leadership, in order to preserve its vitality needs a feeling of effective responsibility to a body of public opinion as wide, as varied, and as exacting as that of the whole community.

Croly predicts how a ruling minority of "Intellectuals" closes into an in-group whose standards turn lax. He makes answerability to the whole community the remedy. The in-group dynamic C9.6 describes is foreseen here, by the founder of the program, as a danger to guard against.

### croly.09 · ch. XII.II–III, "The Recognition of Industrial Organization"; "The Fruits of Industrial Organization" — no "impartial" policy; corporations as agents of the community

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against), C9.8 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XII.II, lines 13066–13070, 13138–13148; ch. XII.III, lines 13618–13631
- **Checked:** text file

> The central government in its policy toward the large corporations must adopt one of two courses. Either it must discriminate in their favor or it must discriminate against them. The third alternative--that of being what is called "impartial"--has no real existence; and it is essential that the illusory nature of a policy of impartiality should in the beginning be clearly understood.

> The process of industrial organization should be allowed to work itself out. Whenever the smaller competitor of the large corporation is unable to keep his head above water with his own exertions, he should be allowed to drown.

> A policy must be adopted of converting them into express economic agents of the whole community, and of gradually appropriating for the benefit of the community the substantial economic advantages which these corporations had succeeded in acquiring.

Croly says outright that state neutrality is an illusion and takes sides, hard ("allowed to drown"). Against C9.6's claimed impartiality. The corporation is a means to be made the community's agent, with no standing of its own as a source of value. Against C9.8 as Croly's view.

### croly.10 · ch. XIII.IV, "Means of Individual Emancipation" (close) — "willing prisoners"; leadership that "can scarcely go astray"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIII.IV; lines 16145–16156
- **Checked:** text file

> Others, although men of inferior achievement, are patriotic and well-intentioned in feeling; and they may little by little be brought to believe that patriotism in a democracy demands the sacrifice of selfish interests and the regeneration of individual rights. Men of this stamp can be made willing prisoners by able and aggressive leaders whose achievements have given them personal authority and whose practical programme is based upon a sound knowledge of the necessary limits of immediate national action. The disinterested and competent individual is formed for constructive leadership, just as the less competent and independent, but well-intentioned, individual is formed more or less faithfully to follow on behind. Such leadership, in a country whose traditions and ideals are sincerely democratic, can scarcely go astray.

This is the elitist side of the program, with leaders and followers and the follower as a "willing prisoner". The leaders are called "disinterested", but here that means not selfish, not value-free. The confidence that it "can scarcely go astray" contrasts with croly.08 and .11.

### croly.11 · ch. XIII.V, "Constructive Individualism" — the specialist who imposes himself and "mold[s] their followers"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIII.V; lines 16176–16180, 16249–16253, 16363–16373
- **Checked:** text file

> How are these eminent specialists, each of whom is admittedly pursuing unscrupulously his own special purpose, to be made serviceable in a coherent national democratic organization? How, indeed, are these specialists to get at the public whom they are supposed to lead?

> They themselves constitute the only efficient source of really formative education. In so far as a public is lacking, a public must be created. They must mold their followers after their own likeness--as all aspirants after the higher individual eminence have always been obliged to do.

> For the rest it is the particular business of the exceptional individual to impose himself on the public [...]. The danger which every peculiarly able individual specialist runs is that of overestimating the value of his own purpose and achievements, and so of establishing a false and delusive relation between his own world and the larger world of human affairs and interests.

Croly's expert is not a neutral instrument. He is a self with "his own special purpose" who makes a public in his own image. That is close to the Self-as-source the essay is about, and Croly names its danger: overvaluing his own purpose, so that his world falls out of true with the larger world. Values here are asserted, not denied.

### croly.12 · ch. XIII.V, "Constructive Individualism" (close) — "the religion of human brotherhood"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against)
- **Location:** ch. XIII.V; lines 16573–16589
- **Checked:** text file

> It is very easy and in a sense perfectly true to declare that democracy needs for its fulfillment a peculiarly high standard of moral behavior; and it is even more true to declare that a democratic scheme of moral values reaches its consummate expression in the religion of human brotherhood. Such a religion can be realized only through the loving-kindness which individuals feel toward their fellow-men and particularly toward their fellow-countrymen [...]. But--at present--a profound sense of human brotherhood is no substitute for specific efficiency. [...] Consummate faith itself is no substitute for good work.

The book ends in an openly religious statement of value. Efficiency comes first in time, but it is subordinate to faith. The Progressive founder is not a nihilist in the strict sense C9.6 means.

### croly.13 · ch. VII.IV, XIII.II — "individual self-expression" as a democratic aim (1909)

- **Claims:** C8.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII.IV, "The Bridge between Democracy and Nationality", lines 7704–7711; ch. XIII.II, "Conditions of Individual Emancipation", lines 14968–14971
- **Checked:** text file

> A democracy dedicated to individual and social betterment is necessarily individualist as well as socialist. It has little interest in the mere multiplication of average individuals, except in so far as such multiplication is necessary to economic and political efficiency; but it has the deepest interest in the development of a higher quality of individual self-expression. There are two indispensable economic conditions of qualitative individual self-expression.

> It is, then, essential to recognize that the individual American will never obtain a sufficiently complete chance of self-expression, until the American nation has earnestly undertaken and measurably achieved the realization of its collective purpose.

The idiom is applied to persons in 1909, not after 1928. But it is the producer's sense (quality of work, tied to a national purpose), not the consumer's. This fits the status §8 finding but complicates C8.5's dating of when the idiom passes to persons.
