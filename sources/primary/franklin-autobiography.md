# Franklin, *Autobiography* (1771–90)

- **Edition used:** ed. Frank Woodworth Pine (New York: Henry Holt, 1916), with an appendix including an abridged "The Way to Wealth"
- **Text file:** `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): ed. Leonard W. Labaree et al. (Yale, 1964), or J. A. Leo Lemay and P. M. Zall, Norton Critical Edition
- **Citation form:** Pine's chapter (I–XIX, an editorial division) + Franklin's part (I: Twyford 1771; II: Passy 1784; III: Philadelphia 1788), plus this file's line
- **Claims to test:** C4.31, C4.4, C4.5, C4.30, C6.*, C8.2

Italics in the text file (`_word_`) are given as *word*. The chapter titles are Pine's. The phrase "self-made men" is the editor's (franklin.14), not Franklin's; it does not occur in Franklin's own text in this file.

## Passages

### franklin.01 · Ch. I (Part I) — "emerged from the poverty and obscurity [...] with the blessing of God"

- **Claims:** C6.* (complicates), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I ¶1; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 552–558
- **Checked:** text file

> Having emerged from the poverty and obscurity in which I was born and bred, to a state of affluence and some degree of reputation in the world, and having gone so far through life with a considerable share of felicity, the conducing means I made use of, which with the blessing of God so well succeeded, my posterity may like to know, as they may find some of them suitable to their own situations, and therefore fit to be imitated.

The founding sentence of the self-made story. It credits the rise to "means" *and* to "the blessing of God," and offers it as a model to be imitated. The self is the means, not the source.

### franklin.02 · Ch. I (Part I) — "thank God for his vanity"; "His kind providence"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against), C4.31 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I ¶¶4–5; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 586–616
- **Checked:** text file

> Most people dislike vanity in others, whatever share they have of it themselves; but I give it fair quarter wherever I meet with it, being persuaded that it is often productive of good to the possessor, and to others that are within his sphere of action; and therefore, in many cases, it would not be altogether absurd if a man were to thank God for his vanity among the other comforts of life. [...] And now I speak of thanking God, I desire with all humility to acknowledge that I owe the mentioned happiness of my past life to His kind providence, which lead me to the means I used and gave them success.

Mandeville domesticated: vanity is useful to oneself "and to others" (Franklin met Mandeville at his London club; ch. VI, lines 2205–2207). But it sits inside providence, which "lead me to the means I used." He is not his own origin.

### franklin.03 · Ch. VII (Part I) — Deism found "not very useful"; actions "forbidden *because* they were bad for us"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 2724–2780
- **Checked:** text file

> I began to doubt of Revelation itself. [...] I soon became a thorough Deist. My arguments perverted some others, particularly Collins and Ralph; but, each of them having afterwards wrong'd me greatly without the least compunction, and recollecting Keith's conduct towards me (who was another free-thinker), and my own towards Vernon and Miss Read, which at times gave me great trouble, I began to suspect that this doctrine, tho' it might be true, was not very useful.

> I grew convinc'd that *truth*, *sincerity* and *integrity* in dealings between man and man were of the utmost importance to the felicity of life; and I form'd written resolutions, which still remain in my journal book, to practice them ever while I lived. Revelation had indeed no weight with me, as such; but I entertain'd an opinion that, though certain actions might not be bad *because* they were forbidden by it, or good *because* it commanded them, yet probably these actions might be forbidden *because* they were bad for us, or commanded *because* they were beneficial to us, in their own natures, all the circumstances of things considered.

Separation from revealed religion, by private judgment, as C4.30 says. But the test that takes him back from free-thinking is social: how free-thinkers treat one another "between man and man." The separated belief is judged by what it does to community.

### franklin.04 · Ch. VII (Part I) — the Junto, "a club of mutual improvement"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C6.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VII; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 2820–2832
- **Checked:** text file

> I should have mentioned before, that, in the autumn of the preceding year, I had form'd most of my ingenious acquaintance into a club of mutual improvement, which was called the Junto; we met on Friday evenings. The rules that I drew up required that every member, in his turn, should produce one or more queries on any point of Morals, Politics, or Natural Philosophy, to be discuss'd by the company; [...] Our debates were to be under the direction of a president, and to be conducted in the sincere spirit of inquiry after truth, without fondness for dispute, or desire of victory; and, to prevent warmth, all expressions of positiveness in opinions, or direct contradiction, were after some time made contraband, and prohibited under small pecuniary penalties.

Self-improvement is done in company and under rules. The community is voluntary and made, not inherited. That fits C6.2 (thin inherited structures), but it shows the gap filled by association rather than left empty (compare Tocqueville).

### franklin.05 · Ch. VIII (Part II) — the library: "put myself as much as I could out of sight"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (against), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3344–3359
- **Checked:** text file

> The objections and reluctances I met with in soliciting the subscriptions, made me soon feel the impropriety of presenting one's self as the proposer of any useful project, that might be suppos'd to raise one's reputation in the smallest degree above that of one's neighbours, when one has need of their assistance to accomplish that project. I therefore put myself as much as I could out of sight, and stated it as a scheme of a *number of friends*, who had requested me to go about and propose it to such as they thought lovers of reading. [...] The present little sacrifice of your vanity will afterwards be amply repaid.

The self-made man hides himself in front of his neighbours, because standing out costs their help. The self is managed for its reception by the community. That is a performance, but of sameness, not of difference.

### franklin.06 · Ch. VIII (Part II) — "Seest thou a man diligent in his calling"

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3361–3378
- **Checked:** text file

> Reading was the only amusement I allow'd myself. I spent no time in taverns, games, or frolicks of any kind; and my industry in my business continu'd as indefatigable as it was necessary. [...] My original habits of frugality continuing, and my father having, among his instructions to me when a boy, frequently repeated a proverb of Solomon, "Seest thou a man diligent in his calling, he shall stand before kings, he shall not stand before mean men," I from thence considered industry as a means of obtaining wealth and distinction, which encourag'd me [...]

The calling text, handed down by a Puritan father, survives, and so do the discipline and frugality. But election has gone. Industry is "a means of obtaining wealth and distinction," not a sign of grace. C4.4's later-Calvinist "proof" is one step back: here the method is kept and the anxiety about election is dropped (see .07).

### franklin.07 · Ch. VIII (Part II) — election "unintelligible"; "rather to make us Presbyterians than good citizens"

- **Claims:** C4.30 (for), C4.31 (against), C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VIII; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3399–3435, 3450–3455
- **Checked:** text file

> I had been religiously educated as a Presbyterian; and though some of the dogmas of that persuasion, such as the *eternal decrees of God*, *election*, *reprobation*, *etc.*, appeared to me unintelligible, others doubtful, and I early absented myself from the public assemblies of the sect, Sunday being my studying day, I never was without some religious principles. I never doubted, for instance, the existence of the Deity; that he made the world, and govern'd it by his Providence; that the most acceptable service of God was the doing good to man; that our souls are immortal; and that all crime will be punished, and virtue rewarded, either here or hereafter. These I esteem'd the essentials of every religion [...] serv'd principally to divide us, and make us unfriendly to one another.

> [...] since not a single moral principle was inculcated or enforc'd, their aim seeming to be rather to make us Presbyterians than good citizens.

> I had some years before compos'd a little Liturgy, or form of prayer, for my own private use (viz., in 1728), entitled, *Articles of Belief and Acts of Religion*. I return'd to the use of this, and went no more to the public assemblies.

The best "for" on religion: a private liturgy in place of the congregation. But he leaves the church for the sake of the *citizen*. Doctrines that "divide us" are dropped and what serves "doing good to man" is kept. He separates from the church, not from community. That is the institutional separation the claims.md pressure point predicts.

### franklin.08 · Ch. IX (Part II) — moral perfection against "natural inclination, custom, or company"; the virtues

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C6.* (for), C4.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IX ¶1 and list; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3468–3482, 3521–3539, 3558–3560
- **Checked:** text file

> It was about this time I conceived the bold and arduous project of arriving at moral perfection. I wish'd to live without committing any fault at any time; I would conquer all that either natural inclination, custom, or company might lead me into. As I knew, or thought I knew, what was right and wrong, I did not see why I might not always do the one and avoid the other. [...] I concluded, at length, that the mere speculative conviction that it was our interest to be completely virtuous, was not sufficient to prevent our slipping; and that the contrary habits must be broken, and good ones acquired and established, before we can have any dependence on a steady, uniform rectitude of conduct.

> 5. Frugality. Make no expense but to do good to others or yourself; *i. e.*, waste nothing.

> 6. INDUSTRY. Lose no time; be always employ'd in something useful; cut off all unnecessary actions.

> 8. Justice. Wrong none by doing injuries, or omitting the benefits that are your duty.

> 13. Humility. Imitate Jesus and Socrates.

The nearest Franklin comes to self-origination: a self-designed project, set against "custom, or company." But the standard is not his own invention ("As I knew, or thought I knew, what was right"). It is a common catalogue gathered from his reading, several virtues are defined by others' good, and the last is imitation of two exemplars.

### franklin.09 · Ch. IX (Part II) — the "little book" of daily examination

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IX; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3584–3596, 3753–3757
- **Checked:** text file

> Conceiving then, that, agreeably to the advice of Pythagoras in his Golden Verses, daily examination would be necessary, I contrived the following method for conducting that examination. I made a little book, in which I allotted a page for each of the virtues. I rul'd each page with red ink, so as to have seven columns, one for each day of the week, marking each column with a letter for the day. I cross'd these columns with thirteen red lines, marking the beginning of each line with the first letter of one of the virtues, on which line, and in its proper column, I might mark, by a little black spot, every fault I found upon examination to have been committed respecting that virtue upon that day.

> I enter'd upon the execution of this plan for self-examination, and continu'd it with occasional intermissions for some time. I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish.

Puritan self-scrutiny in its form: daily, written, fault-counting. But Franklin cites Pythagoras, not Calvin. The book counts faults, not sins, and seeks "satisfaction" in their decline, not assurance of election. The schedule's morning and evening "Question" (lines 3720–3740) asks what good he will do, and has done, each day: self-examination turned outward to service. Secularized in source and aim, but still answerable.

### franklin.10 · Ch. IX (Part II) — the prayer before the tables

- **Claims:** C4.30 (against), C4.31 (against)
- **Location:** ch. IX; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3691–3701
- **Checked:** text file

> And conceiving God to be the fountain of wisdom, I thought it right and necessary to solicit his assistance for obtaining it; to this end I formed the following little prayer, which was prefix'd to my tables of examination, for daily use. "*O powerful Goodness! bountiful Father! merciful Guide! Increase in me that wisdom which discovers my truest interest. Strengthen my resolutions to perform what that wisdom dictates. Accept my kind offices to thy other children as the only return in my power for thy continual favours to me*."

The self-examination book opens with a prayer. The self is answerable to a Father, and its service to others is its debt to him. Self-interest ("my truest interest") and answerability are joined, not opposed.

### franklin.11 · Ch. IX (Part II) — virtue as the way to "a poor man's fortune"

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.5 (for), C4.30 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IX; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3860–3866, 3891–3901
- **Checked:** text file

> It will be remark'd that, tho' my scheme was not wholly without religion, there was in it no mark of any of the distinguishing tenets of any particular sect. I had purposely avoided them; for, being fully persuaded of the utility and excellency of my method, and that it might be serviceable to people in all religions [...]

> In this piece it was my design to explain and enforce this doctrine, that vicious actions are not hurtful because they are forbidden, but forbidden because they are hurtful, the nature of man alone considered; that it was, therefore, everyone's interest to be virtuous who wish'd to be happy even in this world; and I should, from this circumstance (there being always in the world a number of rich merchants, nobility, states, and princes, who have need of honest instruments for the management of their affairs, and such being so rare), have endeavoured to convince young persons that no qualities were so likely to make a poor man's fortune as those of probity and integrity.

The religious frame becomes non-sectarian, and virtue is justified by "the nature of man alone considered" and by fortune. Weber's "spirit" in Franklin's own words. Note that the fortune depends on being useful to "rich merchants, nobility, states, and princes": the self rises by serving others' affairs.

### franklin.12 · Ch. IX (Part II) — humility: "the *reality*" versus "the *appearance*"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C4.25 (for, as a later case), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IX; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3903–3913, 3936–3949
- **Checked:** text file

> My list of virtues contain'd at first but twelve; but a Quaker friend having kindly informed me that I was generally thought proud; that my pride show'd itself frequently in conversation; [...] I determined endeavouring to cure myself, if I could, of this vice or folly among the rest, and I added *Humility* to my list, giving an extensive meaning to the word. I cannot boast of much success in acquiring the *reality* of this virtue, but I had a good deal with regard to the *appearance* of it.

> And to this habit (after my character of integrity) I think it principally owing that I had early so much weight with my fellow-citizens when I proposed new institutions, or alterations in the old, and so much influence in public councils when I became a member [...] In reality, there is, perhaps, no one of our natural passions so hard to subdue as *pride*. Disguise it, struggle with it, beat it down, stifle it, mortify it as much as one pleases, it is still alive, and will every now and then peep out and show itself; you will see it, perhaps, often in this history; for, even if I could conceive that I had compleatly overcome it, I should probably be proud of my humility.

The virtue is added because of how others see him, and kept for its effect on his fellow-citizens. Franklin openly separates appearance from reality, as the "dissembling courtier" does in Mandeville's Remark on l. 182 (`texts/mandeville-fable-of-the-bees.txt` lines 3168–3176). The self is a performance aimed at the community, not a withdrawal from it.

### franklin.13 · Ch. IX (Part III) — the "United Party for Virtue"; "one man of tolerable abilities may work great changes"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (complicates), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IX; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 3975–3998, 4050–4058
- **Checked:** text file

> "That while a party is carrying on a general design, each man has his particular private interest in view. [...] "That few in public affairs act from a mere view of the good of their country, whatever they may pretend; and, tho' their actings bring real good to their country, yet men primarily considered that their own and their country's interest was united, and did not act from a principle of benevolence. [...] "There seems to me at present to be great occasion for raising a United Party for Virtue, by forming the virtuous and good men of all nations into a regular body, to be govern'd by suitable good and wise rules, which good and wise men may probably be more unanimous in their obedience to, than common people are to common laws. "I at present think that whoever attempts this aright, and is well qualified, cannot fail of pleasing God, and of meeting with success.

> [...] I am still of opinion that it was a practicable scheme, and might have been very useful, by forming a great number of good citizens; and I was not discourag'd by the seeming magnitude of the undertaking, as I have always thought that one man of tolerable abilities may work great changes, and accomplish great affairs among mankind, if he first forms a good plan, and, cutting off all amusements or other employments that would divert his attention, makes the execution of that same plan his sole study and business.

Franklin's diagnosis is Mandeville's (private interest drives public life), but his answer is a new community, a disciplined "regular body" with rules and a creed. The self as cause ("one man [...] may work great changes") appears here, aimed at "forming a great number of good citizens" and at "pleasing God."

### franklin.14 · Editor's introduction (1916) — "the greatest of our self-made men"

- **Claims:** C6.8 (for, as reception), C6.* (complicates)
- **Location:** Pine's introduction; `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 298–306
- **Checked:** text file

> Franklin is a good type of our American manhood. Although not the wealthiest or the most powerful, he is undoubtedly, in the versatility of his genius and achievements, the greatest of our self-made men. The simple yet graphic story in the *Autobiography* of his steady rise from humble boyhood in a tallow-chandler shop, by industry, economy, and perseverance in self-improvement, to eminence, is the most remarkable of all the remarkable histories of our self-made men. It is in itself a wonderful illustration of the results possible to be attained in a land of unequaled opportunity [...]

Reception, not Franklin: the 1916 school edition makes him the type of the "self-made" man and credits "a land of unequaled opportunity" (C6.5). Providence (.01, .02, .10) and the clubs (.04, .13) drop out of the summary, which is the same kind of reduction as C6.9 claims for Emerson.

### franklin.15 · Appendix, "The Way to Wealth" (1758) — "God gives all Things to Industry"

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.5 (for), C7.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Appendix, "The Way to Wealth" (abridged in this edition); `texts/franklin-autobiography.txt` lines 7355–7362
- **Checked:** text file

> *He that hath a Trade hath an Estate; and he that hath a Calling, hath an Office of Profit and Honor*; but then the *Trade* must be worked at, and the *Calling* well followed, or neither the *Estate* nor the *Office* will enable us to pay our Taxes. What though you have found no Treasure, nor has any rich Relation left you a Legacy, *Diligence is the Mother of Good-luck*, as *Poor Richard* says, *and God gives all Things to Industry*.

The calling is now "an Office of Profit and Honor," and God's part is to reward industry. It is a persona's voice (Father Abraham quoting Poor Richard), so it is not Franklin's own creed. But this is the step Smiles takes in 1859 (C7.4): God still guarantees the link between effort and outcome, which puts the cause of outcomes in the self.
