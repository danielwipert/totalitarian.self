# Thoreau, *Walden* (1854) and "Civil Disobedience" (1849)

- **Edition used:** original English (Gutenberg #205, *Walden, and On The Duty Of Civil Disobedience*)
- **Text file:** `texts/thoreau-walden.txt` (clean)
- **Standard edition** (for final citations): *The Writings of Henry D. Thoreau* (Princeton): *Walden*, ed. Shanley; *Resistance to Civil Government* in *Reform Papers*, ed. Glick. The essay appeared in 1849 as *Resistance to Civil Government*; the title used in this text is the posthumous one.

- **Chapter starts in the text file:** "Economy" 81, "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For" 2364, "Solitude" 3714, "Visitors" 4013, "The Village" 4786, "Higher Laws" 5977, "Conclusion" 9014, "Civil Disobedience" 9421.

## Reading summary

Read in full: "Economy", "Where I Lived", "Solitude", "Visitors", "Higher Laws", "Conclusion", "Civil Disobedience"; and the jail paragraph of "The Village". All three requested phrases are verified ("quiet desperation", line 235; "live deliberately", line 2649; "different drummer", line 9197; "majority of one", lines 9765 and 9768).

**C6.12 holds in its first half and fails in its second.** Thoreau does withdraw deliberately from society's demands: from inherited property, trade, fashion, philanthropy, the post-office, the church tax and the poll-tax (.02, .03, .06, civil.04). But the withdrawal is bounded by more than nature and conscience, and the community is not absent from the bounds:

- **Time and purpose.** Two years and two months; he is "a sojourner in civilized life again" (.01) and left the woods "for as good a reason as I went there" (.13). It is an experiment, not a way of life he wants others to copy (.05).
- **Higher laws and God.** His genius leads to "a life in conformity to higher principles"; "Man flows at once to God when the channel of purity is open" (.12). He would walk "with the Builder of the universe" (.15); conscience in the essay has "God on their side" (civil.02). This is Emerson's frame (C4.19), not something new.
- **Neighbours.** He borrows the axe, raises the house with friends "for neighborliness", dines out, goes to the village every day or two, has "more visitors" than ever, helps a runaway slave north (.07, .11). He pays the highway tax to be "a good neighbor" and looks for "an excuse for conforming" (civil.04). What he cuts is not the neighbour but the *institution* and the *opinion* of the many.
- **The others' freedom.** He may ignore a wrong, but he must first "get off him" whose shoulders he sits on (civil.02).

**The self is found, not prior.** In "Solitude" he is "a part of herself" (Nature), and his nearest kin is not a person but Nature (.09). He is made: "the workman whose work we are" (.10). The task is to dig "through the mud and slush of opinion" to "a hard bottom and rocks in place, which we can call _reality_" (.08). Against this, two strands point the other way: the "doubleness" of a spectator who is "not wholly involved in Nature" (.10), and lines where the world bends to the self: the landscape "radiated from me" and "The universe constantly and obediently answers to our conceptions" (.08). These are not C5.6's priority (the self before experience); they are an early form of C5.14 step 1 (the self as cause), still inside a lawful universe (.13).

**The "different drummer" is framed.** The sentence comes right after "endeavor to be what he was made", and is followed by "the condition of things which we were made for" (.14). Difference is a given nature to be obeyed and a pace to be kept, not a self that makes itself. Elsewhere Thoreau wants "as many different persons in the world as possible", but each one is steered like a sailor by "the polestar" (.05). This is C4.19's channel model, not C5.9's cut.

**Clothes and fashion (C8.2, C8.7).** Thoreau already describes dress driven by "a regard for the opinions of men" and by "what is respected" rather than "what is truly respectable" (.03). The disguise word is not the self. It is the impersonal **"They"** of the tailoress ("They do not make them so now"), which he compares to the Fates, and the goddess **"Fashion"**, who "spins and weaves and cuts with full authority" (.04). People's own "taste" appears too, but as a "childish and savage" and "merely whimsical" taste the manufacturers exploit. So the 1854 disguise is impersonal necessity, not self-expression; and Thoreau's own remedy is the self ("a new wearer of clothes"; clothes take "the impress of the wearer’s character"). That gives a date for C8.7 and supports C8.2: in 1854 the self is the critic of fashion, not yet its vocabulary.

**"Civil Disobedience."** Conscience is answerability (C1.1) set against the majority: "Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator?" (civil.01). It is not self-will: he is "not born to be forced", but "They only can force me who obey a higher law than I" (civil.03), and he watches himself for "obstinacy" (civil.04). The essay ends by asking the State to treat the individual as "a higher and independent power, from which all its own power and authority are derived", but only individuals "who fulfilled all the duties of neighbors and fellow-men" (civil.05).

## Passages

### thoreau-walden.01 · "Economy" — two years and two months; "a sojourner in civilized life again"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (complicates: the withdrawal is bounded in time)
- **Location:** "Economy", opening paragraph; `texts/thoreau-walden.txt` lines 83–88
- **Checked:** text file

> When I wrote the following pages, or rather the bulk of them, I lived alone, in the woods, a mile from any neighbor, in a house which I had built myself, on the shore of Walden Pond, in Concord, Massachusetts, and earned my living by the labor of my hands only. I lived there two years and two months. At present I am a sojourner in civilized life again.

The first sentence states the withdrawal and its limit together. "A mile from any neighbor" is the measure of the distance: it is a mile, not a frontier. The book is written from inside "civilized life", after the experiment.

### thoreau-walden.02 · "Economy" — "quiet desperation"; "Public opinion is a weak tyrant compared with our own private opinion"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for), C7.4 (complicates), C5.14 (complicates: an early form of step 1, hedged)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 217–228, 235–236
- **Checked:** text file

> It is hard to have a southern overseer; it is worse to have a northern one; but worst of all when you are the slave-driver of yourself. [...] See how he cowers and sneaks, how vaguely all the day he fears, not being immortal nor divine, but the slave and prisoner of his own opinion of himself, a fame won by his own deeds. Public opinion is a weak tyrant compared with our own private opinion. What a man thinks of himself, that it is which determines, or rather indicates, his fate. [...]
>
> The mass of men lead lives of quiet desperation. What is called resignation is confirmed desperation.

The famous line is verified (line 235). Its diagnosis locates the chain inside the person, not in society: the teamster is "the slave-driver of yourself". The phrase "determines, or rather indicates, his fate" makes the self's opinion the cause of its fate and then pulls back to a sign. That is C5.14's step 1 in embryo, with the hedge left in.

### thoreau-walden.03 · "Economy" — clothes: "a regard for the opinions of men"; "not what is truly respectable, but what is respected"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for: adds an 1854 case), C8.2 (for: the self is the critic of fashion, not its vocabulary), C6.12 (for)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 630–632, 640–656, 690–692
- **Checked:** text file

> As for Clothing, to come at once to the practical part of the question, perhaps we are led oftener by the love of novelty, and a regard for the opinions of men, in procuring it, than by a true utility. [...] Every day our garments become more assimilated to ourselves, receiving the impress of the wearer’s character, until we hesitate to lay them aside [...]. No man ever stood the lower in my estimation for having a patch in his clothes; yet I am sure that there is greater anxiety, commonly, to have fashionable, or at least clean and unpatched clothes, than to have a sound conscience. [...] It would be easier for them to hobble to town with a broken leg than with a broken pantaloon. Often if an accident happens to a gentleman’s legs, they can be mended; but if a similar accident happens to the legs of his pantaloons, there is no help for it; for he considers, not what is truly respectable, but what is respected. We know but few men, a great many coats and breeches. [...]
>
> I say, beware of all enterprises that require new clothes, and not rather a new wearer of clothes. If there is not a new man, how can the new clothes be made to fit?

The motive is respect: being well regarded by others, set against "a sound conscience". Thoreau's remedy is the self: clothes should carry "the impress of the wearer’s character". The self here is the authentic alternative to fashion, not the word fashion borrows.

### thoreau-walden.04 · "Economy" — the tailoress's "They"; "We worship not the Graces, nor the Parcæ, but Fashion"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for: the disguise word is the impersonal "They" and "Fashion"), C8.2 (for), C8.1 (for: imitation from a centre)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 727–743, 757–758, 768–771
- **Checked:** text file

> When I ask for a garment of a particular form, my tailoress tells me gravely, “They do not make them so now,” not emphasizing the “They” at all, as if she quoted an authority as impersonal as the Fates, and I find it difficult to get made what I want, simply because she cannot believe that I mean what I say, that I am so rash. [...] We worship not the Graces, nor the Parcæ, but Fashion. She spins and weaves and cuts with full authority. The head monkey at Paris puts on a traveller’s cap, and all the monkeys in America do the same. [...] Every generation laughs at the old fashions, but follows religiously the new. [...]
>
> The childish and savage taste of men and women for new patterns keeps how many shaking and squinting through kaleidoscopes that they may discover the particular figure which this generation requires today. The manufacturers have learned that this taste is merely whimsical.

The answer to the brief's question: Thoreau's disguise word is "They", an authority "as impersonal as the Fates", and the goddess "Fashion". Conformity speaks as necessity and fate, not as one's own taste. "Taste" is present, but Thoreau calls it "merely whimsical", and it serves what "this generation requires". It is not yet offered as self-expression.

### thoreau-walden.05 · "Economy" — "as many different persons in the world as possible", steered by "the polestar"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (complicates: difference valued, but under guidance), C4.19 (for), C6.12 (complicates: no one should copy the experiment)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 2087–2099
- **Checked:** text file

> One young man of my acquaintance, who has inherited some acres, told me that he thought he should live as I did, _if he had the means_. I would not have any one adopt _my_ mode of living on any account; for, beside that before he has fairly learned it I may have found out another for myself, I desire that there may be as many different persons in the world as possible; but I would have each one be very careful to find out and pursue _his own_ way, and not his father’s or his mother’s or his neighbor’s instead. [...] It is by a mathematical point only that we are wise, as the sailor or the fugitive slave keeps the polestar in his eye; but that is sufficient guidance for all our life.

The clearest statement of difference as a value in Thoreau. But "his own way" is to be *found out*, not made, and it is steered by a fixed point outside the traveller. Thoreau also refuses to make his own way a model, so Walden is not offered as a pattern for others.

### thoreau-walden.06 · "Economy" — "very selfish": not forsaking "my particular calling to do the good which society demands of me"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for), C7.2 (for, as an early ideal of withdrawal; textual only), C7.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 2128–2131, 2142–2153
- **Checked:** text file

> But all this is very selfish, I have heard some of my townsmen say. I confess that I have hitherto indulged very little in philanthropic enterprises. I have made some sacrifices to a sense of duty, and among others have sacrificed this pleasure also. [...] You must have a genius for charity as well as for any thing else. As for Doing-good, that is one of the professions which are full. Moreover, I have tried it fairly, and, strange as it may seem, am satisfied that it does not agree with my constitution. Probably I should not consciously and deliberately forsake my particular calling to do the good which society demands of me, to save the universe from annihilation; and I believe that a like but infinitely greater steadfastness elsewhere is all that now preserves it. But I would not stand between any man and his genius; and to him who does this work, which I decline, with his whole heart and soul and life, I would say, Persevere, even if the world call it doing evil, as it is most likely they will.

The strongest "for" passage for C6.12. "The good which society demands of me" is refused in favour of a "calling". But calling is a religious word, and the universe is kept by "a like but infinitely greater steadfastness elsewhere". The self keeps its calling as God keeps his, so this is not self-origination. For C7.8 it complicates: he declines organised charity, not other people (see .11).

### thoreau-walden.07 · "Economy" — the borrowed axe and the raising "for neighborliness"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (against: "rather than by community" cannot stand)
- **Location:** "Economy"; lines 1182–1189, 1306–1311
- **Checked:** text file

> Near the end of March, 1845, I borrowed an axe and went down to the woods by Walden Pond, nearest to where I intended to build my house, and began to cut down some tall, arrowy white pines, still in their youth, for timber. It is difficult to begin without borrowing, but perhaps it is the most generous course thus to permit your fellow-men to have an interest in your enterprise. The owner of the axe, as he released his hold on it, said that it was the apple of his eye; but I returned it sharper than I received it. [...]
>
> At length, in the beginning of May, with the help of some of my acquaintances, rather to improve so good an occasion for neighborliness than from any necessity, I set up the frame of my house. No man was ever more honored in the character of his raisers than I.

The experiment begins with a loan and is raised by neighbours. Thoreau frames both as a gift *to* the community ("the most generous course"), but the dependence is real and he says so. The timber, stones and sand are claimed "by squatter’s right" (line 1441), on land he does not own.

### thoreau-walden.08 · "Where I Lived" — "to live deliberately"; "a hard bottom and rocks in place, which we can call _reality_"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for: deliberate), C5.6 (against: reality is found, not made), C5.14 (complicates: "the universe [...] answers to our conceptions")
- **Location:** "Where I Lived, and What I Lived For"; lines 2649–2660, 2823–2829, 2845–2851
- **Checked:** text file

> I went to the woods because I wished to live deliberately, to front only the essential facts of life, and see if I could not learn what it had to teach, and not, when I came to die, discover that I had not lived. I did not wish to live what was not life, living is so dear; nor did I wish to practise resignation, unless it was quite necessary. I wanted to live deep and suck out all the marrow of life, to live so sturdily and Spartan-like as to put to rout all that was not life, to cut a broad swath and shave close, to drive life into a corner, and reduce it to its lowest terms, and, if it proved to be mean, why then to get the whole and genuine meanness of it, and publish its meanness to the world; or if it were sublime, to know it by experience, and be able to give a true account of it in my next excursion. [...]
>
> God himself culminates in the present moment, and will never be more divine in the lapse of all the ages. And we are enabled to apprehend at all what is sublime and noble only by the perpetual instilling and drenching of the reality that surrounds us. The universe constantly and obediently answers to our conceptions; whether we travel fast or slow, the track is laid for us. Let us spend our lives in conceiving then. [...]
>
> Let us settle ourselves, and work and wedge our feet downward through the mud and slush of opinion, and prejudice, and tradition, and delusion, and appearance, that alluvion which covers the globe, through Paris and London, through New York and Boston and Concord, through church and state, through poetry and philosophy and religion, till we come to a hard bottom and rocks in place, which we can call _reality_, and say, This is, and no mistake; and then begin

The verified line (2649) is about *learning* what life "had to teach": the self goes to be taught, and to "give a true account". Reality is a "hard bottom" under opinion, found by digging. One sentence pulls the other way ("The universe constantly and obediently answers to our conceptions"), but it sits between God in the present moment and "the reality that surrounds us".

### thoreau-walden.09 · "Solitude" — "a part of herself"; the nearest of kin "was not a person nor a villager"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for: nature replaces human company as the bound), C5.6 (against: the self is *in* nature), C7.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Solitude"; lines 3716–3718, 3792–3808
- **Checked:** text file

> This is a delicious evening, when the whole body is one sense, and imbibes delight through every pore. I go and come with a strange liberty in Nature, a part of herself. [...]
>
> I have never felt lonesome, or in the least oppressed by a sense of solitude, but once, and that was a few weeks after I came to the woods, when, for an hour, I doubted if the near neighborhood of man was not essential to a serene and healthy life. To be alone was something unpleasant. But I was at the same time conscious of a slight insanity in my mood, and seemed to foresee my recovery. In the midst of a gentle rain while these thoughts prevailed, I was suddenly sensible of such sweet and beneficent society in Nature, in the very pattering of the drops, and in every sound and sight around my house, an infinite and unaccountable friendliness all at once like an atmosphere sustaining me, as made the fancied advantages of human neighborhood insignificant, and I have never thought of them since. Every little pine needle expanded and swelled with sympathy and befriended me. I was so distinctly made aware of the presence of something kindred to me, even in scenes which we are accustomed to call wild and dreary, and also that the nearest of blood to me and humanest was not a person nor a villager, that I thought no place could ever be strange to me again.

The core text for C6.12's "bounded by nature [...] rather than by community". Nature is not merely a limit here but "society", "kindred" and "the nearest of blood". The self is "a part of herself", which is the opposite of C5.6's self prior to its world. The doubt about "the near neighborhood of man" is real but lasts an hour.

### thoreau-walden.10 · "Solitude" — "the workman whose work we are"; the spectator "not wholly involved in Nature"

- **Claims:** C5.8 (complicates), C5.6 (complicates), C4.19 (for)
- **Location:** "Solitude"; lines 3860–3864, 3885–3901
- **Checked:** text file

> Nearest to all things is that power which fashions their being. _Next_ to us the grandest laws are continually being executed. _Next_ to us is not the workman whom we have hired, with whom we love so well to talk, but the workman whose work we are. [...]
>
> With thinking we may be beside ourselves in a sane sense. By a conscious effort of the mind we can stand aloof from actions and their consequences; and all things, good and bad, go by us like a torrent. We are not wholly involved in Nature. I may be either the drift-wood in the stream, or Indra in the sky looking down on it. [...] I only know myself as a human entity; the scene, so to speak, of thoughts and affections; and am sensible of a certain doubleness by which I can stand as remote from myself as from another. However intense my experience, I am conscious of the presence and criticism of a part of me, which, as it were, is not a part of me, but spectator, sharing no experience, but taking note of it; and that is no more I than it is you. When the play, it may be the tragedy, of life is over, the spectator goes his way. [...] This doubleness may easily make us poor neighbors and friends sometimes.

The self is made ("the workman whose work we are"), so it is not its own origin. But Thoreau also has a mind that can "stand aloof" and is "not wholly involved in Nature", which is the C5.8 move. That spectator is impersonal ("no more I than it is you"), so it is not a private self either. He names the social cost himself: "poor neighbors and friends".

### thoreau-walden.11 · "Visitors" — "I am naturally no hermit"; "more visitors"; the runaway slave

- **Claims:** C6.12 (complicates), C7.8 (against, for Thoreau's own case)
- **Location:** "Visitors"; lines 4015–4019, 4127–4134, 4374–4375
- **Checked:** text file

> I think that I love society as much as most, and am ready enough to fasten myself like a bloodsucker for the time to any full-blooded man that comes in my way. I am naturally no hermit, but might possibly sit out the sturdiest frequenter of the bar-room, if my business called me thither. [...]
>
> As for men, they will hardly fail one any where. I had more visitors while I lived in the woods than at any other period in my life; I mean that I had some. I met several there under more favorable circumstances than I could any where else. But fewer came to see me on trivial business. In this respect, my company was winnowed by my mere distance from town. [...]
>
> One real runaway slave, among the rest, whom I helped to forward toward the northstar.

The withdrawal filters society rather than refusing it: company is "winnowed", not cut off. Helping a fugitive slave north (said almost in passing) is the opposite of indifference to others, and links Walden to the essay's politics.

### thoreau-walden.12 · "Higher Laws" — genius leads to "conformity to higher principles"; "Man flows at once to God"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for: bounded by higher law; complicates: the bound is God, not only nature and conscience), C4.19 (for: the Self as channel), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Higher Laws"; lines 6158–6166, 6226–6233, 6260–6265
- **Checked:** text file

> If one listens to the faintest but constant suggestions of his genius, which are certainly true, he sees not to what extremes, or even insanity, it may lead him; and yet that way, as he grows more resolute and faithful, his road lies. The faintest assured objection which one healthy man feels will at length prevail over the arguments and customs of mankind. No man ever followed his genius till it misled him. Though the result were bodily weakness, yet perhaps no one can say that the consequences were to be regretted, for these were a life in conformity to higher principles. [...]
>
> Our whole life is startlingly moral. There is never an instant’s truce between virtue and vice. Goodness is the only investment that never fails. [...] Though the youth at last grows indifferent, the laws of the universe are not indifferent, but are forever on the side of the most sensitive. [...]
>
> Chastity is the flowering of man; and what are called Genius, Heroism, Holiness, and the like, are but various fruits which succeed it. Man flows at once to God when the channel of purity is open. By turns our purity inspires and our impurity casts us down. He is blessed who is assured that the animal is dying out in him day by day, and the divine being established.

Following one's genius against "the arguments and customs of mankind" is the self-reliant move. But the genius is trusted because it leads to "conformity to higher principles", and the self is "the channel" through which man "flows at once to God". This is Emerson's frame (C4.19), with an ascetic discipline added.

### thoreau-walden.13 · "Conclusion" — "obedience to the laws of his being"; "I left the woods for as good a reason as I went there"; "the direction of his dreams"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for: bounded by law; complicates: the experiment ends), C5.14 (complicates: a precursor of step 1, inside "the laws of the universe")
- **Location:** "Conclusion"; lines 9101–9112, 9125–9134
- **Checked:** text file

> A saner man would have found himself often enough “in formal opposition” to what are deemed “the most sacred laws of society,” through obedience to yet more sacred laws, and so have tested his resolution without going out of his way. It is not for a man to put himself in such an attitude to society, but to maintain himself in whatever attitude he find himself through obedience to the laws of his being, which will never be one of opposition to a just government, if he should chance to meet with such.
>
> I left the woods for as good a reason as I went there. Perhaps it seemed to me that I had several more lives to live, and could not spare any more time for that one. [...]
>
> I learned this, at least, by my experiment; that if one advances confidently in the direction of his dreams, and endeavors to live the life which he has imagined, he will meet with a success unexpected in common hours. He will put some things behind, will pass an invisible boundary; new, universal, and more liberal laws will begin to establish themselves around and within him; or the old laws be expanded, and interpreted in his favor in a more liberal sense, and he will live with the license of a higher order of beings. In proportion as he simplifies his life, the laws of the universe will appear less complex

Against Mirabeau, Thoreau rejects opposition chosen for its own sake. Opposition is allowed only as a by-product of "obedience to yet more sacred laws". The "dreams" sentence is the ancestor of later success literature (C5.14). But what the dreamer meets is "new, universal, and more liberal laws", not a world he authors.

### thoreau-walden.14 · "Conclusion" — "endeavor to be what he was made"; "a different drummer"

- **Claims:** C5.9 (complicates: the drummer is still framed), C4.19 (for), C6.12 (for)
- **Location:** "Conclusion"; lines 9192–9205
- **Checked:** text file

> Let every one mind his own business, and endeavor to be what he was made.
>
> Why should we be in such desperate haste to succeed, and in such desperate enterprises? If a man does not keep pace with his companions, perhaps it is because he hears a different drummer. Let him step to the music which he hears, however measured or far away. It is not important that he should mature as soon as an apple-tree or an oak. Shall he turn his spring into summer? If the condition of things which we were made for is not yet, what were any reality which we can substitute? We will not be shipwrecked on a vain reality. Shall we with pains erect a heaven of blue glass over ourselves, though when it is done we shall be sure to gaze still at the true ethereal heaven far above, as if the former were not?

Verified (line 9197). The context frames the drummer on both sides: "be what he was made", and "the condition of things which we were made for". The drummer is heard, not composed. The difference is one of pace and maturing, like a tree's, and above it is "the true ethereal heaven", which no self-made "heaven of blue glass" can replace. This is not yet difference cut from the common frame.

### thoreau-walden.15 · "Conclusion" — "God will see that you do not want society"; "walk even with the Builder of the universe"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (complicates: God, not only nature and conscience, replaces society), C4.19 (for)
- **Location:** "Conclusion"; lines 9265–9271, 9300–9311
- **Checked:** text file

> Cultivate poverty like a garden herb, like sage. Do not trouble yourself much to get new things, whether clothes or friends. Turn the old; return to them. Things do not change; we change. Sell your clothes and keep your thoughts. God will see that you do not want society. If I were confined to a corner of a garret all my days, like a spider, the world would be just as large to me while I had my thoughts about me. [...]
>
> I delight to come to my bearings,—not walk in procession with pomp and parade, in a conspicuous place, but to walk even with the Builder of the universe, if I may,—not to live in this restless, nervous, bustling, trivial Nineteenth Century, but stand or sit thoughtfully while it goes by. [...] I love to weigh, to settle, to gravitate toward that which most strongly and rightfully attracts me;—not hang by the beam of the scale and try to weigh less,—not suppose a case, but take the case that is; to travel the only path I can, and that on which no power can resist me.

The withdrawal from society is underwritten by God ("God will see"). The self's path is the one it "can" travel, toward what "rightfully attracts" it: it gravitates, it does not originate. "Sell your clothes and keep your thoughts" links back to .03.

### thoreau-civil.01 · "Civil Disobedience" — conscience against the majority: "men first, and subjects afterward"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (for: answerability), C6.12 (for: conscience as bound), C6.10 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Civil Disobedience"; lines 9472–9486
- **Checked:** text file

> After all, the practical reason why, when the power is once in the hands of the people, a majority are permitted, and for a long period continue, to rule, is not because they are most likely to be in the right, nor because this seems fairest to the minority, but because they are physically the strongest. But a government in which the majority rule in all cases can not be based on justice, even as far as men understand it. Can there not be a government in which the majorities do not virtually decide right and wrong, but conscience?—in which majorities decide only those questions to which the rule of expediency is applicable? Must the citizen ever for a moment, or in the least degree, resign his conscience to the legislator? Why has every man a conscience, then? I think that we should be men first, and subjects afterward. It is not desirable to cultivate a respect for the law, so much as for the right. The only obligation which I have a right to assume, is to do at any time what I think right.

This is the C1.1 root in political form: each person answerable on their own, here against the majority. "What I think right" sounds subjective, but the next pages tie it to "the right", justice and God (civil.02, civil.03). For C6.10: Thoreau's target is the same as Tocqueville's (the majority's moral power), but his answer is the conscientious individual, not associations.

### thoreau-civil.02 · "Civil Disobedience" — "get off him first"; "a majority of one"

- **Claims:** C1.1 (for), C6.12 (complicates: conscience is bounded by others' freedom and by God), C7.8 (against)
- **Location:** "Civil Disobedience"; lines 9661–9668, 9762–9768
- **Checked:** text file

> It is not a man’s duty, as a matter of course, to devote himself to the eradication of any, even the most enormous wrong; he may still properly have other concerns to engage him; but it is his duty, at least, to wash his hands of it, and, if he gives it no thought longer, not to give it practically his support. If I devote myself to other pursuits and contemplations, I must first see, at least, that I do not pursue them sitting upon another man’s shoulders. I must get off him first, that he may pursue his contemplations too. [...]
>
> I do not hesitate to say, that those who call themselves abolitionists should at once effectually withdraw their support, both in person and property, from the government of Massachusetts, and not wait till they constitute a majority of one, before they suffer the right to prevail through them. I think that it is enough if they have God on their side, without waiting for that other one. Moreover, any man more right than his neighbors constitutes a majority of one already.

Verified: "majority of one" appears twice (lines 9765, 9768). The one is a majority because God is the "other one": the individual stands with God against the many, not alone. The withdrawal from society's demands has a floor: one may ignore a wrong, but not sit "upon another man’s shoulders".

### thoreau-civil.03 · "Civil Disobedience" — "I was not born to be forced"; "a higher law than I"; "If a plant cannot live according to its nature, it dies"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (for: bounded by higher law and by nature), C5.6 (against), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** "Civil Disobedience"; lines 9941–9958
- **Checked:** text file

> Thus the state never intentionally confronts a man’s sense, intellectual or moral, but only his body, his senses. It is not armed with superior wit or honesty, but with superior physical strength. I was not born to be forced. I will breathe after my own fashion. Let us see who is the strongest. What force has a multitude? They only can force me who obey a higher law than I. They force me to become like themselves. [...] I am not responsible for the successful working of the machinery of society. I am not the son of the engineer. I perceive that, when an acorn and a chestnut fall side by side, the one does not remain inert to make way for the other, but both obey their own laws, and spring and grow and flourish as best they can, till one, perchance, overshadows and destroys the other. If a plant cannot live according to its nature, it dies; and so a man.

The most defiant lines in the essay, and still bounded on both sides. Only "a higher law" may force him, and his freedom is a plant's freedom to "obey their own laws" and live "according to its nature". "I am not responsible for the successful working of the machinery of society" is a clear refusal of social answerability, put in the name of nature.

### thoreau-civil.04 · "Civil Disobedience" — "a good neighbor" and "a bad subject"; "an excuse for conforming"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (against: "rather than by community"), C1.1 (for: self-suspicion)
- **Location:** "Civil Disobedience"; lines 10061–10071, 10081–10084, 10116–10123
- **Checked:** text file

> I have never declined paying the highway tax, because I am as desirous of being a good neighbor as I am of being a bad subject; and, as for supporting schools, I am doing my part to educate my fellow-countrymen now. It is for no particular item in the tax-bill that I refuse to pay it. I simply wish to refuse allegiance to the State, to withdraw and stand aloof from it effectually. [...] In fact, I quietly declare war with the State, after my fashion, though I will still make use and get what advantages of her I can, as is usual in such cases. [...]
>
> This, then, is my position at present. But one cannot be too much on his guard in such a case, lest his actions be biassed by obstinacy, or an undue regard for the opinions of men. Let him see that he does only what belongs to himself and to the hour. [...]
>
> I do not wish to quarrel with any man or nation. I do not wish to split hairs, to make fine distinctions, or set myself up as better than my neighbors. I seek rather, I may say, even an excuse for conforming to the laws of the land. I am but too ready to conform to them.

The key distinction for C6.12: Thoreau withdraws from the *State*, not from the community. He pays the neighbour's tax and refuses the State's. He also suspects himself on both sides: of "obstinacy" (the self's own will) and of "an undue regard for the opinions of men". The essay is not written by a self that cannot be wrong.

### thoreau-civil.05 · "Civil Disobedience" — the individual as "a higher and independent power", among those who fulfil "all the duties of neighbors and fellow-men"

- **Claims:** C6.12 (complicates), C6.1 (for: America as host of the idea), C5.9 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Civil Disobedience", last paragraph; lines 10222–10245
- **Checked:** text file

> The authority of government, even such as I am willing to submit to,—for I will cheerfully obey those who know and can do better than I, and in many things even those who neither know nor can do so well,—is still an impure one: to be strictly just, it must have the sanction and consent of the governed. It can have no pure right over my person and property but what I concede to it. The progress from an absolute to a limited monarchy, from a limited monarchy to a democracy, is a progress toward a true respect for the individual. [...] There will never be a really free and enlightened State, until the State comes to recognize the individual as a higher and independent power, from which all its own power and authority are derived, and treats him accordingly. I please myself with imagining a State at last which can afford to be just to all men, and to treat the individual with respect as a neighbor; which even would not think it inconsistent with its own repose, if a few were to live aloof from it, not meddling with it, nor embraced by it, who fulfilled all the duties of neighbors and fellow-men.

The strongest statement of the individual's priority, but over the *State*: the individual is the source of political authority, not of himself. The few who "live aloof" are pictured as fulfilling "all the duties of neighbors and fellow-men". That is C6.12's withdrawal with the community kept as a duty. He also says he will "cheerfully obey those who know and can do better than I".
