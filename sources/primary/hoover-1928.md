## Summary of the reading (2026-10-25)

**C6.7 (pioneer vocabulary captured against regulation).** The phrase "rugged individualism" occurs twice in the book (by search): New York, line 5412, and Palo Alto, line 7413. In New York it is turned against *government ownership and operation*, "paternalism and state socialism", not against regulation as such (hoover28.16). In the same speech Hoover defends regulation by name: public utilities under "the fullest and most complete control", anti-trust laws, "glass pockets", and an individualism that is "no system of laissez faire" (.18, .19). At St. Louis he insists on "the most strict regulation of public utilities" (.22). So in 1928 Hoover himself does *not* make the phrase a war cry against regulation; he makes it a war cry against public ownership (Muscle Shoals, state liquor sales, farm price-fixing). But the ground has shifted since 1922. Regulation is now a fallback to be avoided: "the duty of government to avoid regulation" unless equal opportunity is invaded (.04), abuses better cured "by inspiration and co-operation rather than by regulation" (.11), and the lumber code that "avoided the necessity of regulation" (.21). The bounds of 1922 (equality of opportunity, "service", anti-domination) are all still named, and Palo Alto binds "rugged individualism" to "ordered liberty", "equality of opportunity" and "idealism" (.25). Verdict: C6.7's endpoint ("a war cry" against regulation) is not in Hoover's own mouth in 1928; it is a short step from what he says, and Dewey's 1930 report must describe a later use, not these speeches. C6.7 should say: by 1928 the phrase is aimed at public ownership and at "bureaucracy", with regulation conceded but demoted.

**Pioneer vocabulary.** The frontier is closed and carried over as in 1922: "Nor are the days of the pioneer over" (.07, cf. hoover.08), and "Our mental and physical frontiers are gone" (.10). The pioneers are "rugged" and "self-reliant" but also "co-operated together" and "God-fearing" (.08). Hoover gives the C6.7 history himself: "a moving frontier preserved that equality of opportunity" for about a hundred years; now government must (.22).

**C6.13 (voluntary checks redirected).** The checks are still claimed, loudly, but almost wholly as *economic* association: trade associations, chambers of commerce, and the Department of Commerce's committees. Association is now so universal that "scarcely an individual" does not belong (.21), and it is valued because it reduces "government interference". "Self-government" itself passes to business: "When business cures its own abuses it is true self-government" (.04). "Service" is attached to the independent business man (.05), to the pioneers (.08), to the town relief committees in the flood (.12) and to America abroad (.20). Religion is present as tolerance, "the help of God", "the guidance of the Almighty" (.01, .03), but the spiritual now rests on the material: material things are "the foundation stones" of the spiritual (.23), the reverse of 1922 (hoover.03). For whom: the farmer and the "independent business man" are the "stronghold of American individuality" (.05); Main Street is the source of national character (.12); the industrial worker figures as wage, job and consumer.

**C8.6 / C8.7 (self-expression; value words dressing conformity).** "Self-expression" does not occur in the 1928 speeches (search), though Hoover used it in 1922 (hoover.04). "Expression" is used only for the ballot and the will of the people (.25; line 7364). The person's word is "individuality", given to the farmer and the small business man (.05, .21). The consumer appears through standards of living, "the full garage" (New York, line 5950), not through self-expression. This supports C8.6's dating. For C8.7, the value words over the standardizing order are "individualism", "self-government", "service", "co-operation" and "equality of opportunity", and Hoover praises sameness openly: "the same news", "the same voices" (.10).

**C9.6 (value choices as findings of science).** Partly for. Hoover claims the experimental method for policy: theories must be weighed "in quantities, not arguments" (.13), and "The truth must be forged from the metal of facts" (.23); waste is a finding of investigation (.09). But he states his values openly and puts them above efficiency: even if government business were more efficient, "the fundamental objection to it would remain" (.15); the purpose is "a human society, not an economic system" (.03). So against the strong form: he does not deny his values or present them as science; he presents tariff and organisation policy as fact-founded.

**C5.14 / C7.4 (self as cause; failure blamed on the person).** Mixed. The race image puts success on the runner's "training", "ability" and "character" once government gives "an equal start" (.02), and poverty is defined for "those who have the will to work" (.01). But Hoover also names structural causes plainly: the great wastes "were not the fault of individuals" (.09); agriculture's failure has listed causes outside the farmer (lines 949–968); the business man needs stability against "ill tides over which he has no control" (line 7044). And for his own career he denies self-making: "My country owes me no debt" (.24). The individual is the *unit* of achievement, not its sole cause.

**C9.1 (opposites carrying the Self).** Only a hint: both sides claim "liberalism", and Hoover says state ownership produces "the spirit of submission" (.17). Not enough to test the claim.

## Passages

### hoover28.01 · Acceptance, Palo Alto, August 11 — poverty for "those who have the will to work"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "Our Prosperity Wisely Enjoyed"; `texts/hoover-new-day-1928.txt` lines 869–882
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> One of the oldest and perhaps the noblest of human aspirations has been the abolition of poverty. By poverty I mean the grinding by undernourishment, cold, and ignorance, and fear of old age of those who have the will to work. We in America today are nearer to the final triumph over poverty than ever before in the history of any land. The poorhouse is vanishing from among us. We have not yet reached the goal, but, given a chance to go forward with the policies of the last eight years, we shall soon with the help of God be in sight of the day when poverty will be banished from this nation. There is no guarantee against poverty equal to a job for every man.

The remedy is policy, not character: poverty falls with jobs. But the definition quietly leaves out those without "the will to work", so the residue of poverty is put down to the person. God is still invoked, as a helper of policy.

### hoover28.02 · Acceptance, August 11 — "The Ideal of Equality" and the race

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C7.4 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "The Ideal of Equality", "Not Socialism"; lines 1770–1805, 1813–1819
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "trich" read as "rich"; section heading between the first two paragraphs marked as a cut

> Equality of opportunity is the right of every American—rich or poor, foreign or native-born, irrespective of faith or color. It is the right of every individual to attain that position in life to which his ability and character entitle him. [...] Only from confidence that this right will be upheld can flow that unbounded courage and hope which stimulate each individual man and woman to endeavor and to achievement. The sum of their achievement is the gigantic harvest of national progress. [...] This ideal of individualism based upon equal opportunity to every citizen is the negation of socialism. It is the negation of anarchy. It is the negation of despotism. It is as if we set a race. We, through free and universal education, provide the training of the runners; we give to them an equal start; we provide in the government the umpire of fairness in the race. The winner is he who shows the most conscientious training, the greatest ability, and the greatest character. Socialism bids all to end the race equally. It holds back the speedy to the pace of the slowest. [...] The Sherman Law was enacted in endeavor to hold open the door of equal opportunity in business. The commissions for regulation of public utilities were created to prevent discrimination in service and prevent extortion in rates—and thereby the destruction of equal opportunity.

The clearest statement of where outcome is placed: once the start is equal, the result follows "ability and character". That is C7.4's attribution in a statesman, though the frame (government trains and umpires) is social. The same paragraph keeps the 1922 defence of anti-trust and utility regulation (hoover.07).

### hoover28.03 · Acceptance, August 11 — "not an end in itself"; "a human society, not an economic system"; religious tolerance

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against), C6.13 (complicates), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "Our Prosperity Wisely Enjoyed", "The Office of President", "Religious Tolerance"; lines 899–916, 1874–1879, 1892–1897, 1572–1579
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> Economic advancement is not an end in itself. Successful democracy rests wholly upon the moral and spiritual quality of its people. [...] Generosity of impulse, cultivation of mind, willingness to sacrifice, spaciousness of spirit—those are the qualities whereby America, growing bigger and richer and more powerful, may become America great and noble. A people or government to which these values are not real, because they are not tangible, is in peril. [...] Our purpose is to build in this nation a human society, not an economic system. We wish to increase the efficiency and productivity of our country, but its final purpose is happier homes. [...] It must be the instrument by which national conscience is livened and it must under the guidance of the Almighty interpret and follow that conscience.

> In this land, dedicated to tolerance, we still find outbreaks of intolerance. I come of Quaker stock. My ancestors were persecuted for their beliefs. Here they sought and found religious freedom. By blood and conviction I stand for religious tolerance both in act and in spirit. The glory of our American ideals is the right of every man to worship God according to the dictates of his own conscience.

The engineer names his values outright, ranks them above efficiency, and admits they are "not tangible". Against C9.6's strong form. The values are generosity and sacrifice, not the Self; the bounded individualism of 1922 is still the official creed. Religion enters the campaign (against Al Smith) chiefly as tolerance and private conscience, a right of the individual rather than a shared authority: this bears on C6.13's "Religion kept belief but lost its dominance".

### hoover28.04 · Acceptance, August 11 — "The Government and Business": avoid regulation; business "true self-government"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for, with limits), C6.13 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "The Government and Business"; lines 1400–1439
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> With the growth and increasing complexity of our economic life the relations of government and business are multiplying daily. [...] It is the duty of government to avoid regulation as long as equal opportunity to all citizens is not invaded and public rights violated. Government should not engage in business in competition with its citizens. Such actions extinguish the enterprise and initiative which has been the glory of America and which has been the root of its pre-eminence among the nations of the earth. On the other hand, it is the duty of business to conduct itself so that government regulation or government competition is unnecessary. [...] Our whole business system would break down in a day if there was not a high sense of moral responsibility in our business world. The whole practice and ethics of business has made great strides of improvement in the last quarter of a century, largely due to the effort of business and the professions themselves. One of the most helpful signs of recent years is the stronger growth of associations of workers, farmers, business men, and professional men with a desire to cure their own abuses and a purpose to serve public interest. Many problems can be solved through co-operation between government and these self-governing associations to improve methods and practices. When business cures its own abuses it is true self-government, which comprises more than political institutions.

The shift from 1922. Then, regulation was the "monument" to equality of opportunity (hoover.07). Now government's "duty" is to avoid it, with equality of opportunity as the limit. The word "self-government", the township's word in Tocqueville, is given to trade associations: C6.13's redirection of the checks, in one sentence.

### hoover28.05 · Acceptance, August 11 — the independent business man, "stronghold of American individuality"; the farm not "a mass-production machine"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for, on "for whom"), C8.6 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "Proper Promotion of Business a Government Function", "Urgent Need of Farm Relief"; lines 1517–1538, 979–983
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> The test of business is not its size—the test is whether there is honest competition, whether there is freedom from domination, whether there is integrity and usefulness of purpose. As Secretary of Commerce I have been greatly impressed by the fact that the foundation of American business is the independent business man. [...] Alike with our farmers his is the stronghold of American individuality. It is here that our local communities receive their leadership. It is here that we refresh our leadership for larger enterprise. We must maintain his opportunity and his individual service. He and the public must be protected from any domination or from predatory business.

> Farming is and must continue to be an individualistic business of small units and independent ownership. The farm is more than a business: it is a state of living. We do not wish it converted into a mass-production machine.

For whom the individualism is claimed: the small proprietor and the farmer, the old pioneer types, protected against "predatory business". The industrial worker is not called an individualist here. The person's word is "individuality" plus "service", not "self-expression" (C8.6).

### hoover28.06 · Acceptance, August 11 — labor as "staunch supporters of American individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Acceptance address, "The Interests of Labor"; lines 1212–1218, 1250–1254
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> The trade union movement in our country has maintained two departures from such movements in all other countries. They have been staunch supporters of American individualism and American institutions. They have steadfastly opposed subversive doctrines from abroad. [...] The Republican platform gives the pledge of the party to the support of labor. It endorses the principle of collective bargaining and freedom in labor negotiations. We stand also pledged to the curtailment of excessive use of the injunction in labor disputes.

"Individualism" is not yet a word against unions: the unions are enlisted under it, and collective bargaining is endorsed. The enemy it names is foreign socialism. This complicates any reading of the term as business's alone in 1928.

### hoover28.07 · West Branch, Iowa, August 21 — "Nor are the days of the pioneer over"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** West Branch speech, "Enduring Nature of American Integrity"; lines 2305–2318
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> The modernizations of our waterways recall again the earlier life of Iowa. At one time its transportation was in large degree over these same rivers, and many of our pioneers reached this state by the old packet boats and their own rafts. Nor are the days of the pioneer over. We have to pioneer through economic problems, through scientific development and invention, on to frontiers just as forbidding, just as romantic, and just as pregnant of added happiness as our fathers ever knew. The test of our generation will be whether we can overcome these frontiers, whether we can hold mastery over the system we have created, whether we can maintain the advantage we have inherited, whether we can hold ourselves a nation dedicated to equal opportunity for all.

Nearly the words of 1922 ("The days of the pioneer are not over", hoover.08): the frontier carried over into economics and science. The test it sets is still equal opportunity and "mastery over the system we have created", which admits that the system is now the problem.

### hoover28.08 · West Branch, August 21 — "self-reliant, rugged, God-fearing" pioneers who "co-operated together"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** West Branch speech; lines 2327–2364
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); "self reliant" at a line break read as "self-reliant"

> Our fathers and grandfathers who poured over the Midwest were self-reliant, rugged, God-fearing people of indomitable courage. They combined to build the roads, bridges, and towns; they co-operated together to erect their schools, their churches, and to raise their barns and harvest their fields. They asked only for freedom of opportunity and an equal chance. In these conceptions lies the real basis of American democracy. [...] When we traverse the memories of those who have builded this state and this nation we recall these acts which are rooted in the soil of service. [...] I do not believe our people have lost those finer qualities of rugged character, self-reliance, or initiative, nor have they lost the great quality which they embedded in American character, the quality of neighborly co-operation and mutual service. It is in this quality that our hopes must lie in the solution of our great problems.

"Rugged" here is bounded on every side: God-fearing, barn-raising, church-building, "mutual service". Hoover's pioneer is Turner's associating frontiersman, not a lone Self. The hope is placed in the co-operative quality, not the rugged one.

### hoover28.09 · Newark, September 17 — wastes "not the fault of individuals"; cured "not by law or regulation"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (against), C6.13 (for), C9.6 (for)
- **Location:** Newark speech, "The Elimination of Waste"; lines 2911–2945
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "simplif-cation" read as "simplification"

> Eight years ago I caused a nation-wide investigation to be undertaken of the whole subject. I felt that it was in the interest of our country to know what opportunities we had to improve our methods. It developed that there were great opportunities for increased efficiency in our whole industrial machine. We have the highest ingenuity and efficiency, in the operation of our individual industries, of any nation. Yet there were great wastes which were not the fault of individuals, employers, or employees. These wastes were due to seasonal unemployment and to unemployment during depressions; to speculation and overproduction during booms; to labor turnover and labor conflicts; to intermittent failure of transportation, of supplies, of fuel, of power, and of credit to synchronize with demand; to lack of simplification and standardization in many of our commonly used commodities; [...] We adopted a new policy in government. That was, that the Secretary of Commerce should co-operate with industry in organization against such waste, not by law or regulation, but by purely voluntary action in which the joint service of the associations representing the managers of a given industry, its employees, its distributors, and its consumers were all enlisted in a common purpose.

Against C7.4: unemployment and depression are named as system faults, expressly not the individual's. The engineer's frame (investigation, "waste", "standardization") turns these into technical findings, and the remedy is voluntary association, not law: C9.6 and C6.13 in one passage.

### hoover28.10 · Elizabethton, Tennessee, October 6 — "the same news", "the same voices"; "frontiers are gone"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C6.7 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Elizabethton speech, opening; lines 3280–3291
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> I am from the West, where our people are proud to be the melted product of both the North and the South. Our accent differs from that of the people of Alabama and Vermont, but we have the same hearts, the same kind of homes, the same ideals and aspirations. Every morning and evening we read the same news; every night we listen by radio to the same voices. Our mental and physical frontiers are gone.

Sameness is praised openly, as national unity, not dressed as individuality. The frontier's closing is extended from land to mind by the man who says, elsewhere, that the days of the pioneer are not over (.07). For C8.2: in 1928 the conformity is not yet spoken as the self; it is spoken as union.

### hoover28.11 · Elizabethton, October 6 — "Democracy [...] must be master in its own house"; "Co-operation, Not Dictation"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** Elizabethton speech, "The Government and Business", "Co-operation, Not Dictation"; lines 3885–3901, 3946–3973
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> I do not favor any general extension of the Federal Government into the operation of business in competition with its citizens. It is not the system of Lincoln or Roosevelt. It is not the American system. It not only undermines initiative but it undermines state and local self-government. It is the destruction of states' rights. Democracy, however, must be master in its own house. It can assure the conservation of our governmentally controlled natural resources in the interest of the people. It has demonstrated that by the power of regulation it can prevent abuse; it can and must control natural monopolies in full public interest. [...] From my experience in government in the past years both in war and peace I have been profoundly impressed with the fact that we have increasing need to replace dictation by law to the fullest extent possible by co-operation between the administrative side of our government and the forces in the community. [...] One test of our economic and social system is its capacity to cure its own abuses. [...] If we are to be wholly dependent upon government to cure every evil, we shall by this very method have created an enlarged and deadening abuse through the extension of bureaucracy and the clumsy and incapable handling of delicate economic forces. And much abuse has been and can be cured by inspiration and co-operation rather than by regulation of the government.

The two halves of Hoover's 1928 position side by side: regulation of natural monopolies is affirmed ("must"), but the preferred cure is co-operation in place of regulation. The line against public ownership is firm; the line against regulation is a preference.

### hoover28.12 · Elizabethton, October 6 — the Mississippi flood and "the sneers at Main Street"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (complicates), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Elizabethton speech, "Basic Integrity of American Life"; lines 3988–4022
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> I, with other Americans, have perhaps unduly resented the stream of criticism of American life, of the stature and character of our people. More particularly have I resented the sneers at Main Street. For I have known that in the cottages that lay behind the street rested the strength of our national character. When it came to the organization necessary to meet that great catastrophe, the pressure of time alone made it necessary to rely wholly upon the leadership, intelligence, the devotion, the sense of integrity and service of hundreds of towns and villages on the border of the flood. [...] In the face of that terrific problem that would test the stamina and quality of any people there was not a failure in a single case. [...] And in no country does there exist the intelligence, the devotion, the probity, the ability to rise to a great emergency that exists in the Main Street of the American town and village. I do not wish to disparage the usefulness of Broadway, Pennsylvania Avenue, or State Street, but it is from Main Street and its countryside that the creative energies of the nation must be replenished and restored.

A direct answer to Lewis (*Main Street*, 1920; *Babbitt*, 1922): the small town's voluntary community is claimed as a living check, proved in the 1927 flood. Against C6.13's picture of the checks as gone in the town; for its picture of a business-class "service" ideal defending the town against its critics.

### hoover28.13 · Boston, October 15 — theories weighed "in quantities, not arguments"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (for)
- **Location:** Boston speech, "The Tariff: Theory versus Experience"; lines 4697–4716
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); page number inside the quotation dropped

> This theory was sound enough in the old days of direct barter of goods between nations. The trouble with it is that it has lost most of its practical application in a modern world and especially as applied to the American situation. Economic theories and hypotheses must stand the test of fact or experience or show application to new circumstances. Responsible men cannot dally with critical policies which affect the well-being of peoples on the sole basis of a theory. The birth of modern science was the realization by the scientists that every theory and every hypothesis must be placed upon the scales where the weights were in quantities, not arguments.

The protective tariff, a choice between interests, is defended as what experiment shows and the free-trade case dismissed as mere "theory". This is C9.6's pattern (value choice presented as a finding of science), though Hoover does give the interests he is protecting elsewhere (wages, farmers).

### hoover28.14 · Boston, October 15 — prosperity "the rich soil" of spiritual virtues

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** Boston speech, closing; lines 5095–5113
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "[|" read as "I"

> Here in New England, American business began, and because the prosperity of industry and commerce affects the life of every man and woman, every boy and girl, I have dealt with it in this address. But there are other things more important. Because I talk of business it does not mean that I place material things above spiritual things. On the contrary, I see prosperity merely as the rich soil from which spiritual virtues as well as education and art and satisfactions in life can grow. Your founders came to these shores not through lure of gold; not with the ambition to establish great mercantile enterprises; not with the thirst for adventure. Their first objectives were far different and more lofty.

He denies putting the material first, then makes it the soil from which the spiritual grows. The Puritan founders are invoked for their "lofty" aims, as a credential, not as a rule. Compare .23.

### hoover28.15 · New York, October 22 — "Even if governmental conduct of business could give us more efficiency"

- **Claims:** C9.6 (against), C6.7 (for)
- **Location:** New York speech, "Fatal to True Liberalism"; lines 5730–5745
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> Liberalism is a force truly of the spirit, a force proceeding from the deep realization that economic freedom cannot be sacrificed if political freedom is to be preserved. Even if governmental conduct of business could give us more efficiency instead of less efficiency, the fundamental objection to it would remain unaltered and unabated. It would destroy political equality. It would increase rather than decrease abuse and corruption. It would stifle initiative and invention. It would undermine the development of leadership. It would cramp and cripple the mental and spiritual energies of our people. It would extinguish equality and opportunity. It would dry up the spirit of liberty and progress. For these reasons primarily it must be resisted. For a hundred and fifty years liberalism has found its true spirit in the American system, not in the European systems.

The engineer explicitly refuses to let efficiency decide: the objection is one of values (liberty, equality, "spiritual energies"). Against C9.6's charge that technocrats dress values as science. It is also the core of the anti-statist case that C6.7 says the vocabulary was captured for.

### hoover28.16 · New York, October 22 — "the American system of rugged individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for, against government ownership; complicates, as to regulation), C9.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** New York speech, "The American System", "Suspended by the War", "Restored under Republican Direction"; lines 5353–5364, 5406–5419, 5436–5442
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> But in addition to this great record of contributions of the Republican Party to progress, there has been a further fundamental contribution—a contribution underlying and sustaining all the others—and that is the resistance of the Republican Party to every attempt to inject the government into business in competition with its citizens. [...] When the war closed, the most vital of all issues both in our own country and throughout the world was whether governments should continue their war-time ownership and operation of many instrumentalities of production and distribution. We were challenged with a peace-time choice between the American system of rugged individualism and a European philosophy of diametrically opposed doctrines—doctrines of paternalism and state socialism. The acceptance of these ideas would have meant the destruction of self-government through centralization of government. It would have meant the undermining of the individual initiative and enterprise through which our people have grown to unparalleled greatness. [...] When the Republican Party came into full power it went at once resolutely back to our fundamental conception of the state and the rights and responsibilities of the individual. Thereby it restored confidence and hope in the American people, it freed and stimulated enterprise, it restored the government to its position as an umpire instead of a player in the economic game.

The primary text for "rugged individualism" (the to-do item in hoover-dewey.md). Its opposite is government "ownership and operation", not regulation: the umpire stays on the field. "Rugged individualism" is a name for a whole "American system", set against "European" doctrine, which makes it a national creed rather than a personal one.

### hoover28.17 · New York, October 22 — bureaucracy and "the spirit of submission"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.1 (complicates), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** New York speech, "Dangers of Bureaucracy", "Fatal to True Liberalism"; lines 5680–5709
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> But what of those who are outside the bureaucracy? What is the effect upon their lives? The area of enterprise and opportunity for them to strive and rise is at once limited. [...] Bureaucracy does not tolerate the spirit of independence; it spreads the spirit of submission into our daily life and penetrates the temper of our people not with the habit of powerful resistance to wrong but with the habit of timid acceptance of irresistible might. [...] Bureaucracy is ever desirous of spreading its influence and its power. You cannot extend the mastery of the government over the daily working life of a people without at the same time making it the master of the people's souls and thoughts. [...] Free speech does not live many hours after free industry and free commerce die.

Hoover locates the danger of conformity in the state, never in business or association. That is the argument C6.7 says the pioneer vocabulary was captured for. For C9.1: his opponents too claimed "liberalism"; both sides speak for the free individual against submission.

### hoover28.18 · New York, October 22 — "It is no system of laissez faire"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against, as to Hoover), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** New York speech, "Flexibility of the American System"; lines 5766–5773
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "‘The" read as "The", and the comma before "On" read as a full stop

> Nor do I wish to be misinterpreted as believing that the United States is free-for-all and devil-take-the-hindmost. The very essence of equality of opportunity and of American individualism is that there shall be no domination by any group or combination in this republic, whether it be business or political. On the contrary, it demands economic justice as well as political and social justice. It is no system of laissez faire.

The same night as "rugged individualism", Hoover repeats the 1922 bound almost word for word ("every man for himself and the devil take the hindmost", hoover.02). Individualism, so defined, forbids domination "whether it be business or political".

### hoover28.19 · New York, October 22 — "Practicability of Regulation": "glass pockets"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against, as to Hoover)
- **Location:** New York speech, "Practicability of Regulation"; lines 5827–5864
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> The American people from bitter experience have a rightful fear that great business units might be used to dominate our industrial life and by illegal and unethical practices destroy equality of opportunity. Years ago the Republican administration established the principle that such evils could be corrected by regulation. [...] It insisted upon the principle that when great public utilities were clothed with the security of partial monopoly, whether it be railways, power plants, telephones, or what not, then there must be the fullest and most complete control of rates, services, and finances by government or local agencies. It declared that these businesses must be conducted with glass pockets. [...] One of the great problems of government is to determine to what extent the government shall regulate and control commerce and industry and how much it shall leave it alone. The decisive "against" for C6.7 as applied to Hoover himself: in the "rugged individualism" speech, regulation is affirmed as Republican principle, and business is told to "keep out of government". The capture C6.7 describes is not in this text.

### hoover28.20 · New York, October 22 — "The New Day": "greater and greater service"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (complicates), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** New York speech, "The New Day"; lines 6186–6210
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "oppor-.:" read as "oppor-"

> My conception of America is a land where men and women may walk in ordered freedom in the independent conduct of their occupations; where they may enjoy the advantages of wealth, not concentrated in the hands of the few but spread through the lives of all; where they build and safeguard their homes, and give to their children the fullest advantages and opportunities of American life; where every man shall be respected in the faith that his conscience and his heart direct him to follow; where a contented and happy people, secure in their liberties, free from poverty and fear, shall have the leisure and impulse to seek a fuller life. Some may ask where all this may lead beyond mere material progress. It leads to a release of the energies of men and women from the dull drudgery of life to a wider vision and a higher hope. It leads to the opportunity for greater and greater service, not alone from man to man in our own land, but from our country to the whole world.

The peroration ends on "service", not on the self. Wealth is to be "not concentrated in the hands of the few". The end is the home and "a fuller life"; the self-realising individual of later rhetoric is absent.

### hoover28.21 · St. Louis, November 2 — "scarcely an individual" outside an association; regulation "avoided"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.7 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** St. Louis speech, "The Principle of Co-operation", "Avoidance of Unnecessary Regulation"; lines 6850–6886, 7007–7010
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> We have in the past quarter of a century evolved a higher sense of organized co-operation than has ever been known before. We have ten thousand examples of this conscious co-operative development in the enormous growth of associational activities. Civic associations, chambers of commerce, trade associations, professional associations, labor unions, trade councils, farm organizations, farm co-operatives, welfare associations—these are so all-embracing that there is scarcely an individual in our country who does not now belong to one or more of them. They represent every phase of our national life both on the economic and on the welfare side. They constitute a vast ferment toward conscious co-operation. They have become a part of the very fabric of American life. While some of them engage in highly objectionable attempts to wrongly influence public opinion and the action of government, the majority of them recognize a responsibility to the public as well as to themselves; and a large part of them are founded solely on public interest. [...] It is a method that reinforces our individualism by reducing, and not increasing, government interference in business and the life of our citizens. [...] This is a clear case where by co-operative methods we have avoided the necessity of regulation with the bureaucracy and interference that flow from it. It is also a clear case of building up of self-government.

Hoover's own dating ("the past quarter of a century") and count of association, the C6.13 change seen from inside. Association is not worn away but universal; its value now is that it "reinforces our individualism" by keeping government out. Note the admission that some associations lobby, "objectionable attempts to wrongly influence public opinion".

### hoover28.22 · St. Louis, November 2 — "a moving frontier preserved that equality of opportunity"; "the most strict regulation"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates; for, on the frontier), C6.3 (for)
- **Location:** St. Louis speech, "Equality of Opportunity"; lines 7097–7145
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "T insist" read as "I insist"

> Government has the definite and manifest obligation of giving constructive leadership to the people. In doing so it must not lessen their initiative and enterprise, upon which we must rely for the progress of the race and of the nation. Our system has been built upon the ideal of equality of opportunity. For perhaps a hundred years after the foundation of the Republic, the opportunities of a moving frontier preserved that equality of opportunity. Now with the settlement of the country and with the astonishing speed and intricate complexity of industrial life, the preservation of equality of opportunity becomes yearly and yearly more difficult, and for that very reason is of higher and higher importance. [...] By this principle we should test every act of government, every proposal, whether it be economic or political. I insist upon the most strict regulation of public utilities, because otherwise they would destroy equality of opportunity. I object to the government going into business in competition with its citizens because that would destroy equality of opportunity. And equality of opportunity is the flux with which alone we can melt out full and able leadership to the nation.

Turner's thesis in the candidate's mouth: the frontier did the work that government must now do. The single principle cuts both ways, for "the most strict regulation" and against public ownership. This is the best evidence that in 1928 the line Hoover draws is ownership, not regulation.

### hoover28.23 · St. Louis, November 2 — "foundation stones" of the spiritual; "The truth must be forged from the metal of facts"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C9.6 (for), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** St. Louis speech, "The American Home"; lines 7179–7199
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); OCR "goy-ernmental" read as "governmental"

> I have tried to interpret to my fellow-countrymen what government means to that home. I stand for a prosperous country because I want good homes. You cannot divide those things that are seen from those that are unseen. The things that we call material are the foundation stones upon which we build the temple of those things that we call spiritual. Prosperity, security, happiness, and peace rest on sound economic life. [...] Solution of these questions is not always easy. Only the inexperienced can be positive in offering solutions of great problems. The first necessity in the handling of such problems is the assembling of the facts in their proper perspective. The truth must be forged from the metal of facts.

The order of 1922 is reversed. Then the economic system could not advance "unless it is inspired by things of the spirit" (hoover.03); now the material is the foundation and the spiritual the temple on top. For C9.6: the method is fact-gathering, with a real caution against certainty.

### hoover28.24 · St. Louis, November 2 (quoting his June telegram to the convention) — "My country owes me no debt"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (against), C7.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** St. Louis speech, "Reply to Senator Moses Reaffirmed"; lines 7216–7223
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> No man can establish such an obligation upon any part of the American people. My country owes me no debt. It gave me, as it gives every boy and girl, a chance. It gave me schooling, independence of action, opportunity for service and honor. In no other land could a boy from a country village, without inheritance or influential friends, look forward with unbounded hope.

The orphan from West Branch, the type of the self-made man, credits his rise to the country, not to himself. Against C5.14's self as its own origin. But the "chance" is given equally to "every boy and girl", which leaves the difference in outcome to the person (.02).

### hoover28.25 · Palo Alto, November 5 — "a nation of individuals"; "our rugged individualism, together with [...] equality of opportunity"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C8.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** closing radio address, Palo Alto; lines 7375–7383, 7408–7417
- **Checked:** text file (OCR)

> I differ widely with many of the principles and views advocated by our opponents and the proposals which they have put forward. But it is not my purpose to review these questions. We are, or should be, a nation of individuals, and should make independent determination of our conclusions. Democracy cannot survive if the ballot is to be cast upon somebody's order or direction. [...] This election is of more momentous order than for many years because we have entered into a new era of economic and moral action, not only in our country but in the world at large. Our national task is to meet our many new problems, and in meeting them to courageously preserve our rugged individualism, together with the principles of ordered liberty and freedom, equality of opportunity, with that idealism to which our nation has been consecrated and which has brought us to the leadership of the world.

The second and last use of the phrase. Here it carries no enemy at all; it stands in a list with "ordered liberty", "equality of opportunity" and "idealism". Individualism means independent judgment at the ballot, not acquisition.
