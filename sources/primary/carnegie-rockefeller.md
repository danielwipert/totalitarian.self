## Summary of the reading (2026-10-25)

**C6.7 (the pioneer / self-made vocabulary).** Complicates. Wilson and Brandeis reported the combiners' defence as necessity and progress, and the combiners' own words bear this out: Carnegie calls the change "beyond our power to alter" and it is "a waste of time to criticize the inevitable" (carnegie.01). Concentration is "a necessity" and "an evolution from the heterogeneous to the homogeneous" (carnegie.14). Rockefeller: "The day of individual competition in large affairs is past and gone" and combinations "are a necessity" (rockefeller.04). The phrase "the day of combination is here," often given as his, is *not* in the *Reminiscences*; I searched for it, and the nearest line is the one just quoted. But the self-made vocabulary *is* there too. It is used for the man's own life story, not as the defence of the combination. Carnegie gives the bobbin boy, the "self-reliant men" who spring from poverty (carnegie.10), and the wish to be "my own master" (carnegie.11). Rockefeller says "we had to hew our own paths along new lines" (rockefeller.05). Both men put the pioneer conditions in the *past*. the day of "small concerns" for "many able men" is "over, never to return" (carnegie.13), and young men now enter "a heritage" (rockefeller.05). Neither turns the pioneer story against regulation: Rockefeller asks for federal incorporation and "state supervision" (rockefeller.04), and Carnegie wants heavy, graduated death duties (carnegie.04). So from the business side the vocabulary is split, not captured: self-made for the biography, necessity and evolution for the corporation. Whether it later becomes "a war cry" against regulation (Dewey, 1930) is not tested here.

**C9.8 (the corporation as a Self, or a structure run by men).** Mostly for the revised claim: a structure run by men. Rockefeller credits "the efficient men who are the controlling forces of the organization" (rockefeller.01). He says it is "the character of the men behind these things" that makes a business (rockefeller.06), and that "nor is its success due to any one man" (ch. III, line 1371). Carnegie *distrusts* the corporation: there "the real owners are unknown" and all depends on "salaried officials." He prefers the partnership, where "the eye of the master" governs (carnegie.13). One complication: Rockefeller writes that "a corporation may be moral or immoral, just as a man may be" (rockefeller.04). This is a moral analogy, not a Self, but it is the nearest these texts come to a corporate person. Enlargement is real: "This bigger system grows bigger men" (carnegie.15). So is the compulsion to grow: "When the manufacturer of steel ceases to grow he begins to decay" (carnegie.11). But the men who are enlarged also **bind themselves**, which runs against the "admitting no frame" half of SPEC §9.3. They bind themselves by trusteeship (carnegie.05, .06; rockefeller.11), by "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced" (carnegie.06), by the 1868 vow "Beyond this never earn" (carnegie.16), and by Rockefeller's nightly "go steady" (rockefeller.02).

**C6.13 (voluntary checks redirected).** For, with a new detail. Every check C6.13 names is present and endorsed: the church (rockefeller.03; carnegie.08), the community's verdict, "The community will surely judge" (carnegie.05), the State taking its share at death (carnegie.04), and local charity carried locally (Reminiscences ch. VII, lines 3581–3587). The redirection runs through business itself. Rockefeller's first ambition to earn came from raising a church debt (rockefeller.03). He then reorganizes giving on "the principle of organization" and "combination" (rockefeller.12; ch. VII, lines 3544–3545), and Carnegie makes the millionaire a "trustee" who administers for the poor "better than they would or could do for themselves" (carnegie.05). So the check is not simply worn away; it is taken over by the same methods that made the fortune. Carnegie's church is a check that must be "supported at home" (carnegie.08): he refuses to let the donor carry it, which keeps it voluntary and local.

**C5.14 / C7.4 (the self as cause of its condition; failure blamed on the person).** Strongly for, and inside a frame. Rockefeller: "the failures which a man makes in his life are due almost always to some defect in his personality," and "the principal cause for the economic differences between people is their difference in personality" (rockefeller.10). Carnegie: want is "the necessary result (accident and sickness excepted) of their own bad habits," and "the true reformer looks more to their habits than to existing conditions" (carnegie.12). This is C7.4 a generation before Trine, without mind-cure. The frame is still there. For Carnegie it is the Spencerian law of competition, which may be hard on the individual but is best "for the race" (carnegie.02), and Christ's words and "the gates of Paradise" (carnegie.09). For Rockefeller it is "natural law" and the "laws of trade" (rockefeller.07). Against or complicating: Carnegie's own family was ruined by structure, not habit. His father "was one of the sufferers by the change" to the factory system (Introduction, lines 356–358). Rockefeller calls "the very poor" probably "the most generous people in the world" (rockefeller.09). Both men credit their *own* success to others: associates, a father's training, a banker's trust (rockefeller.01; Introduction, lines 720–753). So in these texts failure is put on the person, but success is not claimed as the person's alone. At Homestead, Carnegie blames "The men" and his superintendent, not the structure (carnegie.18).

**The pattern (structure kept, limit dropped).** These men keep several limits, and the essay can name them. (1) **Death and the State**: wealth left at death should be heavily taxed (carnegie.04). (2) **Public sentiment**: the community's judgment (carnegie.05, .06). (3) **God**: the needle's eye and Paradise (carnegie.09); Rockefeller's church work (rockefeller.03). (4) **Natural law**: competition as "that great corrective" (carnegie.14), the "laws of trade" (rockefeller.07). (5) **The body**: the rich "cannot purchase a good digestion" (Reminiscences ch. VI, lines 2928–2930). (6) **Self-imposed caps**: the 1868 memorandum (carnegie.16) and "go steady" (rockefeller.02). (7) **Trusteeship**: the wealth is not finally the man's own (carnegie.06; rockefeller.11). The structure here is the self-made man who is the cause of his own and others' condition. The limit attached is trusteeship and the disgrace of dying rich. If the later Self keeps the self-made story and drops trusteeship, that fits the pattern exactly, but it needs a later witness (Hill, 1937; the 1920s business press). One caution: Carnegie admits his own will has no inner brake ("Whatever I engage in I must push inordinately," carnegie.16), and the firm cannot stop growing (carnegie.11). His limits are *external* and *chosen*, which is just what C6.1 says can be left.

**C8.7 in passing.** Trusteeship is a high moral word put on control: the millionaire administers "far better than it could or would have done for itself" (carnegie.06). Rockefeller's "combination and coöperation" (rockefeller.12) is the same word Brandeis caught Morgan using (brandeis.13).

**C9.1.** Nothing here bears on it directly.

### carnegie.01 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Gospel of Wealth" pp. 1–2 — "a waste of time to criticize the inevitable"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against, as written; for the revised wording)
- **Location:** Part I, pp. 1–2; `texts/carnegie-gospel-of-wealth.txt` lines 948–951, 987–991
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> The problem of our age is the proper administration of wealth, that the ties of brotherhood may still bind together the rich and poor in harmonious relationship. [...] But whether the change be for good or ill, it is upon us, beyond our power to alter, and, therefore, to be accepted and made the best of. It is a waste of time to criticize the inevitable.

The essay opens in the vocabulary of necessity that Wilson and Brandeis report, not in pioneer terms. Note also the first aim: "the ties of brotherhood" between rich and poor, which is a relation and not self-sufficiency.

### carnegie.02 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Gospel of Wealth" pp. 4–5 — the law of competition and "the race"

- **Checked:** text file (OCR); garbled words at lines 1066–1068, 1072–1079 and 1117–1123 read and corrected (e.g. "indivj4jja]J : tJsJ^sLfQr..thfi race" as "individual, it is best for the race"); not yet checked against scan

> The price which society pays for the law of competition, like the price it pays for cheap comforts and luxuries, is also great; but the advantages of this law are also greater still than its cost—for it is to this law that we owe our wonderful material development, which brings improved conditions in its train. But, whether the law be benign or not, we must say of it, as we say of the change in the conditions of men to which we have referred: It is here; we cannot evade it; no substitutes for it have been found; and while the law may be sometimes hard for the individual, it is best for the race, because it insures the survival of the fittest in every department. We accept and welcome, therefore, as conditions to which we must accommodate ourselves, great inequality of environment; the concentration of business, industrial and commercial, in the hands of a few; and the law of competition between these, as being not only beneficial, but essential to the future progress of the race. [...] It is a law, as certain as any of the others named, that men possessed of this peculiar talent for affairs, under the free play of economic forces must, of necessity, soon be in receipt of more revenue than can be judiciously expended upon themselves; and this law is as beneficial for the race as the others.

The individual's fate is put inside a law and "the race." The frame is Spencerian, not God. The rich man's surplus is itself a law of nature, not his own making. This is the self bounded by a borrowed frame, as in C5.14 step 1, but the frame is evolution.

- **Checked:** text file (OCR); lines 1142–1143 and 1183 corrected ("for_ciyilization", "da y when t he.capable", "Jh^changing of hu man nature"); not yet checked against scan

> [...] for civilization took its start from the day when the capable, industrious workman said to his incompetent and lazy fellow, "If thou dost not sow, thou shalt not reap," and thus ended primitive Communism by separating the drones from the bees. One who studies this subject will soon be brought face to face with the conclusion that upon the sacredness of property civilization itself depends—the right of the laborer to his hundred dollars in the savings-bank, and equally the legal right of the millionaire to his millions. [...] To those who propose to substitute Communism for this intense Individualism, the answer therefore is: The race has tried that. All progress from that barbarous day to the present time has resulted from its displacement. Not evil, but good, has come to the race from the accumulation of wealth by those who have had the ability and energy to produce it. [...] This is not evolution, but revolution. It necessitates the changing of human nature itself—a work of eons, even if it were good to change it, which we cannot know.

This is the essay on individualism versus socialism that the brief asked for; it sits inside "Wealth" itself. Poverty sorts the "incompetent and lazy" from the "capable" (C7.4). But "human nature" is fixed and slow: the self does not remake itself; the race evolves over "eons."

### carnegie.04 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Gospel of Wealth" p. 11 — the State's share; wealth "from which it chiefly came"

- **Location:** Part I, p. 11; lines 1365–1379

> Men who continue hoarding great sums all their lives, the proper use of which for public ends would work good to the community from which it chiefly came, should be made to feel that the community, in the form of the State, cannot thus be deprived of its proper share. By taxing estates heavily at death the State marks its condemnation of the selfish millionaire's unworthy life.

The richest man in the argument says the wealth "chiefly came" from the community, which is the reverse of "from which all value is imagined to flow." He invites the State to limit him at death. This is a limit kept, and kept by the man himself.

### carnegie.05 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Gospel of Wealth" pp. 15–16 — "the mere trustee and agent for his poorer brethren"

> This, then, is held to be the duty of the man of wealth: To set an example of modest, unostentatious living, shunning display or extravagance; to provide moderately for the legitimate wants of those dependent upon him; and, after doing so, to consider all surplus revenues which come to him simply as trust funds, which he is called upon to administer, and strictly bound as a matter of duty to administer in the manner which, in his judgment, is best calculated to produce the most beneficial results for the community—the man of wealth thus becoming the mere trustee and agent for his poorer brethren, bringing to their service his superior wisdom, experience, and ability to administer, doing for them better than they would or could do for themselves. Trusteeship in the trustee's own words. Wilson reported it as the combiners' defence; it is also a duty the man puts on himself, and the "community" is the judge. But the last clause is C8.7: a high moral word over the claim to judge for others "better than they would or could do for themselves."

### carnegie.06 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Gospel of Wealth" pp. 18–19 — "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against), C6.13 (for); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** Part I, pp. 18–19; lines 1663–1697
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); lines 1664 and 1697 corrected ("accumulationw ill be left f ree"; "The ^ man who die s thus rich dies disgraced£____"); not yet checked against scan. The sentence is repeated cleanly in Part II, line 1794.

> Thus is the problem of rich and poor to be solved. The laws of accumulation will be left free, the laws of distribution free. Individualism will continue, but the millionaire will be but a trustee for the poor, intrusted for a season with a great part of the increased wealth of the community, but administering it for the community far better than it could or would have done for itself. The best minds will thus have reached a stage in the development of the race in which it is clearly seen that there is no mode of disposing of surplus wealth creditable to thoughtful and earnest men into whose hands it flows, save by using it year by year for the general good. This day already dawns. Men may die without incurring the pity of their fellows, still sharers in great business enterprises from which their capital cannot be or has not been withdrawn, and which is left chiefly at death for public uses; yet the day is not far distant when the man who dies leaving behind him millions of available wealth, which was free for him to administer during life, will pass away "unwept, unhonored, and unsung," no matter to what uses he leaves the dross which he cannot take with him. Of such as these the public verdict will then be: "The man who dies thus rich dies disgraced."

The limit is set by "the public verdict," and it falls on the self that holds wealth: the fortune is the community's, held "for a season." "Advantages of Poverty" puts it more sharply: "the aim of the millionaire should be to die poor" (lines 3054–3055). Individualism is kept and bound in the same sentence.

### carnegie.07 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Best Fields for Philanthropy" p. 22 — the "irreclaimably destitute" to be "isolated"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** Part II, p. 22; lines 1813–1837
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); line 1830 corrected ("_pf wordy"). "opportunies" is as in the text.

> It is not the irreclaimably destitute, shiftless, and worthless which it is truly beneficial or truly benevolent for the individual to attempt to reach and improve. For these there exists the refuge provided by the city or the State, where they can be sheltered, fed, clothed, and kept in comfortable existence, and—most important of all—where they can be isolated from the well-doing and industrious poor, who are liable to be demoralized by contact with these unfortunates. One man or woman who succeeds in living comfortably by begging is more dangerous to society, and a greater obstacle to the progress of humanity, than a score of wordy Socialists. The individual administrator of surplus wealth has as his charge the industrious and ambitious; not those who need everything done for them, but those who, being most anxious and able to help themselves, deserve and will be benefited by help from others and by the extension of their opportunies by the aid of the philanthropic rich.

The poor are sorted by character into those who help themselves and the "worthless." This bears on C6.13's class split: philanthropy is aimed at the aspiring, and the rest are left to the State.

### carnegie.08 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Best Fields for Philanthropy" pp. 39–41 — the church as "center of social life," supported "at home"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** Part II, "Seventh," pp. 39–41; lines 2555–2575, 2614–2619
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> Churches as fields for the use of surplus wealth have purposely been reserved until the last, because, these being sectarian, every man will be governed in his action in regard to them by his own attachments; therefore gifts to churches, it may be said, are not, in one sense, gifts to the community at large, but to special classes. Nevertheless, every millionaire may know of a district where the little cheap, uncomfortable, and altogether unworthy wooden structure stands at the cross-roads, in which the whole neighborhood gathers on Sunday, and which, independently of the form of the doctrines taught, is the center of social life and source of neighborly feeling. The voluntary check of C6.1 and C6.13 in a donor's words: the church is valued for its neighbourhood, "independently of the form of the doctrines." It is ranked last among fields for giving, and the donor holds back on purpose so that it stays the congregation's own.

> In Christ's day, it is evident, reformers were against the wealthy. ### carnegie.10 · *Gospel of Wealth*, Introduction "How I Served My Apprenticeship" pp. xii–xiii — "self-reliant men" from "honest poverty"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C7.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** Introduction (1896), pp. xii–xiii; lines 525–534, 548–554
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> It is because I know how sweet and happy and pure the home of honest poverty is, how free from perplexing care, from social envies and emulations, how loving and how united its members may be in the common interest of supporting the family, that I sympathize with the rich man's boy and congratulate the poor man's boy; and it is for these reasons that from the ranks of the poor so many strong, eminent, self-reliant men have always sprung and always must spring. [...] It seems, nowadays, a matter of universal desire that poverty should be abolished. We should be quite willing to abolish luxury, but to abolish honest, industrious, self-denying poverty would be to destroy the soil upon which mankind produces the virtues which enable our race to reach a still higher civilization than it now possesses.

The self-made vocabulary from a corporate leader: "self-reliant men" from poverty. It is the "Youth's Companion" origin story, and it is set inside the family ("the common interest of supporting the family"), not the lone self. Compare lines 356–366: his father was ruined by the factory system, which is a structural cause that Carnegie names for his own family.

### carnegie.11 · *Gospel of Wealth*, Introduction pp. xxi–xxii — "my own master"; "ceases to grow he begins to decay"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C9.8 (complicates); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** Introduction, pp. xxi–xxii; lines 877–893, 916–924
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> I was no longer merely an official working for others upon a salary, but a full-fledged business man working upon my own account.
>
> I never was quite reconciled to working for other people. At the most, the railway officer has to look forward to the enjoyment of a stated salary, and he has a great many people to please; even if he gets to be president, he has sometimes a board of directors who cannot know what is best to be done; and even if this board be satisfied, he has a board of stockholders to criticize him, and as the property is not his own he cannot manage it as he pleases.
>
> I always liked the idea of being my own master, of manufacturing something and giving employment to many men. [...] Always we are hoping that we need expand no farther; yet ever we are finding that to stop expanding would be to fall behind; and even to-day the successive improvements and inventions follow each other so rapidly that we see just as much yet to be done as ever.
>
> When the manufacturer of steel ceases to grow he begins to decay, so we must keep on extending.

Two findings. First, the self-made, independent vocabulary is spoken by the steel master, and it is aimed *against* the corporation (boards, stockholders). Second, the firm has no limit of its own: it must grow or decay. That is the structure without a limit, and here it is located in the enterprise, not in a Self.

### carnegie.12 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "The Advantages of Poverty" pp. 51–52 — "their own bad habits"; "habits" over "existing conditions"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C5.14 (for)
- **Location:** "The Advantages of Poverty" (1891), pp. 51–52; lines 2928–2937, 2966–2975
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> Good and charitable workers among the poor can best accelerate this decreasing process, until something like the American figure is reached, by instilling within the working-classes of Britain those feelings of manly self-respect and those habits of sobriety and thrift which distinguish their race here, and keep it almost free, not only from pauperism, but from want or extreme poverty, except as the necessary result (accident and sickness excepted) of their own bad habits. [...] In these days of excitement and exaggeration, let it always be borne in mind that at no period in the history of the English-speaking race, wherever that race resides, has it been so easy as it is to-day for the masses not only to earn comfortable livelihoods, but to save and have money in bank for a rainy day. When they fail to do so, the true reformer looks more to their habits than to existing conditions for a satisfactory explanation.

The plainest C7.4 sentence in these texts: failure is put on the person's habits rather than on conditions, in 1891, with no mind-cure. Two qualifications matter: "accident and sickness excepted," and the claim rests on a reading of conditions (that saving has never been easier).

### carnegie.13 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "Popular Illusions about Trusts" pp. 87–88 — "the eye of the master"; small concerns "over, never to return"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against), C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** "Popular Illusions about Trusts" (1900), pp. 87–88; lines 4276–4290, 4306–4317
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> Another highly important step forward in this domain resulted from the authorization of limited partnerships, by which the undoubted advantages of individual over corporate management could be secured without danger of ruin to the members, whose liability is limited to the amount of the capital stock of the partnership. In the great corporation the shares are generally bought and sold upon the stock exchange, and the real owners are unknown. All depends upon salaried officials, who may or may not have a dollar in the enterprise. In the limited partnership, on the contrary, only shareholders can be members; the shares are not sold to outsiders, and thus is insured the eye of the master over all. [...] By this plan it is possible to provide for the rise of the poor but able employee, thus neutralizing, to some extent, the acknowledged difficulty of men rising to ownership in our day, because of the enormous amount of capital required for successful operations under present, and probably enduring, conditions. The day of small concerns within the means of many able men seems to be over, never to return. The rise to partnership in vast concerns must come chiefly through such means as these permitted by the laws of limited partnership.

The corporation is not a Self for Carnegie; it is ownerless and run by hired men, and he prefers the partnership. He also concedes Wilson's point: the conditions for the self-made rise are "over, never to return." The rise now passes through admission to the firm. Compare *Autobiography* ch. XVI, line 6999: "the great advantage of a partnership over a corporation."

### carnegie.14 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "Popular Illusions about Trusts" pp. 88–89 — "an evolution from the heterogeneous to the homogeneous"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** "Popular Illusions about Trusts," pp. 88–89; lines 4328–4347
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> We see in all these efforts of men the desire to furnish opportunities to mass capital, to concentrate the small savings of the many and to direct them to one end. The conditions of human society create for this an imperious demand; the concentration of capital is a necessity for meeting the demands of our day, and as such should not be looked at askance, but be encouraged. The defence of combination is Spencer's law (the formula is his, in reverse order), plus "necessity," not self-making. The same essay keeps a limit on the trusts: each failed monopoly bears a wound "inflicted by that great corrective, competition" (lines 4889–4893).

### carnegie.15 · *Gospel of Wealth*, "Popular Illusions about Trusts" p. 94 — "This bigger system grows bigger men"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (complicates); bears on open question 4
- **Location:** "Popular Illusions about Trusts," p. 94; lines 4553–4571
- **Checked:** text file (OCR); clean in these lines

> In other words, the small, petty master in his little store has given place to the bigger, much more important manager of a department, whose revenues generally exceed those of the petty owner he has supplanted. Nor is this all: the field for the display of exceptional ability is much wider than it could possibly be in the smaller establishment, and will as often win partnership in one of these establishments, or at least an equivalent of partnership, as the owner of the small store achieved success. This bigger system grows bigger men, and it is by the big men that the standard of the race is raised. The race of shopkeepers is bound to be improved, and to become not only better business men, and better men in themselves, but more valuable citizens for the State. Dealing with petty affairs tends to make small men; dealing with larger affairs broadens and strengthens character.

Open question 4 answered from the business side: the organization *enlarges* selves, and Carnegie says this is good. It is Wilson's "exalted" few (wilson.01) seen from inside, and stretched to the managers below them. The enlarged self is still measured by "character" and service to "the State."

### carnegie.16 · *Autobiography* ch. XI, footnote 30 — the 1868 memorandum: "Beyond this never earn"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** ch. XI "New York as Headquarters," editor's footnote 30 (memorandum dated St. Nicholas Hotel, December 1868); `texts/carnegie-autobiography.txt` lines 5054–5079
- **Checked:** text file

> Thirty-three and an income of $50,000 per annum! By this time two years I can so arrange all my business as to secure at least $50,000 per annum. Beyond this never earn--make no effort to increase fortune, but spend the surplus each year for benevolent purposes. Cast aside business forever, except for others. [...] Man must have an idol--the amassing of wealth is one of the worst species of idolatry--no idol more debasing than the worship of money. Whatever I engage in I must push inordinately; therefore should I be careful to choose that life which will be the most elevating in its character. To continue much longer overwhelmed by business cares and with most of my thoughts wholly upon the way to make more money in the shortest time, must degrade me beyond hope of permanent recovery.

A self that knows it has no inner brake ("I must push inordinately") and so sets an outer one. He did not keep the vow; he went on for thirty-three more years. Ch. XIX (lines 8086–8089) records the later decision: "it was inevitable that I should live up to its teachings by ceasing to struggle for more wealth." The limit is chosen, and for decades it was not kept, which bears on C6.1's checks that could be left.

### carnegie.17 · *Autobiography* ch. XIV — "the standpoint of the evolutionist"; "All our duties lie in this world"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C7.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. XIV "Partners, Books, and Travel"; lines 6534–6553
- **Checked:** text file

> A new horizon was opened up to me by this voyage. It quite changed my intellectual outlook. Spencer and Darwin were then high in the zenith, and I had become deeply interested in their work. I began to view the various phases of human life from the standpoint of the evolutionist. [...] The result of my journey was to bring a certain mental peace. Where there had been chaos there was now order. My mind was at rest. I had a philosophy at last. The words of Christ "The Kingdom of Heaven is within you," had a new meaning for me. Not in the past or in the future, but now and here is Heaven within us. The frame changes, but it is not dropped. Evolution replaces theology (lines 6548–6550), and "Heaven within us" moves the sacred inward, close to Emerson and the Over-Soul. But it still gives "duties," and they are this-worldly. This is a halfway point between Smiles's Providence and Trine's mind.

### carnegie.18 · *Autobiography* ch. XVII — Homestead: "The men were outrageously wrong"

- **Checked:** text file

> I was traveling in the Highlands of Scotland when the trouble arose, and did not hear of it until two days after. ### rockefeller.01 · *Reminiscences* ch. I — "much more credit than I deserve"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for, as revised), C6.7 (against)
- **Location:** ch. I "Some Old Friends"; `texts/rockefeller-reminiscences.txt` lines 227–232
- **Checked:** text file

> Speaking of Mr. Archbold leads me to say again that I have received much more credit than I deserve in connection with the Standard Oil Company. It was my good fortune to help to bring together the efficient men who are the controlling forces of the organization and to work hand in hand with them for many years, but it is they who have done the hard tasks.

Not the self-made man: the founder credits the "efficient men" and his "good fortune." The organization is a group of men, and the self-description is the organizer's, not the pioneer's. Compare ch. ### rockefeller.02 · *Reminiscences* ch. II — "go steady": the nightly self-check

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** ch. II "The Difficult Art of Getting"; lines 1040–1050
- **Checked:** text file

> Then, and indeed for many years after, it seemed as though there was no end to the money needed to carry on and develop the business. As our successes began to come, I seldom put my head upon the pillow at night without speaking a few words to myself in this wise:
>
> "Now a little success, soon you will fall down, soon you will be overthrown. Because you have got a start, you think you are quite a merchant; look out, or you will lose your head--go steady." These intimate conversations with myself, I am sure, had a great influence on my life. I was afraid I could not stand my prosperity, and tried to teach myself not to get puffed up with any foolish notions.

The self binds itself against its own enlargement, in a nightly practice like a Puritan self-examination. Ch. I adds: "I know of nothing more despicable and pathetic than a man who devotes all the waking hours of the day to making money for money's sake" (lines 563–565).

### rockefeller.03 · *Reminiscences* ch. II — church trustee at seventeen; "my first ambition to earn more money"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for, with a twist)
- **Location:** ch. II, section "Raising Church Funds"; lines 1132–1136, 1158–1165
- **Checked:** text file

> When I was but seventeen or eighteen I was elected as a trustee in the church. It was a mission branch, and occasionally I had to hear members who belonged to the main body speak of the mission as though it were not quite so good as the big mother church. This strengthened our resolve to show them that we could paddle our own canoe. [...] This campaign for raising the money which started that morning after church, lasted for several months. The church is the voluntary check of C6.1. It is also where the ambition to earn begins, and Rockefeller says so himself. The congregation trains the money-raiser. Note "trustee" here, a generation before Carnegie's use of the word.

### rockefeller.04 · *Reminiscences* ch. III, "The Modern Corporation" — "The day of individual competition in large affairs is past and gone"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against), C9.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III "The Standard Oil Company," section "The Modern Corporation"; lines 1391–1413, 1447–1473
- **Checked:** text file

> Beyond question there is a suspicion of corporations. There may be reason for such suspicion very often; for a corporation may be moral or immoral, just as a man may be moral or the reverse; but it is folly to condemn all corporations because some are bad, or even to be unduly suspicious of all, because some are bad. But the corporation in form and character has come to stay--that is a thing that may be depended upon. Even small firms are becoming corporations, because it is a convenient form of partnership.
>
> It is equally true that combinations of capital are bound to continue and to grow, and this need not alarm even the most timid if the corporation, or the series of corporations, is properly conducted with due regard for the rights of others. The day of individual competition in large affairs is past and gone--you might just as well argue that we should go back to hand labour and throw away our efficient machines--and the sober good sense of the people will accept this fact when they have studied and tried it out. [...] It is too late to argue about advantages of industrial combinations. They are a necessity. [...] Combination is necessary and its abuses can be minimized; otherwise our legislators must acknowledge their incapacity to deal with the most important instrument of industry.
>
> In the hearing of the Industrial Commission in 1899, I then said that if I were to suggest any legislation regarding industrial combinations it would be: First, Federal legislation under which corporations may be created and regulated, if that be possible. Second, in lieu thereof, state legislation as nearly uniform as possible, encouraging combinations of persons and capital for the purpose of carrying on industries, but permitting state supervision, not of a character to hamper industries, but sufficient to prevent frauds upon the public. I still feel as I did in 1899.

This is the passage behind "the day of combination is here," which is not in the book. Its vocabulary is necessity and machinery, and it ends asking for federal regulation; there is no war cry against regulation. For C9.8, the corporation is "moral or immoral, just as a man may be," a moral analogy. It is also "a convenient form of partnership," an "instrument." It is not a Self.

### rockefeller.05 · *Reminiscences* ch. III, "The New Opportunities" — "we had to hew our own paths"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for, complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III, section "The New Opportunities"; lines 1478–1489, 1496–1498
- **Checked:** text file

> I am far from believing that this will adversely affect the individual. The great economic era we are entering will give splendid opportunity to the young man of the future. One often hears the men of this new generation say that they do not have the chances that their fathers and grandfathers had. How little they know of the disadvantages from which we suffered! In my young manhood we had everything to do and nothing to do it with; we had to hew our own paths along new lines; we had little experience to go on. The pioneer image does appear in Rockefeller, and right after his case for combination. It answers the reformers' complaint (Wilson's "men who are on the make") by putting hardship in the past and calling the present "a heritage." So the vocabulary is contested here: the same words, used to say the opposite of what Wilson said.

### rockefeller.06 · *Reminiscences* ch. IV, "Character the Essential Thing" — "the coming together of the men"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (for, as revised)
- **Location:** ch. IV "Some Experiences in the Oil Business," section "Character the Essential Thing"; lines 1987–1995
- **Checked:** text file

> In speaking of the real beginning of the Standard Oil Company, it should be remembered that it was not so much the consolidation of the firms in which we had a personal interest, but the coming together of the men who had the combined brain power to do the work, which was the actual starting-point. ### rockefeller.07 · *Reminiscences* ch. V, "Follow the Laws of Trade" — "you must have a larger ambition"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C7.4 (complicates); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** ch. V "Other Business Experiences and Business Principles," section "Follow the Laws of Trade"; lines 2805–2808, 2817–2829
- **Checked:** text file

> The underlying, essential element of success in business affairs is to follow the established laws of high-class dealing. Keep to broad and sure lines, and study them to be certain that they are correct ones. Watch the natural operations of trade, and keep within them. [...] Study diligently your capital requirements, and fortify yourself fully to cover possible set-backs, because you can absolutely count on meeting set-backs. Success-advice in the Smiles mode, but the self stays "within" laws it did not make, and its real capital is others' "confidence" in it. The frame is natural law, kept.

### rockefeller.08 · *Reminiscences* ch. VI, "Disinterested Service the Road to Success" — "not needed fail, and ought to fail"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C6.7 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. VI "The Difficult Art of Giving," section "Disinterested Service the Road to Success"; lines 2989–2998, 3017–3021
- **Checked:** text file

> Investigation will show that the great fortunes which have been made in this country, and the same is probably true of other lands, have come to men who have performed great and far-reaching economic services--men who, with great faith in the future of their country, have done most for the development of its resources. The man will be most successful who confers the greatest service on the world. Commercial enterprises that are needed by the public will pay. Commercial enterprises that are not needed fail, and ought to fail. [...] The penalty of a selfish attempt to make the world confer a living without contributing to the progress or happiness of mankind is generally a failure to the individual. The same section calls the duplicate competitor a waster "taking the bread from the labourer" (lines 3004–3008): competition, Carnegie's "great corrective," is a vice to Rockefeller.

### rockefeller.09 · *Reminiscences* ch. VI, "The Generosity of Service" — "the most generous people in the world are the very poor"

- **Location:** ch. VI, section "The Generosity of Service"; lines 3026–3041
- **Checked:** text file

> Probably the most generous people in the world are the very poor, who assume each other's burdens in the crises which come so often to the hard pressed. The mother in the tenement falls ill and the neighbour in the next room assumes her burdens. The father loses his work, and neighbours supply food to his children from their own scanty store. ### rockefeller.10 · *Reminiscences* ch. VI, "The Fundamental Thing in All Help" — failure "due almost always to some defect in his personality"

- **Claims:** C7.4 (for), C5.14 (for)
- **Location:** ch. VI, section "The Fundamental Thing in All Help"; lines 3231–3258
- **Checked:** text file

> Men who are studying the problem of disease tell us that it is becoming more and more evident that the forces which conquer sickness are within the body itself, and that it is only when these are reduced below the normal that disease can get a foothold. [...] In the same way the failures which a man makes in his life are due almost always to some defect in his personality, some weakness of body, or mind, or character, will, or temperament. The only way to overcome these failings is to build up his personality from within, so that he, by virtue of what is within him, may overcome the weakness which was the cause of the failure. It is only those efforts the man himself puts forth that can really help him.
>
> We all desire to see the widest possible distribution of the blessings of life. Many crude plans have been suggested, some of which utterly ignore the essential facts of human nature, and if carried out would perhaps drag our whole civilization down into hopeless misery. It is my belief that the principal cause for the economic differences between people is their difference in personality, and that it is only as we can assist in the wider distribution of those qualities which go to make up a strong personality that we can assist in the wider distribution of wealth. Under normal conditions the man who is strong in body, in mind, in character, and in will need never suffer want. But these qualities can never be developed in a man unless by his own efforts, and the most that any other can do for him is, as I have said, to help him to help himself.

The strongest C7.4 and C5.14 text in the file: the self is the cause of its condition, and wealth follows "personality." It comes in 1909 from the richest man in America, not from New Thought, and with the medical image of forces "within" that is close to mind-cure. Hedges: "almost always," "Under normal conditions."

### rockefeller.11 · *Reminiscences* ch. VI, "Some Underlying Principles" — "they do not and cannot use them for themselves"

- **Claims:** C9.8 (against), C8.7 (complicates); bears on the pattern
- **Location:** ch. VI, section "Some Underlying Principles"; lines 3363–3384
- **Checked:** text file

> It may be asked: How is it consistent with the universal diffusion of these blessings that vast sums of money should be in single hands? The reply is, as I see it, that, while men of wealth control great sums of money, they do not and cannot use them for themselves. They have, indeed, the legal title to large properties, and they do control the investment of them, but that is as far as their own relation to them extends or can extend. The money is universally diffused, in the sense that it is kept invested, and it passes into the pay-envelope week by week.
>
> Up to the present time no scheme has yet presented itself which seems to afford a better method of handling capital than that of individual ownership. We might put our money into the Treasury of the Nation and of the various states, but we do not find any promise in the National or state legislatures, viewed from the experiences of the past, that the funds would be expended for the general weal more effectively than under the present methods, nor do we find in any of the schemes of socialism a promise that wealth would be more wisely administered for the general good. It is the duty of men of means to maintain the title to their property and to administer their funds until some man, or body of men, shall rise up capable of administering for the general good the capital of the country better than they can.

Rockefeller's own trusteeship, with a provisional limit: the rich hold title only "until" better administrators arise. The self's relation to its wealth is shrunk to control of investment. That is a limit spoken, and also a defence of keeping control (C8.7). Compare "Limitations of the Rich" (lines 2921–2937): the rich "cannot purchase a good digestion," a bodily limit.
