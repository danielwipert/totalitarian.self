## Reading summary

**Does it forsake Reason (C9.2)? Mostly no, in 1910–15.** Most of the essays argue *from* scholarship and evidence, against scholarship and evidence. Hague says "No really healthy Christian mind can advocate obscurantism" and faults Driver because "of proofs or reason there is not a trace" (.01, .02). Orr accepts "some form of evolutionary origin of species" and long creation-periods (.04). Wright says the Bible "teaches a system of evolution" and calls Christianity "a free-born son in the family of the inductive sciences" (.05). Torrey stakes everything on historical proof: if the resurrection "cannot be established, Christianity must go" (.06). The *enemy* is named over and over: not reason but "rationalism", meaning naturalistic presuppositions (Hague .02, Reeve .10). The one essay that does reject reason outright is Mauro's "Modern Philosophy": philosophy and revelation are "utterly irreconcilable" (.12). So C9.2 as written ("forsook Reason") fails for the founding text. It may hold for a later stage, which this text cannot test.

**Does it forbid doubt (C9.3)? It ends doubt; it does not forbid the path through it.** The testimonies run from doubt to certainty: Reeve accepted the critics and "after further consideration" rejected them (.09); Kelly came "out of uncertainty and doubt" (.17); Torrey praises Thomas as "an honest doubter" (vol. 2, line 13759). But the goal is a faith without doubt: "without objection and without a doubt" (Hague .03), "not a shadow of doubt" (Kelly .17). Mauro gives the theory: doubts are "disguises and pretexts supplied by the enemy of souls", and the renewed mind is "purged of its pestilential brood of doubts and reasonings" (.13). Mullins: intellectual difficulties "cease to be relevant" (.15). So "permits neither skepticism nor doubt" is close for the *state* aimed at. But **"ecclesiastical" is wrong.** Authority is not a church. It is the Book, read by the lay believer and confirmed in his own experience. The essays attack Rome precisely for forbidding "the right of private judgment" (.20) and closing the Bible to the laity (.21). Ryle's true Church is "dependent upon no ministers upon earth" (.22). A layman tells liberal clergy to leave the Church (.23).

**Surrender or self-assertion? Both, in Luther's shape.** The believer judges for himself: an unlearned man who yields his "spiritual judgment" to a scholar abdicates "his birthright as a man" (Hague .01). The English reader "is abundantly able to decide upon these questions for himself" (Gray .08). Kelly tests the Bible "as I would use a text-book in any science" (.17). Mullins makes experience the evidence (.15). This is C4.3's Reformation answerability, intact, with the same bounds: Scripture and a "spiritual community" of witnesses (Mullins .14). Then the self is *given over*: Christ becomes "final for his reason, final for his conscience" (Mullins .14); Kelly will "go without question" and look on the "ratiocinations of men as folly if opposed to Him" (.17).

**The projected-Self structure (C9.4): present in form, explicitly reversed in content.** The form C9.4 predicts is there: one source, all-or-nothing, no competing frame. "If we include Bible history at all, we must include the whole of it" (Gray .07); "they all draw their religion from one single Book" (Ryle .22); one who has the truth "is no longer a seeker" (Mauro .12); "Nothing higher can be conceived" (Mullins .14). But the corpus *names the Self as its enemy*. Critics who make reason "his lord" make each man "practically his own god" (Reeve .10). The verbal-inspiration debate is "the race [...] trying to hammer out the deification of itself" (Gray .07). No man can "transform himself" (Mullins .16). C9.4 can only stand as an analogy of form drawn by the essay, not as the movement's self-understanding. And it must face the fact that this "single authority" is held by each believer's own judgment and experience.

**Against the mind-cure self (C5.14, C9.1): yes, directly.** Wilson's attack on Christian Science targets the very doctrine C5.14 tested: sin and sickness "may be banished by a process of thought" (.18). It rejects Eddy's reading of "Believe" as "self-reliant trustworthiness": "Jesus is the One upon whom he must rely!" (.19). Mullins mocks the Christian Science cobble stone (vol. 4, lines 13552–13565). The two are opponents, and in 1910–15 they saw themselves so. Oddly, the fundamentalist accuses the Scientists of the vice C9.3 lays on fundamentalism: they accept "the utterances and decrees of a superior" more "slavishly" than any people (.18).

### fundamentals.01 · Vol. 1 ch. I, Dyson Hague, "The History of the Higher Criticism" — the unlearned believer's "right of private judgment"

- **Claims:** C9.2 (against), C4.3 (for: continuity), C9.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** vol. 1, ch. I, pp. 37–38; `texts/fundamentals-vol1.txt` lines 1636–1641, 1645–1651, 1654–1657
- **Checked:** text file (OCR "asa man" → "as a man")

> The desire to receive all the light that the most fearless search for truth by the highest scholarship can yield is the desire of every true believer in the Bible. No really healthy Christian mind can advocate obscurantism. The obscurant who opposes the investigation of scholarship, and would throttle the investigators, has not the spirit of Christ. [...] But it is the duty of every Christian who belongs to the noble army of truth-lovers to test all things and to hold fast that which is good. He also has rights even though he is, technically speaking, unlearned, and to accept any view that contradicts his spiritual judgment simply because it is that of a so-called scholar, is to abdicate his franchise as a Christian and his birthright as a man. [...] And in his right of private judgment he is aware that while the privilege of investigation is conceded to all, the conclusions of an avowedly prejudiced scholarship must be subjected to a peculiarly searching analysis.

The founding essay rejects obscurantism by name. It grounds resistance to the critics in the lay believer's own judgment, Luther's "right" of the unlearned (cf. luther-nobility.03), now against professors instead of popes. Note the asymmetry: only "prejudiced" scholarship gets the "searching analysis."

> No expert scholarship can settle questions that require a humble heart, a believing mind and a reverent spirit, as well as a knowledge of Hebrew and philology; and no scholarship can be relied upon as expert which is manifestly characterized by a biased judgment, a curious lack of knowledge of human nature, and a still more curious deference to the views of men with a prejudice against the supernatural. Hague turns reason against the critics: he demands proofs and faults Driver's bare assertions. The limit he sets is not on reason but on what scholarship alone can settle, and the target is "rationalists" (naturalistic presupposition), not reasoning.

> The word evolution is in itself innocent enough, and has a large range of legitimate use. The Bible, indeed, teaches a system of evolution. Christianity is presented as an inductive science of fact and evidence, weighed against evolution's evidence. The one limit is on specialists' authority in religion, which divides the fields; it does not abandon reason.

> While the literal bodily resurrection of Jesus Christ is the corner-stone of Christian doctrine, it is also the Gibraltar of Christian evidence, and the Waterloo of infidelity and rationalism. > Hebrew and Greek, and no sensible man will underrate them for the theologian or the Bible scholar, but they are entirely unnecessary to an understanding of the doctrine of inspiration or any other doctrine of Holy Writ. The intelligent reader of the Bible in the English tongue, especially when illuminated by the Holy Spirit, is abundantly able to decide upon these questions for himself. The Dean of Moody Bible Institute puts the decision about inspiration itself in the lay reader's hands, not those of clergy or scholars. This is Reformation answerability made democratic and anti-expert: neither ecclesiastical nor a surrender of judgment.

### fundamentals.12 · Vol. 4 ch. I, Philip Mauro, "Modern Philosophy" — "utterly irreconcilable"; "no longer a seeker"

- **Claims:** C9.2 (for), C9.4 (for), C9.3 (for)
- **Location:** vol. 4, ch. I, pp. 12–13; lines 419–421, 442–446, 457–461
- **Checked:** text file

> In our day it has become a tenet of popular theology that the human reason is the final court of appeal in all matters of doctrine. [...] It follows of necessity that philosophy and divine revelation are utterly irreconcilable. The very existence of philosophy as an occupation for the human mind depends upon the rigid exclusion of every explanation of the universe which is not reached by a speculative process. > all my doubts, questionings, skepticism and criticism concerning God the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, concerning the full inspiration, accuracy and authority of the Holy Scriptures as the incorruptible Word of God, [...] were swept away completely. Every student of the Bible knows that these are the words of Paul to the Philippian jailer, and that he says, "Believe on the Lord Jesus Christ, and thou shalt be saved." The Apostle is enjoining the very opposite of "self-reliant trustworthiness." He is showing to the trembling jailer that Jesus is the One upon whom he must rely!

The plainest clash in the corpus: Eddy turns faith into self-reliance, and the fundamentalist turns it back into reliance on another. On C9.4: what is "projected" is not the Self. It is reliance taken *away* from the Self, and the essay says so.

> They worship differently and after various fashions; some worship with a form of prayer, and some with none; some worship kneeling, and some standing; but they all worship. with one heart. They are all led by one Spirit; they all build upon one foundation; they all draw their religion from one single Book—that is the Bible. They are all joined to one great center—that is Jesus Christ. One source, one centre (C9.4's form), with diversity of practice allowed and no clergy needed. The authority is a book, not an institution. That is why "ecclesiastical" misnames it.

> Moreover, they seem to completely ignore, and to be utterly unable to testify from personal experience to, the regenerating power of the Holy Spirit working upon men's hearts through the Word. Far better would it be for all concerned if these ministers had the courage of their convictions, and sense of honor enough to compel them to leave the Christian Church, taking with them those of their flocks who think like them and wish to follow, for they may be sure that the pretty little amenities of morality and sociology which they have substituted for the Gospel of regeneration can never take its place, or lead a single soul out of the death and darkness of sin into the life and light that are to be found in Christ alone.
