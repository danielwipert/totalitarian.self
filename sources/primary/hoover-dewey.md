## Summary of the reading (2026-10-23)

**Hoover (1922)** states the ideology at its most bounded. His individualism is "tempered" by equality of opportunity and "service" (.01), has "long since abandoned" laissez faire (.02), backs anti-trust regulation against "autocracy of economic power" (.07), and rests on "the divine spark" in each soul (.03). He is no simple spokesman for monopoly, so C6.7's line from Turner does not fit him. But two things in him bear out C6.13. (a) The pioneer is carried on as the image of industry and science ("The days of the pioneer are not over", .08). (b) The vocabulary that passes to business is the vocabulary of the *checks*, not only of the Self: "service", "community responsibility", "mutuality" are given to corporations, stockholders and trade associations (.05, .06). He dates the change himself: consolidation and government regulation began "three decades ago" and economic associations grew "in the last thirty years" (.05, .07), so from about 1890. He already uses "self-expression" for persons, in a producer's sense (.04), which complicates C8.6.

**Dewey (1930)** diagnoses the erosion directly and gives a mechanism. The "loyalties which once held individuals [...] have well-nigh disappeared" (.10). Corporateness has detached individuals "from their old local ties and allegiances" (.12). Religion has lost its force as "a vitally integrative and directive force" while staying respectable (.03, .12). The cause is the "novel combination of the machine and money" (.05), "dominant corporateness" in place of "an earlier pioneer individualism" (.07). Association has not vanished. It has been absorbed: it "more and more" defines individuals' choices (.07), and the "joining" habit is a substitute for lost communion (.15). The old ideal has been perverted "to conform to the practices of a pecuniary culture" (.05) and "shrunk to a pecuniary scale and measure" (.16). "Rugged individualism" is the war cry against regulation, used "in high quarters" (.07, .17). That is, conformity under the banner of individualism (.16), C8.2's thesis with "individualism" as the disguise word.

Dewey gives no year for the start. His markers are relative: the "rapid industrialization" that "took us unawares" (.04), the "almost forgotten" era of trust-busting (.07), the "new economy" of the 1920s (.09). Against the thesis: (i) He thinks the old order "covered up" defects that the present only reveals (.06). (ii) He sees the corporate age as an integration "on a scale never known before", not only a loss (`texts/dewey-individualism-old-and-new.txt` lines 1279–1288). (iii) He does not blame individualism as such; he wants a new one (.19). (iv) He denies that the talk of "service" is a conscious cloak (.11). (v) He himself ends by putting responsibility for being "lost" on the individual (.18). His best single passage for §5 is the footnote on Waldo Frank: the "primacy of the self" is "a reaction of the romantic and subjective age to the dissolution" (.13).

## Passages: Hoover

### hoover.01 · "American Individualism", pp. 8–9 — individualism "tempered"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** opening section, pp. 8–9; `texts/hoover-american-individualism.txt` lines 299–325
- **Checked:** text file

> No doubt, individualism run riot, with no tempering principle, would provide a long category of inequalities, of tyrannies, dominations, and injustices. America, however, has tempered the whole conception of individualism by the injection of a definite principle, and from this principle it follows that attempts at domination, whether in government or in the processes of industry and commerce, are under an insistent curb. If we would have the values of individualism, their stimulation to initiative, to the development of hand and intellect, to the high development of thought and spirituality, they must be tempered with that firm and fixed ideal of American individualism—an equality of opportunity. If we would have these values we must soften its hardness and stimulate progress through that sense of service that lies in our people.

The answer to the brief's first question: the future president's individualism is bounded, by equality of opportunity and "service", and it curbs domination "in the processes of industry and commerce". It is not stated as a licence for business.

### hoover.02 · "American Individualism", pp. 10–11 — laissez faire "abandoned"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** opening section, pp. 10–11; lines 352–390
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "hindmost/'" read as "hindmost.'"

> Individualism cannot be maintained as the foundation of a society if it looks to only legalistic justice based upon contracts, property, and political equality. Such legalistic safeguards are themselves not enough. In our individualism we have long since abandoned the laissez faire of the 18th Century—the notion that it is "every man for himself and the devil take the hindmost." We abandoned that when we adopted the ideal of equality of opportunity—the fair chance of Abraham Lincoln. [...] We have also learned that fair division can only be obtained by certain restrictions on the strong and the dominant. We have indeed gone even further in the 20th Century with the embracement of the necessity of a greater and broader sense of service and responsibility to others as a part of individualism.

"Responsibility" here means responsibility *to others*, the opposite of the later "personal responsibility". Hoover puts the limit inside the concept itself. For C6.13 this is the vocabulary of the checks being taken into the ideology, not yet dropped from it.

### hoover.03 · "Philosophic Grounds", p. 14; "Spiritual Phases", pp. 26–27 — "the divine spark" alone in the individual

- **Claims:** C5.14 (for, step 1), C6.1 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 14, 26–27; lines 449–457, 765–789
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "inteUigence" read as "intelligence"; the page number "26" (OCR "se") inside "pro-portion" dropped

> On the philosophic side we can agree at once that intelligence, character, courage, and the divine spark of the human soul are alone the property of individuals. These do not lie in agreements, in organizations, in institutions, in masses, or in groups. They abide alone in the individual mind and heart.

> Our social and economic system cannot march toward better days unless it is inspired by things of the spirit. It is here that the higher purposes of individualism must find their sustenance. Men do not live by bread alone. Nor is individualism merely a stimulus to production and the road to liberty; it alone admits the universal divine inspiration of every human soul. [...] Spirituality with its faith, its hope, its charity, can be increased by each individual's own effort. And in proportion as each individual increases his own store of spirituality, in that proportion increases the idealism of democracy.

"The spiritual" is real in Hoover and keeps a divine frame. But every value is placed "alone" in the individual, none in institutions, and spirituality grows by "each individual's own effort". This is C5.14's step 1 in a statesman: the self as cause, with the frame still borrowed. It also gives institutions no moral standing of their own, which is the opposite of the voluntary checks of C6.1.

### hoover.04 · pp. 21–22, 37, 42 — "self-expression" for persons, 1922

- **Claims:** C8.6 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** "Philosophic Grounds", pp. 21–22; "Economic Phases", pp. 37, 42; lines 658–672, 1059–1063, 1192–1197
- **Checked:** text file

> If we examine the impulses that carry us forward, none is so potent for progress as the yearning for individual self-expression, the desire for creation of something. Perhaps the greatest human happiness flows from personal achievement. Here lies the great urge of the constructive instinct of mankind. But it can only thrive in a society where the individual has liberty and stimulation to achievement. [...] Our American individualism, indeed, is only in part an economic creed. It aims to provide opportunity for self-expression, not merely economically, but spiritually as well. [...] Nevertheless, in these groups the individual finds an opportunity for self-expression and participation in the moulding of ideas, a field for training and the stepping stones for leadership.

C8.6 dates the idiom's passage to persons "after 1928". But in 1922 Hoover already gives "self-expression" to individuals, as the chief motive of progress and as what trade associations and chambers of commerce offer. It is the *producer's* self-expression: "creation of something", "achievement", leadership. What C8.6 still needs to date is the consumer's use. (The word also appears among inherited instincts, "desire for self-expression, for power, for adulation", line 481.)

### hoover.05 · "Spiritual Phases", pp. 27–28; "Economic Phases", pp. 41–42 — association, deeper than "joining"; economic associations displace church and party

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for, on dating and on association to economic association; complicates, on Hoover's own view), C6.2 (complicates), C8.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 27–28, 41–42; lines 810–827, 1179–1192, 1199–1205
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "spirituaUty" read as "spirituality"

> The vast multiplication of voluntary organizations for altruistic purposes are themselves proof of the ferment of spirituality, service, and mutual responsibility. These associations for advancement of public welfare, improvement, morals, charity, public opinion, health, the clubs and societies for recreation and intellectual advancement, represent something moving at a far greater depth than "joining." They represent the widespread aspiration for mutual advancement, self-expression, and neighborly helpfulness.

> There has been in the last thirty years an extraordinary growth of organizations for advancement of ideas in the community for mutual cooperation and economic objectives—the chambers of commerce, trade associations, labor unions, bankers, farmers, propaganda associations, and what not. These are indeed variable mixtures of altruism and self-interest. [...] The number of leaders in local and national life whose opportunity to service and leadership came through these associations has become now of more importance than those through the direct lines of political and religious organization.

Hoover's own date (1922 minus thirty years, so about 1892) and his own account of the shift: economic associations now matter more than "political and religious organization". That is C6.13's change of association in the words of its defender. For Hoover it is no erosion: the new groups are "a school of public responsibility" (line 1228). Note "joining": Dewey answers this passage in dewey.15.

### hoover.06 · "Economic Phases", pp. 40–41 — corporations given "community responsibility" and "service"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for, with a correction), C9.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 40–41; lines 1131–1143, 1163–1177
- **Checked:** text file

> Thus large capital is steadily becoming more and more a mobilization of the savings of the small holder—the actual people themselves—and its administration becomes at once more sensitive to the moral opinions of the people in order to attract their support. The directors and managers of large concerns, themselves employees of these great groups of individual stockholders, or policy holders, reflect a spirit of community responsibility. [...] the very general desire to yield a measure of service with the goods sold. Another phase of this same development in administration of capital is the growth of a sort of institutional sense in many large business enterprises. The encouragement of solidarity in all grades of their employees in the common service and common success, the sense of mutuality with the prosperity of the community are both vital developments in individualism.

This is the passage that most directly shows a moral vocabulary passing to business. But the words are "community responsibility", "service", "solidarity", "mutuality", the language of the old checks, not of the Self. C6.13 should say both vocabularies passed. Against C9.3: the corporation here is not a singular Self but a "mobilization" of small savers.

### hoover.07 · "Political Phases", pp. 52–54 — consolidation "three decades ago"; regulation against "autocracy of economic power"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (against, as to Hoover; for, as to dating), C6.13 (for, on dating)
- **Location:** pp. 52–54; lines 1469–1494, 1521–1525
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "capitalism''" read as "capitalism"

> The entrance of the Government began strongly three decades ago, when our industrial organization began to move powerfully in the direction of consolidation of enterprise. We found in the course of this development that equality of opportunity and its corollary, individual initiative, was being throttled by the concentration of control of industry and service, and thus an economic domination of groups builded over the nation. At this time, particularly, we were threatened with a form of autocracy of economic power. Our mass of regulation of public utilities and our legislation against restraint of trade is the monument to our intent to preserve an equality of opportunity. This regulation is itself proof that we have gone a long way toward the abandonment of the "capitalism" of Adam Smith. [...] To curb the forces in business which would destroy equality of opportunity and yet to maintain the initiative and creative faculties of our people are the twin objects we must attain.

Hoover dates consolidation to the 1890s, as Turner does. But he turns individualism *against* monopoly: equality of opportunity justifies the anti-trust laws. So the claim that corporate leaders kept the pioneer vocabulary for monopoly is not true of this text. It is true of the use Dewey reports eight years later (dewey.07, .17). Hoover also lists the system's faults plainly (12-hour day, child labour, "some fortunes excessive", lines 1656–1688), though he calls them "steadily more local".

### hoover.08 · "The Future", pp. 63–67 — "The days of the pioneer are not over"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C6.13 (for), C6.8 (complicates)
- **Location:** pp. 63–67; lines 1767–1782, 1792–1800, 1813–1817, 1844–1850
- **Checked:** text file

> The American pioneer is the epic expression of that individualism, and the pioneer spirit is the response to the challenge of opportunity, to the challenge of nature, to the challenge of life, to the call of the frontier. That spirit need never die for lack of something for it to achieve. There will always be a frontier to conquer or to hold as long as men think, plan, and dare. [...] The days of the pioneer are not over. There are continents of human welfare of which we have penetrated only the coastal plain. The great continent of science is as yet explored only on its borders, and it is only the pioneer who will penetrate the frontier in the quest for new worlds to conquer. [...] After the absorption of the great plains of the West came the era of industrial development with the new complex of forces that it has brought us. [...] Salvation will not come to us out of the wreckage of individualism. What we need today is steady devotion to a better, brighter, broader individualism—an individualism that carries increasing responsibility and service to our fellows.

This is C6.7's ideal outliving its conditions, said outright: the frontier closes, and the pioneer is carried over into "industrial development" and science. Hoover still binds it to "responsibility and service to our fellows". The phrase "rugged individualism" does not occur in this book (checked by search); Dewey's "in high quarters" (dewey.07) points to Hoover's later use of it.

## Passages: Dewey

### dewey.01 · ch. I "The House Divided Against Itself", pp. 9–10 — "our rugged—or is it ragged?—individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I, pp. 9–10; `texts/dewey-individualism-old-and-new.txt` lines 81–103
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "arc" read as "are"

> Anthropologically speaking, we are living in a money culture. Its cult and rites dominate. "The money medium of exchange and the cluster of activities associated with its acquisition drastically condition the other activities of the people." This, of course, is as it should be; people have to make a living, do they not? [...] So far, all is for the best in the best of all possible cultures: our rugged—or is it ragged?—individualism.

The opening of the book. The inner quotation is from the Lynds' *Middletown*: Dewey builds his ch. I on it (verify in `lynd-middletown.md`). "Money culture" is the name he gives the order that replaced the old one; "rugged individualism" is its name for itself.

### dewey.02 · ch. I, pp. 13–14 — "the loudest and most frequently professed philosophy"

- **Claims:** C8.7 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I, pp. 13–14; lines 199–207, 219–221
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "lias" read as "has"

> Instead of materialism, our idealism is probably the loudest and most frequently professed philosophy the world has ever heard. We praise even our most successful men, not for their ruthless and self-centered energy in getting ahead, but because of their love of flowers, children, and dogs, or their kindness to aged relatives. Anyone who frankly urges a selfish creed of life is everywhere frowned upon. [...] We are surcharged with altruism and bursting with desire to "serve" others.

The professed creed is not the Self at all but "idealism", "altruism", "serve". In 1930 the value word dressing the money culture is service and idealism. This bears on C8.7's list, and warns against assuming the disguise word was already the Self.

### dewey.03 · ch. I, pp. 14–15 — religion "so thoroughly respectable [...] and so nearly totally disconnected from life"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.1 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I, pp. 14–15; lines 231–249
- **Checked:** text file

> It may all be summed up, perhaps, by saying that nowhere in the world at any time has religion been so thoroughly respectable as with us, and so nearly totally disconnected from life. [...] The glorification of religion as setting the final seal of approval on pecuniary success, and supplying the active motive to more energetic struggle for such success, and the adoption by the churches of the latest devices of the movies and the advertiser, approach too close to the obscene.

One of Tocqueville's checks, religion, has not disappeared. It has been turned into a support of "pecuniary success" and taken over the methods of advertising. Erosion by capture, not by abandonment.

### dewey.04 · ch. I, p. 16 — "the more we depart from them in fact, the more loudly we proclaim them"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C6.8 (for, on dating), C8.2 (for)
- **Location:** ch. I, p. 16; lines 293–303
- **Checked:** text file

> It is evident enough that the rapid industrialization of our civilization took us unawares. Being mentally and morally unprepared, our older creeds have become ingrowing; the more we depart from them in fact, the more loudly we proclaim them. In effect we treat them as magic formulae. By repeating them often enough we hope to ward off the evils of the new situation, or at least to prevent ourselves from seeing them—and this latter function is ably performed by our nominal beliefs.

Dewey's mechanism in one sentence: the creed outlives its conditions, hardens, and works to stop people seeing the new situation. That is C6.8's "dogmatic" turn, and it is already here in 1930. It is also close to C8.2's claim that the vocabulary can no longer see what it covers.

### dewey.05 · ch. I, pp. 17–18 — "a perversion of the whole ideal of individualism"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.7 (for), C6.1 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. I, pp. 17–18; lines 317–326, 342–361
- **Checked:** text file

> Our tradition, our heritage, is itself double. It contains in itself the ideal of equality of opportunity and of freedom for all, without regard to birth and status, as a condition for the effective realization of that equality. This ideal and endeavor in its behalf once constituted our essential Americanism; that which was prized as the note of a new world. It is the genuinely spiritual element of our tradition. [...] Meanwhile our institutions embody another and older tradition. Industry and business conducted for money profit are nothing new [...] But the invention of the machine has given them a power and scope they never had in the past from which they derive. Our law and politics and the incidents of human association depend upon a novel combination of the machine and money, and the result is the pecuniary culture characteristic of our civilization. The spiritual factor of our tradition, equal opportunity and free association and intercommunication, is obscured and crowded out. Instead of the development of individualities which it prophetically set forth, there is a perversion of the whole ideal of individualism to conform to the practices of a pecuniary culture. It has become the source and justification of inequalities and oppressions.

The central passage for C6.13. The old ideal included "free association" as its "spiritual factor". Machine and money crowded it out, and the vocabulary of individualism was bent to serve "a pecuniary culture". It complicates C6.1: Dewey's America had two traditions from the start, one of them business for profit, which is "older" than the democratic ideal.

### dewey.06 · ch. II "'America'—By Formula", pp. 26–28 — what "the older type of culture covered up"

- **Claims:** C6.1 (complicates), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. II, pp. 26–28; lines 603–606, 633–639, 670–676
- **Checked:** text file

> I rather wish to raise the question as to how many of the defects and evils that are supposed to belong to the present order are in fact projections into it of a departing past order. [...] I wonder very much whether many of the things that are objected to in the present scene—and justly so—are not in fact revelations of what the older type of culture covered up, and whether their perceptible presence is not to be credited rather than debited to the forces that are now active. [...] The prized and vaunted "individuality" of European culture that is threatened by the leveling standardization and uniformity of the American type was a very limited affair. The strongest "against" in Dewey. The erosion story assumes the old order was sound. Dewey says much of what looks like decay is the old order's defects coming into view. He says it of Europe, but the same chapter holds that "the American type" is as much a survival of the old European tradition as a new thing (lines 772–776).

### dewey.07 · ch. III "The United States, Incorporated", pp. 35–37 — "from an earlier pioneer individualism to a condition of dominant corporateness"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C6.13 (for), C8.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 35–37; lines 861–877, 893–902, 907–917
- **Checked:** text file; last two sentences read by eye (a running head splits them, so check_quotes scores 0.84)

> It was not long ago that it was fashionable for both American and foreign observers of our national scene to sum up the phenomena of our social life under the title of "individualism." [...] Individualism is still carried on our banners and attempts are made to use it as a war cry, especially when it is desired to defeat governmental regulation of any form of industry previously exempt from legal control. Even in high quarters, rugged individualism is praised as the glory of American life. But such words have little relation to the moving facts of that life. [...] We may then say that the United States has steadily moved from an earlier pioneer individualism to a condition of dominant corporateness. The influence business corporations exercise in determining present industrial and economic activities is both a cause and a symbol of the tendency to combination in all phases of life. Associations tightly or loosely organized more and more define the opportunities, the choices and the actions of individuals. [...] The era of trust-busting is an almost forgotten age. Not only are big mergers the order of the day, but popular sentiment now looks upon them with pride rather than with fear.

The direct witness for C6.7 as revised: by 1930 the individualist word is "a war cry" against regulation, praised "in high quarters" (the president). Compare hoover.07, where in 1922 the same individualism justified regulation. The vocabulary has changed sides within eight years. Note that association is not gone. It has become corporate.

### dewey.08 · ch. III, pp. 41–43 — "the business mind"; "the publicity agent"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.6 (complicates), C9.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 41–43; lines 1049–1067, 1094–1112
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "Kdisons", "Kotarians", "Ki- wanians" read as "Edisons", "Rotarians", "Kiwanians"

> Attention has recently been called to a new phenomenon in human culture:—the business mind, having its own conversation and language, its own interests, its own intimate groupings in which men of this mind, in their collective capacity, determine the tone of society at large as well as the government of industrial society, and have more political influence than the government itself. [...] The fact significant for present discussion is that we now have, although without formal or legal status, a mental and moral corporateness for which history affords no parallel. Our indigenous heroes are the Fords and Edisons who typify this mind to the public. Critics may find amusement in ridiculing Rotarians, Kiwanians and Lions, but the latter can well afford to disregard the ridicule because they are representatives of the dominant corporate mentality. [...] We live exposed to the greatest flood of mass suggestion that any people has ever experienced. The need for united action, and the supposed need of integrated opinion and sentiment, are met by organized propaganda and advertising. The publicity agent is perhaps the most significant symbol of our present social life. There are individuals who resist; but, for a time at least, sentiment can be manufactured by mass methods for almost any person or any cause.

C6.13's "club" and "mass media" exactly: the service clubs as the "corporate mentality", and "organized propaganda and advertising" filling the place of shared opinion. Dewey calls this a mind with "its own conversation and language" but does not say its language is the Self's. Against C9.3: what is "corporate" here is a shared mentality, not an organization behaving as a single Self.

### dewey.09 · ch. III, pp. 43–44 — thrift to buying as "duty"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C8.6 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. III, pp. 43–44; lines 1125–1144, 1158–1163
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "o\'", "oi%" read as "of"; "buying," at the page break read as "buying."

> What has become of the old-fashioned ideal of thrift? Societies for the promotion of savings among the young were much hurt in their feelings when Henry Ford urged a free scale of expenditures instead of a close scale of personal savings. But his recommendation was in line with all the economic tendencies of the day. Speeded-up mass production demands increased buying. It is promoted by advertising on a vast scale, by instalment selling, by agents skilled in breaking down sales resistance. Hence buying becomes an economic "duty" which is as consonant with the present epoch as thrift was with the period of individualism. [...] In effect, the individual is told that by indulging in the enjoyment of free purchasing he performs his economic duty, transferring his surplus income to the corporate store where it can be most effectively used. Virtue departs from mere thrift.

A moral word passing to the market, dated to the 1920s (Ford). But the consumer's word in 1930 is "duty", not self-expression. This supports C8.6's late date for the consumer's self-expression: in Dewey consumption is still a duty to industry.

### dewey.10 · ch. IV "The Lost Individual", pp. 52–53 — the loyalties "have well-nigh disappeared"

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.1 (for), C7.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IV, pp. 52–53; lines 1378–1393, 1396–1399, 1411–1416
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "as- lacking" read as "as lacking", "cany" as "carry"

> What is here meant by "the lost individual" is, however, so irrelevant to this question that it is not necessary to decide between the two views. For by it is meant a moral and intellectual fact which is independent of any manifestation of power in action. The significant thing is that the loyalties which once held individuals, which gave them support, direction, and unity of outlook on life, have well-nigh disappeared. In consequence, individuals are confused and bewildered. It would be difficult to find in history an epoch as lacking in solid and assured objects of belief and approved ends of action as is the present. Stability of individuality is dependent upon stable objects to which allegiance firmly attaches itself. [...] For the others, traditional objects of loyalty have become hollow or are openly repudiated, and they drift without sure anchorage. [...] Assured and integrated individuality is the product of definite social relationships and publicly acknowledged functions. Judged by this standard, even those who seem to be in control, and to carry the expression of their special individual abilities to a high pitch, are submerged.

Wording verified: Dewey's phrase is "the lost individual", in his own quotation marks. The brief's paraphrase (an individual cut loose from old loyalties) is not his wording. The nearest wording is "loosened from the ties that once gave order and support to their lives" (lines 1938–1940). He names the erosion directly, but for C7.2 the cause of the drift is corporateness, not self-reliance in surfeit.

### dewey.11 · ch. IV, pp. 56–57 — "service": not "a cloak", but "the lady doth protest too much"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (complicates), C8.7 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IV, pp. 56–57; lines 1521–1538
- **Checked:** text file

> I should explain a seeming hypocrisy on the same ground. We are not consciously insincere in our professions of devotion to ideals of "service"; they mean something. Neither the Rotarian nor the big business enterprise uses the term merely as a cloak for "putting something over" which makes for pecuniary gain. But the lady doth protest too much. The wide currency of such professions testifies to a sense of a social function of business which is expressed in words because it is so lacking in fact, and yet which is felt to be rightfully there.

An important qualification for §8. Dewey denies the value word is a conscious disguise. It is sincere, and it is loud *because* the thing is missing. That is a different mechanism from C8.2's disguise: the word compensates for an absence rather than hiding a practice. (Answers Hoover's "service with the goods sold", hoover.06.)

### dewey.12 · ch. IV, pp. 59, 61–63, 69 — local ties, religion and the household

- **Claims:** C6.13 (for), C6.1 (complicates), C6.4 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IV, pp. 59, 61, 62–63, 69; lines 1606–1609, 1665–1670, 1704–1710, 1727–1732, 1922–1927
- **Checked:** text file

> An economic individualism of motives and aims underlies our present corporate mechanisms, and undoes the individual. [...] I do not think it is fantastic to connect our excited and rapacious nationalism with the situation in which corporateness has gone so far as to detach individuals from their old local ties and allegiances but not far enough to give them a new center and order of life. [...] It is probably easy to exaggerate the extent of the decadence of religion in an outward sense, church membership, church-going and so on. But it is hardly possible to overstate its decline as a vitally integrative and directive force in men's thought and sentiments. [...] Religion does not now effect this result. The divorce of church and state has been followed by that of religion and society. Wherever religion has not become a merely private indulgence, it has become at best a matter of sects and denominations [...] It was not deliberate human intention that undermined the traditional household as the center of industry and education and as the focus of moral training; that sapped the older institution of enduring marriage.

Dewey's mechanism for the erosion: corporateness detaches people from "old local ties" (the township's world), religion becomes "a merely private indulgence", the household loses its functions. It complicates C6.1's "because these checks bound by consent [...] they could be left": in Dewey nobody chose to leave them ("not deliberate human intention"). They were dissolved by industrial structure.

### dewey.13 · ch. IV, pp. 63–64 and note — "the primacy of the self" as "a reaction [...] to the dissolution"

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates; was "for, step 2" before Frank was checked), C5.3 (complicates), C6.13 (for)
- **Location:** ch. IV, pp. 63–65 and footnote p. 64; lines 1744–1746, 1757–1767, 1778–1785, 1795–1799
- **Checked:** text file. The words Dewey quotes are Waldo Frank's (*The Re-discovery of America*, 1929), at second hand; checked against Frank on 2026-10-24: the words match (Dewey drops "I said"), but see simmel-frank.02.

> They wish to reverse the operation and to form the social bond of unity and of allegiance by regeneration of the isolated individual soul. [...] The very attempt to secure integration for the individual, and through him for society, by means of a deliberate and conscious cultivation of religion, is itself proof of how far the individual has become lost through detachment from acknowledged social values. It is no wonder that when the appeal does not take the form of dogmatic fundamentalism, it tends to terminate in either some form of esoteric occultism or private estheticism. [Dewey's note on Frank:] he goes on to say "man's need of order and his making of order are his science, his art, his religion; and these are all to be referred to the initial sense of order called the self," quite oblivious of the fact that this doctrine of the primacy of the self is precisely a reaction of the romantic and subjective age to the dissolution he has depicted, having its meaning only in that dissolution. [...] For the idea that the outward scene is chaotic because of the machine, which is a principle of chaos, and that it will remain so until individuals reinstitute wholeness within themselves, simply reverses the true state of things.

**Corrected 2026-10-24:** Dewey's reading of Frank does not hold. Two sentences after the line Dewey quotes, Frank corrects himself: the self is "a relative focus in the unity of all men and all being" (simmel-frank.02), bounded by the Whole. So this is not evidence for C5.14's step 2. What stands is Dewey's own analysis: he explains the turn to the individual soul as a product of the "dissolution" of loyalties. That is the thesis's own causal order (frame loss leads to the self as origin), stated in 1930, with "dogmatic fundamentalism" as the other response (compare §9.1).

### dewey.14 · ch. V "Toward a New Individualism", pp. 77, 80, 92 — "a dying echo"; "Individuality was a reality because it corresponded to conditions"

- **Claims:** C6.7 (for), C6.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V, pp. 77, 80, 92; lines 2173–2176, 2248–2259, 2639–2643
- **Checked:** text file

> In America, the older individualism assumed a romantic form. It was hardly necessary to elaborate a theory which equated personal gain with social advance. [...] The shift that makes the older individualism a dying echo is more marked as well as more rapid in this country. Where is the wilderness which now beckons creative energy and affords untold opportunity to initiative and vigor? Where is the pioneer who goes forth rejoicing, even in the midst of privation, to its conquest? The wilderness exists in the movie and the novel; and the children of the pioneers, who live in the midst of surroundings artificially made over by the machine, enjoy pioneer life idly in the vicarious film. [...] The wilderness was a reality and it had to be subdued. The type of character that evolved was strong and hardy, often picturesque, and sometimes heroic. Individuality was a reality because it corresponded to conditions.

For C6.7: the ideal survives as an image ("the vicarious film") after its conditions. For C6.3: Dewey, unlike Turner, grants the frontier's individuality was real and fitted its conditions. That sits close to the SPEC's original "survival requirement", but he gives no support for a lone self: the initiative, enterprise and vigour he names went into work that "furthered the national life".

### dewey.15 · ch. V, pp. 83–84, 87–88 — conformity and "joining" fill a "vacuum"

- **Claims:** C8.5 (for), C8.4 (for), C8.3 (for), C8.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V, pp. 83–84, 87–88; lines 2352–2364, 2487–2507
- **Checked:** text file; OCR "censensus" read as "consensus", "90 averse bo" as "so averse to", "huilt" as "built"

> [...] is suggested by the great pressure now brought to bear to effect conformity and standardization of American opinion. Why should regimentation, the erection of an average struck from the opinions of large masses into regulative norms, and in general the domination of quantity over quality, be so characteristic of present American life? I see but one fundamental explanation. The individual cannot remain intellectually a vacuum. If his ideas and beliefs are not the spontaneous function of a communal life in which he shares, a seeming consensus will be secured as a substitute by artificial and mechanical means. [...] The "joining" habit of the average American, and his excessive sociability, may well have an explanation like that of conformity. They, too, testify to nature's abhorrence of that vacuum which the passing of the older individualism has produced. We should not be so averse to solitude if we had, when we were alone, the companionship of communal thought built into our mental habits. In the absence of this communion, there is the need for reinforcement by external contact. Our sociability is largely an effort to find substitutes for that normal consciousness of connection and union that proceeds from being a sustained and sustaining member of a social whole.

Close to §8: belonging pursued through conformity because the communal life that gave it is gone (C8.5's structure), and sociability as "substitutes" (C8.3's False Groups). But in Dewey the vacuum is left by "the passing of the older individualism", and the conformity is not yet spoken as self-expression. Reply to Hoover's "deeper than 'joining'" (hoover.05).

### dewey.16 · ch. V, pp. 90–91 — "the irony of the gospel of 'individualism' in business conjoined with suppression of individuality"

- **Claims:** C8.2 (for), C8.7 (for), C6.13 (for), C6.7 (for)
- **Location:** ch. V, pp. 90–91; lines 2569–2575, 2583–2605
- **Checked:** text file

> Why, once more, is there such zeal for standardized likeness? It is not, I imagine, because conformity for its own sake appears to be a great boon. It is rather because a certain kind of conformity gives defense and protection to the pecuniary features of our present regime. [...] It is not too much to say that the whole significance of the older individualism has now shrunk to a pecuniary scale and measure. The virtues that are supposed to attend rugged individualism may be vocally proclaimed, but it takes no great insight to see that what is cherished is measured by its connection with those activities that make for success in business conducted for personal gain. Hence, the irony of the gospel of "individualism" in business conjoined with suppression of individuality in thought and speech. One cannot imagine a bitterer comment on any professed individualism than that it subordinates the only creative individuality—that of mind—to the maintenance of a regime which gives the few an opportunity for being shrewd in the management of monetary business.

The answer to the brief's C8 question: yes. In 1930 Dewey describes conformity under the banner of individualism, enforced to protect the pecuniary order. It is C8.2's mechanism with "rugged individualism" as the disguise word. It should be added to C8.7's list. The motive he gives is defence of the regime, not belonging.

> For anyone to call attention to this discrepancy is considered an aspersion on our rugged individualism and an attempt to stir up class feeling. [...] We already have the Interstate Commerce Commission, the Federal Reserve Board, and now the Farm Relief Board—a socialistic undertaking on a large scale sponsored by the party of individualism. [...] On the other hand, the older individualism is still sufficiently ingrained to obtain allegiance in confused sentiment and in vocal utterance. ### dewey.18 · ch. IV, p. 56; ch. VIII "Individuality in Our Day", pp. 166–167 — causes social, responsibility individual

- **Claims:** C7.4 (complicates), C5.14 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. IV, p. 56, lines 1501–1513; ch. VIII, pp. 166–167, lines 4905–4914, 4958–4961
- **Checked:** text file

> The unrest, impatience, irritation and hurry that are so marked in American life are inevitable accompaniments of a situation in which individuals do not find support and contentment in the fact that they are sustaining and sustained members of a social whole. They are evidence, psychologically, of abnormality, and it is as idle to seek for their explanation within the deliberate intent of individuals as it is futile to think that they can be got rid of by hortatory moral appeal.

> All of these considerations would lead to the conclusion that depression of the individual is the individual's own liability, were it not for the time it takes for a new principle to make its way deeply into individual mind on a large scale. But as time goes on, the responsibility becomes an individual one. For individuality is inexpugnable and it is of its nature to assert itself. The first move in recovery of an integrated individual is accordingly with the individual himself. [...] If, in the long run, an individual remains lost, it is because he has chosen irresponsibility; and if he remains wholly depressed, it is because he has chosen the course of easy parasitism.

Against C7.4 (failure rarely put down to social factors): the leading philosopher of 1930 puts the malaise down to social conditions, not individual intent. But he ends by making staying "lost" the individual's choice. Even the critic of the old individualism falls back on individual responsibility in the end, though only "in the long run" and after the conditions are seen.

### dewey.19 · ch. V, p. 82; ch. VII "The Crisis in Culture", p. 143; ch. VIII, pp. 168, 171 — the new individualism, bounded

- **Claims:** C5.14 (complicates), C5.6 (against, for Dewey), C6.13 (complicates)
- **Location:** ch. V, p. 82, lines 2309–2313; ch. VII, p. 143, lines 4200–4202; ch. VIII, pp. 168, 171, lines 4986–4990, 5075–5081
- **Checked:** text file; first sentence read by eye (a running head splits it, so check_quotes scores 0.85)

> Individuals who are not bound together in associations, whether domestic, economic, religious, political, artistic or educational, are monstrosities. [...] Originality and uniqueness are not opposed to social nurture; they are saved by it from eccentricity and escape. [...] It is not something complete in itself, like a closet in a house or a secret drawer in a desk, filled with treasures that are waiting to be bestowed on the world. [...] Our garden is the world, in the angle at which it touches our own manner of being. By accepting the corporate and industrial world in which we live, and by thus fulfilling the precondition for interaction with it, we, who are also parts of the moving present, create ourselves as we create an unknown future.

How Dewey's "new individualism" is bounded: by association, by "social nurture", by interaction with conditions, and by science as a shared, communal method (lines 4544–4550). Individuality is not a prior inner treasure, which is the opposite of C5.6's Self "prior to" experience. He does say "create ourselves", but only through accepting the world. Self-creation is a phrase here, not self-origination.
