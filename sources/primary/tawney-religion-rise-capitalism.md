# R. H. Tawney, *Religion and the Rise of Capitalism* (1926)

- **Edition used:** original English (New York: Harcourt, Brace, 1926; Holland Memorial Lectures, 1922)
- **Text file:** `texts/tawney-religion-rise-capitalism.txt` (clean; Project Gutenberg #71223)
- **Kind of source:** secondary. Tawney is an economic historian. Where he quotes Luther, Calvin, Baxter, Bradford and others he is *reporting* primary sources (check them against our own primary files before relying on them); everything else is his interpretation.
- **Citation form:** chapter and section (e.g. II.ii "Luther"), with this edition's line numbers. Page numbers of the 1926 printing are not in the text file.
- **Quotation conventions:** footnote markers such as [II-50] are omitted; italics marked with underscores in the file are kept.
- **Claims to test:** C4.2, C4.3, C4.4, C4.5, C6.4, C6.5; also C1.1, C4.30, C4.31, C7.4

## Passages

### tawney.01 · Ch. I (opening) — the synthesis breaks into "separate and parallel compartments"

- **Claims:** C4.31 (for), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** Ch. I, before section i; `texts/tawney-religion-rise-capitalism.txt` lines 390–400
- **Checked:** text file

> Its essence is the secularization of social and economic philosophy. The synthesis is resolved into its elements--politics, business, and spiritual exercises; each assumes a separate and independent vitality and obeys the laws of its own being. The social functions matured within the Church, and long identified with it, are transferred to the State, which in turn is idolized as the dispenser of prosperity and the guardian of civilization. The theory of a hierarchy of values, embracing all human interests and activities in a system of which the apex is religion, is replaced by the conception of separate and parallel compartments, between which a due balance should be maintained, but which have no vital connection with each other.

Tawney's frame is our "institutions separate" pattern applied to *spheres of life*: business, politics and religion come apart, and nothing here says the Self leaves God. **Complicates** C4.30: in his account religion does not just lose its coercive institutions; it shrinks to "a department of life" (line 334), a deeper separation than Locke's voluntary church.

### tawney.02 · I.i "The Social Organism" — the medieval order of stations

- **Claims:** C4.2 (for, as background)
- **Location:** I.i; lines 799–802 and 1058–1060
- **Checked:** text file

> Society, like the human body, is an organism composed of different members. Each member has its own function, prayer, or defense, or merchandise, or tilling the soil. Each must receive the means suited to its station, and must claim no more. [...] It is right for a man to seek such wealth as is necessary for a livelihood in his station. To seek more is not enterprise, but avarice, and avarice is a deadly sin.

Tawney's summary of the scholastic doctrine: the "old social order" of C4.2 assigned each person a function and capped their gain at their station. This is what a self that directs its own economic future would have to break.

### tawney.03 · I.i — suspicion of economic motives lasts "till Calvinism"

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against, as regards Calvin)
- **Location:** I.i; lines 1121–1124
- **Checked:** text file

> The suspicion of economic motives had been one of the earliest elements in the social teaching of the Church, and was to survive till Calvinism endowed the life of economic enterprise with a new sanctification.

Early warning that Tawney does not put Calvin with Luther. For him Calvinism is the *end* of the Church's suspicion of commerce, not one more instance of it. See .09.

### tawney.04 · Ch. II (opening) — the principal causes "lay in another region"

- **Claims:** C4.5 (for), C4.2 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. II, before section i; lines 1937–1938 and 1946–1952
- **Checked:** text file

> The strands in this movement were complex, and the formula which associates the Reformation with the rise of economic individualism is no complete explanation. [...] But it is not to under-estimate the effect of the Reformation to say that the principal causes making the age a watershed, from which new streams of social theory descend, lay in another region. Mankind does not reflect upon questions of economic and social organization until compelled to do so by the sharp pressure of some practical emergency. The sixteenth century was an age of social speculation for the same reason as the early nineteenth--because it was an age of social dislocation.

Tawney's own thesis statement for the Reformation chapter, and it is close to C4.5: the economic upheaval comes first, and the new thinking is a response to "social dislocation." The section that follows (II.i, lines 1961–2322) describes that upheaval: the Discoveries, Antwerp, the international money-market.

### tawney.05 · II.ii "Luther" — "without design, and against the intention of most reformers"

- **Claims:** C4.2 (for), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** II.ii; lines 2457–2471 and 2477–2480
- **Checked:** text file

> If it is true that the Reformation released forces which were to act as a solvent of the traditional attitude of religious thought to social and economic issues, it did so without design, and against the intention of most reformers. [...] To think of the abdication of religion from its theoretical primacy over economic activity and social institutions as synchronizing with the revolt from Rome, is to antedate a movement which was not finally accomplished for another century and a half, and which owed as much to changes in economic and political organization, as it did to developments in the sphere of religious thought. [...] If anything, indeed, their tendency was to interpret them with a more rigorous severity, as a protest against the moral laxity of the Renaissance, and, in particular, against the avarice which was thought to be peculiarly the sin of Rome.

This is the strongest support in the book for C4.2's "partly *against* the intentions" and C4.5's "unintended contributors." Note the weighting, though: "as much to" economic change "as it did to" religious thought, which is two causes of equal weight. Tawney does not let economics do all the work.

### tawney.06 · II.ii — Luther "hated commerce and capitalism" and kept the hierarchy

- **Claims:** C4.2 (for, as regards Luther), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** II.ii; lines 2599–2601, 2619–2621, 2665–2667, 2690–2691
- **Checked:** text file

> Of that revolutionary conservatism Luther, who hated the economic individualism of the age not less than its spiritual laxity, is the supreme example. [...] Luther, who has earned eulogy and denunciation as the grand individualist, would have been horrified, could he have anticipated the remoter deductions to be derived from his argument. [...] The social theory of Luther, who hated commerce and capitalism, has its nearest modern analogy in the Distributive State of Mr. Belloc and Mr. Chesterton. [...] Luther accepted the social hierarchy, with its principles of status and subordination, though he knocked away the ecclesiastical rungs in the ladder.

This confirms our primary findings (luther-nobility.02, .09) on both counts: anti-commercial, and station kept. Tawney backs it with Luther's own words on the peasants' demand: "An earthly kingdom cannot exist without inequality of persons. Some must be free, others serfs" (lines 2714–2716, Luther as reported by Tawney).

### tawney.07 · II.ii — *solus cum solo*: the soul isolated, all men on one footing before God

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates), C1.1 (for)
- **Location:** II.ii; lines 2793–2798 and 2832–2838
- **Checked:** text file

> God speaks to the soul, not through the mediation of the priesthood or of social institutions built up by man, but _solus cum solo_, as a voice in the heart and in the heart alone. Thus the bridges between the worlds of spirit and of sense are broken, and the soul is isolated from the society of men, that it may enter into communion with its Maker. [...] In one sense, the distinction between the secular and the religious life vanished. Monasticism was, so to speak, secularized; all men stood henceforward on the same footing towards God; and that advance, which contained the germ of all subsequent revolutions, was so enormous that all else seems insignificant. In another sense, the distinction became more profound than ever before. For, though all might be sanctified, it was their inner life alone which could partake of sanctification.

**For** C1.1: each person answerable on their own, with no estate between them and God. **Complicates** C4.3: Tawney reads Luther as *isolating* the soul from all social mediation, which is stronger than our primary reading that mediation moved into the word and the congregation (luther-nobility.01). This is interpretation; our own Luther files should decide, not Tawney.

### tawney.08 · II.ii — the logic of the religious premises did the work "in spite of" Luther

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates), C4.30 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.ii; lines 2910–2916 and 2922–2923
- **Checked:** text file

> The logic of Luther’s religious premises was more potent for posterity than his attachment to the social ethics of the past, and evolved its own inexorable conclusions in spite of them. It enormously deepened spiritual experience, and sowed the seeds from which new freedoms, abhorrent to Luther, were to spring. But it riveted on the social thought of Protestantism a dualism which, as its implications were developed, emptied religion of its social content, and society of its soul. [...] He himself was conscious that he had left the world of secular activities perilously divorced from spiritual restraints.

This is an "against" for any purely economic reading. Here the unintended effect comes from the *theology* (inner faith against outer works), not from capital. So "unintended contributor" holds, but the contribution is real and doctrinal. It also dates the religion/society split to Luther, well before the Enlightenment (C4.30).

### tawney.09 · II.iii "Calvin" — Calvin accepted commerce and applauded "the economic virtues"

- **Claims:** C4.2 (against, as regards Calvin), C4.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.ii–iii; lines 2725–2727, 3019–3022, 3034–3040, 3081–3083
- **Checked:** text file

> Calvin, with all his rigor, accepted the main institutions of a commercial civilization, and supplied a creed to the classes which were to dominate the future. [...] “What reason is there,” wrote Calvin to a correspondent, “why the income from business should not be larger than that from land-owning? Whence do the merchant’s profits come, except from his own diligence and industry?” [...] But it no longer suspects the whole world of economic motives as alien to the life of the spirit, or distrusts the capitalist as one who has necessarily grown rich on the misfortunes of his neighbor, or regards poverty as in itself meritorious, and it is perhaps the first systematic body of religious teaching which can be said to recognize and applaud the economic virtues. Its enemy is not the accumulation of riches, but their misuse for purposes of self-indulgence or ostentation. [...] The picture of Calvin, the organizer and disciplinarian, as the parent of laxity in social ethics, is a legend.

**Against** C4.2's "Luther and Calvin, who [...] condemned commerce": Tawney sets Calvin *apart* from Luther on exactly this point. The quotation is from Calvin's letter on usury (reported, not in our texts), and it sits awkwardly beside calvin-institutes.11, where Calvin says not to credit wealth to one's own industry. That tension should be checked in the letter itself. What Tawney says Calvin condemned is laxity and the misuse of wealth, not commerce.

### tawney.10 · II.iii — works "indispensable as a proof"; individualism *or* collectivism

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.iii; lines 3136–3137, 3243–3244, 3262–3266
- **Checked:** text file

> Good works are not a way of attaining salvation, but they are indispensable as a proof that salvation has been attained. [...] Both an intense individualism and a rigorous Christian Socialism could be deduced from Calvin’s doctrine. [...] the social ethics of the heroic age of Calvinism savored more of a collectivist dictatorship than of individualism. The expression of a revolt against the medieval ecclesiastical system, it stood itself, where circumstances favored it, for a discipline far more stringent and comprehensive than that of the Middle Ages.

Tawney's "indispensable as a proof" is stronger than what we found in Calvin's own text (calvin-institutes.01–.04: a secondary sign, not a ground of assurance), so on that point he is closer to Weber than to the *Institutes*. The second half helps C4.4's "later Calvinists" wording: in Calvin's Geneva the result was collective discipline (the Consistory, lines 3413–3433), not the self-directing individual.

### tawney.11 · II.iii and IV.ii — New England: the strictest discipline, then undermined

- **Claims:** C6.4 (for), C6.5 (for), C6.5 (complicates)
- **Location:** II.iii, lines 3641–3645 and 3655–3657; IV.ii, lines 6610–6614
- **Checked:** text file

> Of English-speaking communities, that in which the social discipline of the Calvinist Church-State was carried to the furthest extreme was the Puritan theocracy of New England. Its practice had more affinity with the iron rule of Calvin’s Geneva than with the individualistic tendencies of contemporary English Puritanism. [...] Governor Bradford, observing uneasily how men grew “in their outward estates,” remarked that the increase in material prosperity “will be the ruin of New England, at least of the Churches of God there.” [...] In America, the theocracy of Massachusetts, merciless alike to religious liberty and to economic license, was about to be undermined by the rise of new States like Rhode Island and Pennsylvania, whose tolerant, individualist and utilitarian temper was destined to find its greatest representative in the golden common sense of Benjamin Franklin.

**For** C6.4 (communal, covenantal) and for C6.5's claim that early Puritan New England was not commercial: its authorities regulated prices and profits, and the Boston church tried the shopkeeper Robert Keane for overcharging (lines 3664–3754, reported from Winthrop's journal). **Complicates** C6.5: Tawney puts the turn to individualism *outside* New England (Rhode Island, Pennsylvania, Franklin), which matches what Crèvecoeur says of the middle colonies. Bradford's words are quoted at second hand.

### tawney.12 · Ch. III and III.i — the merchant carves "his way to solitary preëminence"

- **Claims:** C4.2 (for), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. III, before section i, lines 3866–3877; III.i, lines 4135–4139
- **Checked:** text file

> It was a society in rapid motion, swayed by new ambitions and haunted by new terrors, in which both success and failure had changed their meaning. [...] The prosperous merchant, once content to win a position of dignity and power in fraternity or town, now flung himself into the task of carving his way to solitary preëminence, unaided by the artificial protection of gild or city. To the immemorial poverty of peasant and craftsman, pitting, under the ever-present threat of famine, their pigmy forces against an implacable nature, was added the haunting insecurity of a growing, though still small, proletariat, detached from their narrow niche in village or borough, the sport of social forces which they could neither understand, nor arrest, nor control. [...] That creed was that the individual is absolute master of his own, and, within the limits set by positive law, may exploit it with a single eye to his pecuniary advantage, unrestrained by any obligation to postpone his own profit to the well-being of his neighbors, or to give account of his actions to a higher authority.

This is the economic origin in its plainest form. Tudor England's commercial and agrarian changes produced the self-directing individual (the merchant, the enclosing landlord's "creed") *before* and apart from any Puritan theology. It also produced the opposite: people "detached from their narrow niche" who did not direct their own futures at all. C4.2 should keep both sides.

### tawney.13 · III.iii "The Growth of Individualism" — "economics were one thing and ethics another"; Indifferentism

- **Claims:** C4.31 (for), C4.30 (for), C4.5 (for)
- **Location:** III.iii; lines 5138–5142 and 5287–5295
- **Checked:** text file

> It was precisely this whole conception of a social theory based ultimately on religion which was being discredited. While rival authorities were discussing the correct interpretation of economic ethics, the flank of both was turned by the growth of a powerful body of lay opinion, which argued that economics were one thing and ethics another. [...] But the conception that the Church possessed, of its own authority, an independent standard of social values, which it could apply as a criterion to the practical affairs of the economic world, grew steadily weaker. The result, neither immediate nor intended, but inevitable, was the tacit denial of spiritual significance in the transactions of business and in the relations of organized society. Repudiating the right of religion to advance any social theory distinctively its own, that attitude became itself the most tyrannical and paralyzing of theories. It may be called Indifferentism.

The separation is again of *institutions and spheres*: the Church loses its jurisdiction over business, and lay opinion, not theology, drives it. Tawney's "neither immediate nor intended" supports C4.5's unintended-contributor reading. His word "tyrannical" for a theory that leaves religion only the private soul is worth noting for §8.

### tawney.14 · IV.ii "A Godly Discipline *versus* the Religion of Trade" — the Puritan "would have been scandalized by economic individualism"

- **Claims:** C4.4 (complicates), C4.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.ii; lines 5885–5888 and 5904–5912
- **Checked:** text file

> There was in Puritanism an element which was conservative and traditionalist, and an element which was revolutionary; a collectivism which grasped at an iron discipline, and an individualism which spurned the savorless mess of human ordinances [...] That it swept away the restrictions imposed by the existing machinery is true; neither ecclesiastical courts, nor High Commission, nor Star Chamber, could function after 1640. But, if it broke the discipline of the Church of Laud and the State of Strafford, it did so but as a step towards erecting a more rigorous discipline of its own. It would have been scandalized by economic individualism as much as by religious tolerance, and the broad outlines of its scheme of organization favored unrestricted liberty in matters of business as little as in the things of the spirit.

This is an "against" for any simple line from Puritanism to the self-directing economic individual. Early Puritanism (to 1640) wanted *more* discipline. Baxter's *Christian Directory* (1673) still teaches just price and fair rent like "a medieval Schoolman" (line 6235). So C4.4's "later Calvinists" has to mean *late*: after the Civil War.

### tawney.15 · IV.ii — the capitalist spirit "was not [...] the offspring of Puritanism"

- **Claims:** C4.5 (for), C4.4 (for), C6.4 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.ii; lines 6281–6284, 6291–6301, 6306–6307
- **Checked:** text file

> “The capitalist spirit” is as old as history, and was not, as has sometimes been said, the offspring of Puritanism. But it found in certain aspects of later Puritanism a tonic which braced its energies and fortified its already vigorous temper. [...] Like traits of individual character which are suppressed till the approach of maturity releases them, the tendencies in Puritanism, which were to make it later a potent ally of the movement against the control of economic relations in the name either of social morality or of the public interest, did not reveal themselves till political and economic changes had prepared a congenial environment for their growth. Nor, once those conditions were created, was it only England which witnessed the transformation. In all countries alike, in Holland, in America, in Scotland, in Geneva itself, the social theory of Calvinism went through the same process of development. It had begun by being the very soul of authoritarian regimentation. It ended by being the vehicle of an almost Utilitarian individualism. [...] So little do those who shoot the arrows of the spirit know where they will light.

This is Tawney's answer to *where* the change happens: in the interaction. The economic change comes first and sets the conditions; Puritanism is a "tonic" and an "ally," not the parent. **For** C4.5 and C4.4 (the shift belongs to later Calvinism). It **complicates** C6.4: America is named among the places where Calvinist regimentation turned into individualism, so the communal phase was a phase.

### tawney.16 · IV.iii "The Triumph of the Economic Virtues" — the Puritan alone before God; rights from responsibility

- **Claims:** C4.3 (complicates), C1.1 (for), C4.31 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.iii; lines 6314–6317, 6338–6342, 6371–6375, 6378–6381
- **Checked:** text file

> While the revelation of God to the individual soul is the center of all religion, the essence of Puritan theology was that it made it, not only the center, but the whole circumference and substance, dismissing as dross and vanity all else but this secret and solitary communion. [...] Through it he must take his way, alone. No aid can avail him: no preacher, for only the elect can apprehend with the spirit the word of God; no Church, for to the visible Church even reprobates belong; no sacrament, for sacraments are ordained to increase the glory of God, not to minister spiritual nourishment to man [...] The moral self-sufficiency of the Puritan nerved his will, but it corroded his sense of social solidarity. For, if each individual’s destiny hangs on a private transaction between himself and his Maker, what room is left for human intervention? [...] A spiritual aristocrat, who sacrificed fraternity to liberty, he drew from his idealization of personal responsibility a theory of individual rights, which, secularized and generalized, was to be among the most potent explosives that the world has known.

**For** C1.1: standing alone and answerable, and from that, rights. This is the clearest statement in any source so far of answerability turning into the separate Self. **Complicates** C4.3: Tawney gives the full isolation (no preacher, no Church) to *Puritanism*, not to Luther, and his footnote IV-55 credits Weber for it. **Complicates** C4.31: here the Self *does* begin to separate from community, a century before the Enlightenment, though it is still bound to God.

### tawney.17 · IV.iii — "rejected as a means is resumed as a consequence"; the calling chosen, not assigned

- **Claims:** C4.4 (for), C4.2 (for)
- **Location:** IV.iii; lines 6386–6390 and 6684–6695
- **Checked:** text file

> For, since conduct and action, though availing nothing to attain the free gift of salvation, are a proof that the gift has been accorded, what is rejected as a means is resumed as a consequence, and the Puritan flings himself into practical activities with the dæmonic energy of one who, all doubts allayed, is conscious that he is a sealed and chosen vessel. [...] Luther had advanced it as a weapon against monasticism. But for Luther, with his patriarchal outlook on economic affairs, the calling means normally that state of life in which the individual has been set by Heaven, and against which it is impiety to rebel. On the lips of Puritan divines, it is not an invitation to resignation, but the bugle-call which summons the elect to the long battle which will end only with their death. “The world is all before them.” They are to hammer out their salvation, not merely _in vocatione_, but _per vocationem_. The calling is not a condition in which the individual is born, but a strenuous and exacting enterprise, to be undertaken, indeed, under the guidance of Providence, but to be chosen by each man for himself, with a deep sense of his solemn responsibilities.

This is the hinge of C4.2 and C4.4 in one passage. Luther's calling is a *station*; the later Puritan calling is *chosen*, which is the "direct their own futures" of C4.2. Note "all doubts allayed": Tawney's Puritan works from assurance, not from Weber's anxiety. The station has not quite gone, either: a divine he quotes still says God "hath appointed to every man his proper post and province" (lines 6671–6672).

### tawney.18 · IV.iii — economics makes conduct profitable; morals make it admired

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates), C4.4 (for)
- **Location:** IV.iii; lines 6839–6843, 6849–6850, 6874–6876
- **Checked:** text file

> The springs of economic conduct lie in regions rarely penetrated by moralists, and to suggest a direct reaction of theory on practice would be paradoxical. But, if the circumstances which determine that certain kinds of conduct shall be profitable are economic, those which decide that they shall be the object of general approval are primarily moral and intellectual. [...] What is significant is the change of standards which converted a natural frailty into a resounding virtue. [...] The transition from the anabaptist to the company promoter was less abrupt than might at first sight be supposed. It had been prepared, however unintentionally, by Puritan moralists.

Tawney's division of labor, and the most useful sentence for rewording C4.5. Economic change explains the *behavior*; religion explains why it became a *virtue*. If the Self is a value and not just a behavior, then on Tawney's account its moral standing came from the religious side, not from capital alone. "However unintentionally" supports the unintended-contributor clause.

### tawney.19 · IV.iii–iv — "character is all and circumstances nothing": individualism in religion becomes individualist morality

- **Claims:** C1.1 (for), C7.4 (for), C4.3 (complicates)
- **Location:** IV.iii, lines 6394–6396; IV.iv "The New Medicine for Poverty", lines 7047–7053 and 7066–7069
- **Checked:** text file

> Convinced that character is all and circumstances nothing, he sees in the poverty of those who fall by the way, not a misfortune to be pitied and relieved, but a moral failing to be condemned [...] To insist that the individual is responsible, that no man can save his brother, that the essence of religion is the contact of the soul with its Maker, how true and indispensable! But how easy to slip from that truth into the suggestion that society is without responsibility, that no man can help his brother, that the social order and its consequences are not even the scaffolding by which men may climb to greater heights, but something external, alien and irrelevant [...] Individualism in religion led insensibly, if not quite logically, to an individualist morality, and an individualist morality to a disparagement of the significance of the social fabric as compared with personal character.

This is the thesis's own structure, put by Tawney: a true acknowledgment ("how true and indispensable!") slides into a distortion. For C7.4 it gives blame for poverty arriving in the seventeenth century, from making character the cause, with God fully in place; this anticipates Smiles. It is outside this file's main claims but strong; tag it in the §7 notes.

### tawney.20 · Note IV-32 — where Tawney disagrees with Weber

- **Claims:** C4.5 (for), C4.4 (for), C4.2 (complicates)
- **Location:** Notes, Ch. IV, note IV-32; lines 9350–9364 and 9381–9386
- **Checked:** text file

> Weber seems to me to explain by reference to moral and intellectual influences developments which have their principal explanation in another region altogether. [...] The development of capitalism in Holland and England in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries was due, not to the fact that they were Protestant powers, but to large economic movements, in particular the Discoveries and the results which flowed from them. Of course material and psychological changes went together, and of course the second reacted on the first. But it seems a little artificial to talk as though capitalist enterprise could not appear till religious changes had produced a capitalist spirit. It would be equally true, and equally one-sided, to say that the religious changes were purely the result of economic movements. [...] the Calvinists of the sixteenth century (including English Puritans) were believers in a rigorous discipline, and the individualism ascribed not unjustly to the Puritan movement in its later phases would have horrified them. The really significant question is that of the causes of the change from the one standpoint to the other, a question which Weber appears to ignore.

Tawney makes three objections to Weber (the third, on Weber leaving out Machiavelli and the economists, is at lines 9366–9374). Economics comes first: **for** C4.5. Early and later Calvinism are different, so the turn is late: **for** C4.4. But he also refuses the reverse simplification, a purely economic cause of the religious changes, and C4.5 as written ("not a pure act of thought") stays just inside that limit. He accepts Weber's point about "calling" (lines 9343–9344).

### tawney.21 · Ch. V "Conclusion" — several causes; forces within and beyond human control

- **Claims:** C4.5 (complicates), C4.30 (for), C4.31 (for)
- **Location:** Ch. V; lines 7645–7650, 7697–7701, 7703–7709
- **Checked:** text file

> The elements which combined to produce that revolution are too numerous to be summarized in any neat formula. But, side by side with the expansion of trade and the rise of new classes to political power, there was a further cause, which, if not the most conspicuous, was not the least fundamental. It was the contraction of the territory within which the spirit of religion was conceived to run. [...] The former takes as its province the individual soul, the latter the intercourse of man with his fellows in the activities of business and the affairs of society. Provided that each keeps to its own territory, peace is assured. They cannot collide, for they can never meet. [...] History is a stage where forces which are within human control contend and coöperate with forces which are not. The change of opinion described in these pages drew nourishment from both. The storm and fury of the Puritan revolution had been followed by a dazzling outburst of economic enterprise, and the transformation of the material environment prepared an atmosphere in which a judicious moderation seemed the voice at once of the truest wisdom and the sincerest piety. But the inner world was in motion as well as the outer.

Tawney's last word on the cause has three parts: trade, new classes, and the shrinking of religion's province. "The inner world was in motion as well as the outer." The economic origin is load-bearing but not the only support. The settlement he describes (religion gets "the individual soul," business gets society) is the institutional separation of C4.30/C4.31, finished by the eighteenth century.
